TL;DR

  • What it is: From 2017 Perfect Diary pulled marketplace and store buyers into WeChat with a red-packet card in every parcel. Buyers added "Xiaowanzi" (小完子), a persona run by hundreds of staff on hundreds of accounts, and were invited into thousands of 500-person groups called 小完子玩美研究所 ("Xiaowanzi's Perfect Beauty Institute"). The setup became China's textbook "private traffic" case (Jiemian, 2021; Yatsen F-1, 2020).
  • Why people came back: cheap, time-boxed deals (the same products at roughly 50-60% of the Tmall price), a friendly persona who greeted, posted selfies and said goodnight, and a nonstop schedule of drops, livestreams and lucky draws. A former staffer said the groups' strongest pull was coupons; one heavy user called them "groups that exist, simply and crudely, for coupons" (woshipm, 2020; Jiemian, 2021).
  • What the company fed it: a very large human team (1,056 online sales and customer-service staff in Sep 2020, 1,232 by Dec 2020), in-house group-control tooling, SCRM vendors, a Tencent partnership on the Xiaowanzi Weixin Shop, and its own price tier and exclusive SKUs (F-1; FY2020 20-F).
  • Biggest mistake: it optimised the "community" for conversion, not for members. Staff were judged on sales and on nobody leaving, not on activity. Off-topic chat was deliberately cut back, shill accounts kept up the chatter, and by 2021 the groups sold other brands' underwear and pillows. Members noticed and muted or left the groups (Jiemian, Sep 2021; Jiemian/海豚智库, Jun 2021).
  • The one lesson: a direct channel you own is valuable, but a channel isn't a community. Once the product edge and the price edge faded, nothing members had built or felt attached to was left to hold them.

What the community is

Perfect Diary (完美日记), launched on Tmall in March 2017 by Guangzhou-based Yatsen, became China's top-selling colour-cosmetics brand on Tmall by Singles' Day 2018 (IT Consultis). Its WeChat "private domain" (私域) has four layers that point at each other:

  1. Xiaowanzi personal accounts (later WeCom/企业微信 accounts). Hundreds of accounts share one name and one avatar. The persona introduces herself as "your personal benefits officer and beauty adviser" and opens by saying "I'm really not a robot!" She posts about three Moments a day (PingWest; Runwise via Zhihu, 2021). Store visitors were routed to a sister persona, "Xiaomeizi" (小美子) (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  2. WeChat groups named 小完子玩美研究所, capped at 500 members each. By 2019-20 there were "more than 6,500" of them (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  3. The WeChat official (service) account, used for content and events (woshipm).
  4. Mini-programs: the Xiaowanzi Weixin Shop for buying, plus content and check-in areas such as 完子说 and 小美说 (Yatsen 20-F FY2024; Sohu, Feb 2021).

Yatsen runs all of it directly. There is no meaningful unofficial, fan-run space: English Reddit only has scattered r/AsianBeauty product reviews, and Chinese members' voices show up as complaints on Xiaohongshu that journalists quoted. Status: the structure still exists. Yatsen's 2025 20-F still describes "Weixin groups hosted by our online beauty advisors", but the staff behind it has been cut by about 90% and the company no longer reports any metric for it (FY2025 20-F).

Origin and strategy

  • Why: paid public traffic (Tmall, Weibo) kept getting more expensive, and WeChat let a brand reach a buyer again for free. A Yatsen-linked talk listed four aims: new incremental revenue (a target of about 5% of revenue from private-domain business), "die-hard fans", a place to incubate and test new products, and building digital teams (Sina/亿邦, Apr 2021).
  • Role in the model: retention, not acquisition. The public funnel (around 15,000 KOLs on Xiaohongshu, Douyin and Bilibili) produced first purchases, and the private domain was meant to produce the second, third and fourth. A former head of fan operations, Ma Ke (马珂), found that over 75% of new group members in 2019-20 had already bought once or visited a store: "repeat purchase is the vital point of Perfect Diary's private-domain play" (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  • How Yatsen pitched it in the IPO: "industry pioneering use of company channels on Weixin where customers can interact with our team of dedicated beauty advisors and other beauty customers to discover beauty tips, receive new product promotions and share feedback... This direct engagement fosters long-term loyalty and promotes repeat purchases in a cost-efficient manner" (F-1/424B4, Nov 2020).
  • Champion: founder and CEO Huang Jinfeng (黄锦峰), a former COO of Yunifang who came up through e-commerce. No dedicated community executive was ever named publicly. Once the stock fell, Huang talked about R&D, not community (see below).
  • Timing luck: early on WeChat tolerated third-party group-control and SCRM tools (Aike SCRM was an early partner), which made the mass funnel possible. When WeChat tightened its rules, "the effect of private-domain traffic diversion weakened" (Jiemian, Mar 2021).

Business context: Yatsen's net revenue went RMB0.64bn (2018) → 3.03bn (2019) → 5.23bn (2020) → 5.84bn (2021) → 3.71bn (2022) → 3.41bn (2023) → 3.39bn (2024) → 4.30bn (2025). The 2025 rebound came from skincare (+63.5%), not colour cosmetics (+1.9%) (F-1; daxue; FY2023; FY2024; FY2025). The filings' wording slid from Weixin channels that "play an increasingly important role" (F-1) to "an important role" (FY2020/21) to "also play a role" (FY2024). Trajectory: peak 2019-2020, sharp shrinkage in 2021, a marginal channel since.

What keeps people coming back

  • A persona as a friend. Xiaowanzi posts selfies, check-ins at trendy spots and food photos. She joked on Qixi (Chinese Valentine's Day) about being a "single office worker", sends goodnight messages and anniversary thank-you notes, and asks buyers to reply with a number rather than pushing a link at them. Observers found that different accounts' Moments were not identical, which suggests segmentation (woshipm 2019; PingWest; Zhihu/Runwise). This is a parasocial tie to staff wearing a mask: each account was a "fan operations specialist" paid RMB5,000-10,000 a month (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  • Ritual cadence built around a working woman's phone habits. Moments go out at 9-10am, 4-5pm and 9pm-midnight. Group posts run from 8am to midnight, a batch of about 8 messages every 30-60 minutes. Limited flash sales happen at 11am-noon and 8-9pm, so members learn when to show up. Recurring tags such as #宠粉回馈# ("thanks to our fans") and #限时1小时秒杀# ("one-hour flash sale") brand the drops (woshipm, May 2020).
  • An exclusive price tier. The same eyeshadow or mascara cost about 50-60% of the Tmall price in the WeChat shop, and the groups carried SKUs Tmall didn't (personal care, partner brands) (woshipm; IT Consultis). This was the main switching cost.
  • Onboarding hook. A scratch-off red-packet card in each parcel leads to the service account, then the Xiaowanzi QR code, then the mini-program, which pays out RMB1-2. For about RMB1-2 the brand gained a follower, a friend and a group member (Zhihu/Runwise).
  • Light gamification. Mini-program check-in topics (5-day skincare, makeup check-ins) with prizes, lucky draws and livestreams. A later article mentions "美粉值" (beauty-fan points) for referrals, from a low-quality 2025 PR piece, so treat it as unverified (Sohu; softunis).
  • What was missing: status, levels that meant anything, recognition of members, and peer bonds. Yatsen's own 2018 analysis found that the content generating the most heat matched promotions, so the persona's "unimportant chat" was "gradually restricted". The result: "members rarely chat; once a promotion appears, the mood heats up" (Jiemian, Mar 2021).

The content engine

  • Output: about 3 Moments posts a day. One 2020 tally split them into WeChat-shop promos 35%, Tmall promos 26%, reviews and recommendations 20%, promo previews 16% (IT Consultis). The service account was capped at 4 pushes a month, sent Tue-Thu at 9:45am. 76% of its content was discount offers ("RMB1", "RMB9.9", "second item free"); the rest was tutorials, trials and recruiting (woshipm). The menu bar had a "惊喜" (surprise) collection of makeup tutorials and product guides.
  • Formats: product videos, swatch photos, tutorials, livestreams. Yatsen ran 50+ simultaneous WeChat livestreams by store beauty advisors from 2019 (F-1). Content went to the official account or mini-program first, then was forwarded to Moments and groups (IT Consultis).
  • Member-made content: close to none inside the groups. External KOC content was "integrated" and reposted (Fabernovel, 2020). The F-1 mentions "inviting customers to share their beauty experience with our content community" and "social sharing games", but gives no numbers. Product feedback and surveys ran in groups (Zhihu/Runwise). There is no evidence of a co-creation programme with named members.

People, programs and governance

  • Staffing: at the peak, online sales and customer service (1,056 in Sep 2020) was Yatsen's largest online function, bigger than marketing (249) and data/tech (211) combined (F-1). Chinese press variously describe "nearly 100" or "100+" fan-operations specialists on WeCom (PingWest) and up to about 1,000 community staff (圈量). The difference is probably whether pure customer service is counted.
  • Incentives: KPIs were sales, repeat-purchase rate and retention; "activity was not a priority weight." Each group's links carried their own UTM codes, apparently to credit sales to the account owner. "Better to keep 300 silent lurkers than have one person quit the group": every exit triggered a review of that specialist's chat log and possible "punishment" (Jiemian, Mar 2021; Zhihu/Runwise).
  • Shills (托/水军): at least ~4 non-official accounts per group fed staff the right questions (woshipm). A vendor case study says flatly that "every group has 水军 (paid posters) setting the rhythm" (圈量).
  • Rules: posting off-topic ads gets you a "plane ticket" out (removal) (WeChat official developer-community guide quoting the group rules; search snippet).
  • Tech: in-house group control. Job ads asked for Xposed/reverse-engineering skills (Zhihu/Runwise). Later came the move to WeCom (from 2020) with SCRM auto-group-building and broadcast (圈量), and joint work with Tencent product managers on the Weixin Shop (F-1).
  • Ambassador or superuser programme: no evidence found.

Growth loops

  • Parcel → WeChat: the main loop. Every marketplace order seeded the private pool (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  • Store → Xiaomeizi: in-store advisers added visitors; the stores peaked at about 280 in Dec 2022 and were down to 104 by early 2024 (OFweek, 2024).
  • Referral: members inviting friends ("老带新", bring a friend, with gifts) plus staff who trawled Tieba, Zhihu and QQ groups for prospects. Jiemian calls this loop clearly the weakest (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  • Reverse effect: when a long-time KOL defected to a competitor over fees, "related fans left the groups en masse" and followed the KOL (Jiemian, Mar 2021). Members' loyalty sat with the influencer, not with the community.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard (company-audited): repeat purchase by quarterly cohort rose from 8.1% (Q3 2017 cohort) to 38.9% (Q3 2018) to 41.5% (Q3 2019), and 30% of 2018-19 first-time buyers bought again by Q2 2020 (F-1). The filing does not attribute this to the WeChat groups. Analysts tied the 2018-19 jump mainly to SKU expansion and new brands (Jiemian/海豚智库, Jun 2021). Jiemian reported that growth in the "first-time buyers who repurchase the next year" metric fell from 30.8% (2019) to 2.6% (2020) (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  • Claims: RMB1bn private-domain GMV, about 20% of GMV in 2020, comes from an SCRM vendor's marketing (圈量). Strategist Doris (女子刀法), who says her 2019 article made Perfect Diary everyone's case study, countered that it "put a 100+ person team on private domain, but the sales it brings are actually a very small share" (Marketup repost). A third account estimated RMB20M+ a month (Zhihu). The company never disclosed a figure.
  • What management said: on the Q3 2021 call, asked "how to improve customers' loyalty toward our brands, especially makeup", Huang answered R&D (new powder technology) and expanding into base makeup. He did not mention the community (Q3 2021 call). On data law, he said private-domain data was "provided voluntarily by consumers" (Q2 2021 call).
  • Cost side: selling and marketing was 62% of revenue in Q3 2020 (Jiemian) and still about 72% in Q1 2026 (Yahoo Finance). So the "free re-reach" never freed Yatsen from paid traffic.

What they did right

  1. Captured the buyer at the moment of delight. The parcel card turned anonymous Tmall orders into first-party contacts for about RMB1-2 each, with 75%+ of group joiners already customers (Jiemian).
  2. One consistent, human face. A single persona across hundreds of accounts gave trust and a single brand voice, and real staff answered tricky questions quickly ("if it's a robot, it passed the Turing test") (Zhihu/Runwise).
  3. Habit-forming schedule. Fixed daily drop windows and recurring tags trained members to check in at known times (woshipm).
  4. Restraint rules. A product bundle was promoted for at most 2 days and 3-4 posts, to avoid fatigue (woshipm).
  5. Integrated the shop. One tap from a post to a mini-program checkout, with instrumentation (UTM per group) good enough to manage by numbers (Zhihu/Runwise).

What they did wrong

  1. Fake sociality. Shill accounts and recycled scripts. Members who added nicknames found "at least a dozen shills per group" and concluded that "except me, everyone in the group is probably a shill". The consequence: they muted or left (Jiemian, Sep 2021).
  2. Suppressed real conversation. Chit-chat was curbed because promos produced more heat, and staff were told to "say less so you can't say anything wrong" (Jiemian, Mar 2021). That removed the peer bonds that make communities stick.
  3. Wrong KPI. Punishing exits rewarded silent lurkers over engaged members (Jiemian, Mar 2021).
  4. Turned the brand club into a discount mall. From 2021, "完子之家 × [brand]" sales of Ubras underwear, NetEase pillows and hair-removal devices ran 3-4 times a week. Critics called it "playing traffic discounts... no different from a clearance platform", and members asked "why not just push your own products?" (Jiemian/海豚智库, Jun 2021; Jiemian, Sep 2021).
  5. A price war with its own flagship store. Selling at 40-60% below Tmall kept members price-anchored and hurt brand equity. In 2022 the brand had to "control discounts" for a "brand upgrade" (Ping An Securities, Feb 2023).
  6. Built on a borrowed, tolerant platform. The funnel relied on grey-area WeChat group-control tools, and its reach weakened when WeChat tightened rules. The Personal Information Protection Law (Nov 2021) ended Yatsen's DTC-customer reporting (Jiemian; FY2021 20-F).
  7. Community couldn't outrun the product. Hit products backfired (complaints about the broken "小细跟" lipstick and its price per gram), and Jiemian argued the brand's repurchase hinged on product interest that marketing couldn't replace (Jiemian, Sep 2021). Huang later called the growth-hacking era "my most painful time" (Brandmine, quoting a 2022 interview).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Put the invitation inside the product moment. A card in the box, a prompt right after the first success, a small instant reward: capture contacts when delight peaks.
  2. Give the brand one warm, consistent voice, staffed by real people who answer fast. Disclose that it is a team, though; Perfect Diary's single-persona mask became a liability once members saw it.
  3. Publish a predictable cadence (fixed drop or office-hour windows, named recurring formats) so checking in becomes a habit.
  4. Cap repetition (Perfect Diary's 2-day / 3-4-post rule) and segment what each cohort sees.
  5. Instrument everything (per-space attribution) and connect the community to checkout or activation in one tap.

Avoid these

  1. Don't fake the crowd. Shills and scripted chat get detected and turn members into cynics. Seed real conversation with real customers instead.
  2. Don't measure retention by "nobody left" or by sales per host. Measure member-to-member interaction and contributions, or hosts will suppress exactly the behaviour that builds belonging.
  3. Don't make the community a cheaper back door to your own product, or a store for other brands. Price-led loyalty leaves the moment the discount or the hit product does, and it trains members to wait for deals.

Score justifications. Retention mechanics 3: strong cadence, persona and exclusive pricing, but almost all of it extrinsic (coupons). Content engine 3: very high volume but 76% discounts, little education depth. Member ownership 1: members created nothing, chit-chat was curbed, shills held the floor. Staff investment 5: roughly 1,000-1,200 dedicated staff, custom tooling, a Tencent partnership. Business-impact evidence 2: audited repeat-purchase data exists but isn't attributed; the GMV-share figures are vendor or pundit claims that contradict each other. Durability 2: staff cut about 90% by 2021-24 and the channel is now described only in passing.

Open questions

  • What the private domain actually contributed to revenue in any year (the company never said; claims range from "very small" to 20% of GMV).
  • How many WeCom contacts and groups survive today, and whether Xiaowanzi still posts at its former cadence (no 2024-26 primary data found).
  • Whether repeat-purchase rates for group members ever differed measurably from non-members.
  • Whether the 2021 cut in online sales and customer-service staff (1,232 → 294) was a deliberate wind-down of the groups or a reclassification into "Online Operation" (which rose from 265 to 878).
