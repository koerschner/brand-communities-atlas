TL;DR

  • What it was: a Lithium-hosted peer-support forum at community.telkom.co.za. South Africa's former state telco launched it in November 2015 as part of a "customer-first" turnaround, and it was a 2016 Lithy finalist for Community Design of the Year (Skype won). It closed in late 2020 and now redirects to an automated self-help portal.
  • Why people came: to get a broken line, slow LTE or a stuck cancellation fixed when the call centre had failed them. Most came once. Registrations kept climbing (≈36,000 accounts by Aug 2020), but messages per year halved from ≈6,500 (2017–18) to ≈3,300 (2019). The homepage "top kudoed authors" in 2017–2019 had only 2 to 8 kudos each.
  • What Telkom fed it: out-of-the-box Lithium gamification (kudos, accepted solutions, ranks, badges), a handful of staff how-to guides, and a corporate blog (partnership announcements, workplace tips). There was one named community manager. The moderator list stayed "Update coming shortly".
  • Biggest mistake: a policy and demand mismatch. Telkom declared the community "not a service channel" and said "we won't be answering service related questions here", yet its most-used tags were bad service, slow, cancellation and complaint. Customers wrote "Dear Telkom…" posts to a company that had said it wouldn't answer.
  • The lesson: a peer-support community only works where peers can actually solve the problem. When most issues need the company's back office (faults, billing, cancellations), you need staffed escalation, or the forum becomes a public complaints wall and loses to a chatbot.

What the community is

Telkom Community ("Get involved. Problem solved.") was an owned Lithium (now Khoros) forum, tenant tiphq36682, hosted at community.telkom.co.za and skinned to match telkom.co.za [1][2][8]. Its categories were Internet (ADSL/VDSL/Fibre, LTE/3G), Voice, Small Business, My Telkom (billing; for example the "Understanding Your Bill" board), Welcome & Guides, an off-topic board called "The Lounge", and a Telkom Blog [2][9][10][11]. The homepage showed Recent Topics, Latest Accepted Solutions, Top Kudoed Posts and Authors, Featured Topics, a tag cloud, Users Online and Recent Blog Articles [8][12].

Timeline

  • Oct 2015: the CMO announced it [4].
  • 17 Nov 2015: soft launch with seeded members (the first posts are dated 17-11-2015) [13].
  • 27 Nov 2015: official launch [1].
  • 3 Dec 2015: press coverage [3].
  • Between 7 Aug and 29 Sep 2020: a banner appeared: "Important! Community will be closing. Please go to gethelp.telkom.co.za" [12][14].
  • 11 Nov 2020: the root URL was 302-redirecting to gethelp.telkom.co.za, and it still was in 2023 [14][15].
  • Nov 2026: the host resets connections from outside (direct fetch and the r.jina.ai proxy both failed) [16].

Other spaces

  • MyBroadband forum: Telkom's social media team kept an official "TelkomZA" thread and PM channel there from 2013 [17]. A rep calling himself a "Call Centre Agent, Trainer and Online Community Rep" also posted DIY fault-logging syntax there in 2016 [18].
  • Facebook: an unofficial "Telkom Community Complaints" group exists (size unknown) [19].
  • Since 2020: support runs through the Get Help portal, the Thuso virtual agent (web, app, WhatsApp) and @TelkomZA on social media [20][21].

Origin and strategy

  • MyBroadband trigger: in October 2015 MyBroadband ran a column titled "Telkom's biggest problem: you cannot buy its products". CMO Enzo Scarcella replied publicly with a list of digital fixes and promised "a Telkom Community, where customers can share their experiences with the brand and engage with other Telkom customers about sales and service. This will help us improve our service delivery on a continual basis" [4].
  • Turnaround strategy: in Telkom's 2016 Lithy entry, operational manager Adam Wesson described the community as "a very real manifestation and important part of our business turnaround strategy from a reactive, closed-off organization to a customer-first, proactive company". It was also "a critical component of our brand strategy, which aims to win hearts and minds and drive customer trial" [1].
  • Who built it: the Telkom Digital Marketing team, working with Lithium and a digital agency. The design brief was a "Smile in the mind" [1].
  • Business model link: deflection. The Lithy entry claims the community was "reducing the load on our call centre, improving our digital NPS, making us more operationally efficient" [1]. That same year Telkom outsourced its fixed-line call centre as part of a cost-efficiency programme [22].
  • Champions: CMO Scarcella (public sponsor) [4]; Adam Wesson, who led the community. He had spent 15 years at Telkom as an ADSL Tier 1/2 agent, then managed the support teams and Telkom's Social Media Support team [23]. Charl ("CO", Super Admin), the TelkomZA rep already popular on MyBroadband, seeded the launch [13].

Trajectory. Message throughput was about 6,000 a year in 2016–17 and about 6,500 a year in 2017–18. It fell to about 3,300 in 2019 and about 3,000 a year (annualised) in 2020. Registrations meanwhile accelerated, to about 7,000 a year. That gap is the signature of a drive-by complaint channel: people register to post one problem and don't come back. By 2020 the community had well under one message per registered account. For scale, Telkom had 12 million mobile customers by March 2020 [25]. Peak concurrency was about 130 to 190 users online.

What keeps people coming back

There was little to bring people back. What existed was mostly Lithium's default kit:

  • Status and progress:
    • Ranks: New Member, Member, Established Member, Senior Member, Contributor, Regular and Valued Contributor [13][26].
    • Badges for milestones such as "receiving 100 Kudos, writing your first Accepted Solution, being a Founder Member". Each new badge triggered an email ("Every time you reach a certain milestone, you will get an email notification") [27].
    • The Lithy entry describes custom "icons showing gamification progression" and "fun designs for Avatars" [1].
    • The badges annoyed some early members (see Member voice).
  • Recognition: kudos and Accepted Solutions. A guide explained that marking a solution "gives the author status within the Community". Telkom's own pitch was "Post a solution. Be the next Telkom whizkid" [8][28].
  • Notification hooks: board and topic subscriptions, plus "the system will also send you an email reminder, prompting you to check if your question has received a solution yet" [28].
  • Belonging: the guidelines invited members to "Come and be part of the family!" and offered "The Lounge" for off-topic chat. No rituals, events, challenges, seasons or member identity ever appeared in the record [9].
  • Parasocial pull: the one real retention asset was a person, Charl/TelkomZA. Members followed him over from MyBroadband: "You can not go wrong if you go to Charl – The TelkomZA Guy" [13]. The pull was "a Telkom employee who can actually fix my line", not peers.
  • No superuser core: the homepage "Top Kudoed Authors" widget (probably a rolling window) shows leaders with 5 kudos (Jun 2017), 8 kudos (Jun 2018) and 3 kudos (Jan 2019) [12][24]. By Dec 2019 one member, EnhleManax, led with 111 kudos, much of it on blog-comment threads ("Re: Internet of Things", "Re: Thank you, new friends") [24]. There is no evidence of a champion or MVP programme.

    Press releases such as the LIT streaming launch were republished there [30]. This content filled the homepage but answered nobody's fault ticket.

  • Member content: essentially Q&A threads. No templates, recipes or member-guide programme was found.

People, programs and governance

  • Team:
    • Community manager Adam Wesson [23].
    • Super Admin Charl ("CO") from the social media team at launch [13].
    • "Moderators: Update Coming shortly", still the text in a July 2019 snapshot, over 3.5 years after launch [23].
    • The Lithy entry credits the Digital Marketing team plus an agency, not a support or community org [1].
  • Rules:
    • The guidelines banned bad language and "angry rant[s]". They also banned discussing "competing products from our competitors, including making reference to them without actually naming them".
    • Posts "abusive in any way to any company or product may be edited or even removed".
    • "We ask that you do not argue or discuss any moderator's decisions… If you argue, these posts will be deleted!" [9]
    • For a brand with a famously angry customer base, these rules confirmed what sceptics had predicted at launch (see Member voice).
  • Scope policy:
    • The welcome post said: "The Community won't be another service channel. Rather, it will be a place of Peer Support" [29].
    • "How to get help" said: "This is not a service channel… We won't be answering service related questions here", and listed faults, fault progress and billing as off-limits [10].
    • In practice, staff sometimes did log cases. One 2019 cancellation thread shows a reply giving a case reference number and promising contact [31].
  • Feedback loop: the CMO promised the community "will help us improve our service delivery" [4]. No evidence was found of an ideas board, product-feedback programme or closed-loop reporting.

Growth loops

  • SEO: within five months the community ranked #1 on Google for "Telkom Community" [1]. Thread titles such as "How do you cancel a Telkom Service?" and "Telkom LTE Slow" are still indexed years after closure [31][32]. Search was the main acquisition channel, which explains rising registrations alongside falling engagement.
  • Hand-off from social: launch seeding pulled members from MyBroadband's TelkomZA following [13].
  • Cross-promotion: "How to get help" pages and telkom.co.za navigation linked to the community [10].
  • No referral, ambassador or event loop was found.

Evidence of business impact

  • Company claims, all unquantified: the community had "solved thousands of users' problems, reducing the load on our call centre, improving our digital NPS… making a meaningful, measurable contribution to changing perceptions of Telkom" [1]. The traffic numbers (624k page views in 5 months) are Telkom's own [1].
  • Hard evidence: none found. No deflection rate, cost-saving figure or NPS delta was published. The community does not appear in the integrated-report material reviewed. No academic study was found (an OpenAlex search returned none specific to it).
  • Revealed preference: in 2020 Telkom closed the forum and pointed users to Get Help. That portal (built with CLEVVA) and the Thuso virtual agent "not only [help] diagnose the issue but also… resolve it by triggering relevant actions in back office systems" [20][21]. That is exactly what a peer forum could not do. The closure suggests Telkom judged automated self-service a better deflection tool.

What they did right

  1. Real executive sponsorship tied to a public promise. The CMO named the community in a public reply to a damaging MyBroadband column, and it shipped within about 6 weeks [3][4].
  2. Seeded launch with a trusted face. Soft-launching on 17 Nov 2015 with Charl, whom members already credited with fixing lines, gave the first threads warmth and testimonials [13].
  3. Strong design and fast SEO. It was a Lithy finalist alongside Google, and #1 for its brand query within months, with 151k unique visitors in 5 months [1][5].
  4. Clear onboarding documentation: guides on kudos, badges, accepted solutions and posting [27][28].
  5. Durable how-to content for the common jobs: viewing bills, USSD codes, cancelling online [12][24].

What they did wrong

  1. They declared the main use case off-limits. Customers came with faults, bills and cancellations, which only Telkom could resolve. Telkom said it would not answer those questions [10][29].
    • The tag cloud read bad service, slow, Cancellation, No Service, poor service, complaint [24].
    • Threads became open letters ("Dear Telkom My Super Fast LTE… Please if someone can contact me ASAP"), with peers able to offer only "Try remove and reinsert Sim… All I can offer" [32].
    • Consequence: users judged it one more dead end. A 2020 Reddit post lists "Telkom community forums" among the channels that failed [33].
  2. No peer-expert bench. Telecom faults are mostly account- or network-specific. Few customers could answer them, and Telkom never recruited, empowered or rewarded superusers. Top authors had single-digit kudos [12][24].
  3. Gamification without substance. Badge emails arrived before members got any value. Early users mocked them: "I feel like I'm back in the Boy Scouts with all the 'Badges'" [6].
  4. Heavy moderation rules in a low-trust brand. Banning rants, competitor mentions and arguments about moderation confirmed launch-day scepticism that Telkom would "close or lock or mark 'resolved'" critical posts [6][9].
  5. Under-staffing and marketing ownership. One community manager and a moderator roster never filled in [23]. The blog drifted into generic corporate content (IoT, workplace creativity, sports sponsorships) instead of support [12][24].
  6. Quiet sunset, no migration. A banner and then a redirect. No archive or knowledge-base migration was found, and member contributions disappeared behind the redirect [14][15]. Telkom's presence on MyBroadband appears to have faded too: "There's no Telkom representatives here anymore, as far as I know" (Jan 2021) [34].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Launch with people members already trust. Seeding with a named, effective staffer (Charl) gave the first threads credibility no design could [13].
  2. Make the top tasks findable. Pin staff-written guides for the five things customers search for most (billing, balances, cancellation), and keep them updated [12][24].
  3. Treat search as the front door. The forum's acquisition came almost entirely from Google [1]. Title threads and guides the way customers phrase their problems.
  4. Write the onboarding guides. Short posts explaining kudos, solutions and posting reduce confusion for first-time posters [27][28].
  5. Use a public promise to force execution. Naming the community in a response to public criticism got it shipped quickly [4].

Avoid these

  1. Don't build peer support for problems peers can't solve. If most issues need back-office access (faults, billing, cancellations), staff the forum with agents who can act, or route those posts into tickets. Otherwise it becomes a public complaints wall.
  2. Don't lead with badges. Gamification without a core of real experts, and without recognition that carries real privileges, reads as noise. Recruit and empower superusers before adding badges.
  3. Don't moderate away the anger. In a low-trust brand, deleting rants and banning competitor mentions confirms the suspicion that the space is PR. Answer and escalate instead.

Related communities worth adding

  • TelkomZA on MyBroadband: Telkom's social media team running an official support thread and PMs inside a third-party enthusiast forum (2013–c.2020). It is a "go where customers already are" model that arguably beat the owned forum.
  • MyBroadband Forum: Broad Media. South Africa's biggest tech forum and the de facto independent telecom-support community; ISPs and operators staff threads there.
  • Afrihost on MyBroadband: a South African ISP known for staff answering on MyBroadband. A useful contrast in staffed third-party presence.
  • Slovak Telekom "Telekom Forum": Deutsche Telekom group. Closed 15 June 2022 after seven years, another telecom forum shutdown [36].
  • Vodacom / MTN South Africa support communities: competitors. Check whether either ran owned forums. None was found in this research.
  • DStv (MultiChoice) customer portal or forum: cited by members in 2015 as a censored company portal [6]. Verify.

  • Staff investment 2: one named community manager and a launch-time Super Admin; the moderator roster was never published.
