Fiat Mio (fiatmio.cc, the FCC III crowdsourced concept car) · Fiat Automóveis (Fiat Brazil; now Stellantis)

A well-run 15-month co-design campaign that drew 17,000 people, then left its community to rot once the concept car was unveiled

Peak

About 17,500 registered participants from about 160 countries, 10,600 ideas, 13,500 comments and 2.3M unique visitors by the concept's unveiling at the São Paulo Motor Show, Oct 2010 (AgênciaClick Isobar art director's portfolio; Car Body Design)

Now

Defunct. Activity stopped after the Oct 2010 unveiling (real counts were almost flat from Oct 2011 to Jun 2012), and the social accounts went quiet in Jul 2011. From 2013 the unmoderated site's counters filled with what looks like spam (Dec 2014: 220,092 'ideas', 201,283 'participants', 2 comments). The site returned errors in 2013-15, and by Nov 2026 the domain hosted an unrelated freight-forwarding SEO site. Fiat still celebrates Mio in PR (15th-anniversary release, Oct 2025)

How it pays off for the company

Brand positioning and earned media rather than retention: an 'innovation and dialogue' halo for Brazil's market-leading carmaker, cheap qualitative consumer research for the design centre, and an award and conference case. Fiat's marketing director said it 'was never about sales' but would lift brand consideration. No revenue, sales or NPS figures were ever published

TL;DR

  • What it is: Fiat Brazil's fiatmio.cc (Aug 2009–Oct 2010) asked the public "In the future we are building, what must a car have in order for me to call it mine, without ceasing to serve other people?" About 17,000 registered people posted around 10,600 ideas. Designers turned them into 21 discussion topics, two rival design lines and a run of votes, and the result was the FCC III "Mio" concept shown at the 2010 São Paulo Motor Show [1][2][3][4].
  • Why people came back: a steady flow of new briefs, which the researchers call an "accordion" (open the floor, digest, issue a sharper brief). Each topic was open for 10 days, design-direction and paint votes followed, three inspiration posts went up a day, and making-of videos showed ideas being built. Add a points ranking and the promise of seeing your idea on a real car at the show [1][14][8].
  • What Fiat did to feed it: AgênciaClick Isobar ran the site with a single "brand content editor" as the bridge to Fiat's engineers. Fiat published 281 reference posts, 21 YouTube episodes (1.1M+ views), newsletters, a book and a concept film, and invited participants to the motor-show stand as "Fiat Mio Creators" [1][8][9][10].
  • Biggest mistake: no plan for after the show, and recognition that felt token. Top contributors got a tin of cookies and vague hints of a prize. The selection criteria were never explained. Twitter and Facebook stopped in July 2011, and the "open" platform Fiat promised to keep running filled with spam before it went offline [8][9][11].
  • The one lesson: staged, well-briefed co-creation can get thousands of people to do serious design work for a brand. But if the project has a fixed end date, plan the ending: say up front how ideas will be judged, credit people by name, and either retire the space cleanly or give it a standing job.

What the community is

Fiat Mio ("My Fiat") was the third in Fiat Brazil's series of São Paulo Motor Show concept cars: FCC I (2006), FCC II Bugster (2008), FCC III Mio (2010). It was the only one designed in public [1][4]. The space was fiatmio.cc, a WordPress-based site (the ".cc" nodded to Creative Commons). It went live with the key question in early August 2009 and was translated into English and Spanish after foreign demand appeared [1][9][12]. Reading needed no login; registration was needed only to post ideas or comments [9].

The site's sections were Ideias (sortable by Highlights, Most Voted and Most Discussed, with like/dislike buttons that scored each idea), Participantes (a public points ranking), Inspirações (Fiat's reference posts), a factory blog, a universities section and Sobre o projeto [12][13]. Around it sat a Twitter account, a Facebook presence (set up, unusually, as a personal profile rather than a Page), a YouTube making-of series, email newsletters, and Facebook Connect for sign-in [9][10]. Offline there was the Casa Mio, an open house at Fiat where the public could watch the car being built ("as we work in an industry founded on secrecy, we created a house open for everyone to enter", said marketing director João Ciaco) [14], and the motor-show stand, where participants were invited to see "their" car [1][10].

Every idea was published under a Creative Commons licence, which a 2013 study describes as non-commercial and share-alike, so in principle rivals could use them too [8][9]. No unofficial fan space was found; the only later discussion is occasional threads such as a 2023 r/todayilearned post (514 points, 44 comments) [15].

Origin and strategy

The seed was a 2006 "tournament of ideas" for Fiat's 30 years in Brazil. It began as market research and turned into a marketing campaign once the public piled in [1]. For FCC III, a Fiat executive wanted to shorten the lag between consumer research and launch. AgênciaClick Isobar, Fiat's digital agency for about a decade, sent executives copies of Jeff Jarvis's What Would Google Do? and proposed a three-phase plan: Mapping Scenarios, Concept Ideas and Concept Design [1][8]. Six automotive journalists were commissioned to map "the car of the future and the future of cars", and their report was distilled at a workshop into the single open question [1].

The internal champion was João Batista Ciaco, Fiat's director of advertising and relationship marketing, who later presented the case on the main stage at Cannes Lions 2011 [16][17]. Peter Fassbender, head of Fiat's Latin American Style Center, fronted the design side [18]. The stated goals were "create a product and engage consumers" [1]. Ciaco was explicit that it was not a sales or retention play: "The goal of Fiat Mio was never sales, but to get closer to people… That ends up affecting brand consideration in the future purchase of a car" (2013, translated) [8]. Researchers note that executives "felt it was improbable that lay people could come up with an idea that would surpass the quality of ideas from the experts". The crowd was there to break the experts' fixation on past solutions, not to replace them [1].

Reading the curve. Almost all genuine activity happened in the 15 months before the unveiling. Between October 2011 and June 2012 the site gained 30 ideas, 244 comments and 158 registrations, and the visitor counter had already stopped at 2,313,914. The jump from about 18,000 to 100,000+ "ideas" in 2013, with comments flat and no new visitors counted, is very likely spam (my inference). One 2012 registrant ranked last on the leaderboard was "Catheyorangel3" [9]. Participants were about 99% Brazilian [8]. One analysis puts them as mostly men aged 20 to 40; the 2013 study puts women at 25 to 30% [8][9]. At least 6,000 registrants never posted an idea [9].

What keeps people coming back

1. Staged briefs: the "accordion". The researchers' central finding is that Fiat never ran one long funnel. It alternated wide-open idea phases with internal digestion, then released a sharper brief: an open question → 21 topics, each open for 10 days with an explanatory sub-question → a vote between two design lines ("Sense", organic, versus "Precision", minimalist; Precision won) → votes on technologies, details and finally four paint schemes just before the show [1][2][4]. Each new brief was a fresh reason to return, and each vote had a visible result.

2. Cadence. Three posts a day plus Twitter reminders "inviting followers to access the Fiat Mio website and collaborate" [1]. The October 2010 home page showed a countdown ("17 days left until the Motor Show"), an open discussion question and a making-of prompt [13].

3. Seeing it get built. The episodic making-of on YouTube and the "factory blog" showed engineers and designers turning ideas into sketches, clay and the prototype, so members could see their input being used [9][10][22]. Fassbender: "It is the hand of the internet user telling us: you have to draw a car that goes in this direction" (translated) [9].

4. Status and ranking. Ideas earned points through likes and dislikes, and a public leaderboard ranked members by ideas and comments. In Nov 2013 the leader, Douglas Espíndola, had 420 points and 175 ideas [13]. The leader said he "would come home from work and stay up late sending ideas" (translated) [8]. The fourth-placed member found the scoring "a bit strange" [8].

5. A named payoff. Participants were titled "Fiat Mio Creators" and invited to the motor-show stand, where "hundreds" stood beside the car and some gave video testimonials [1]. Some top members were asked to contribute to the project book [8]. "Many of the participants also became briefly well-known as being the owner of a specific idea materialized in the prototype" [1].

What was missing: no peer-to-peer structure beyond comment threads, no lasting ritual, and nothing to do after October 2010 apart from "keep sending ideas for Fiat's next concept car", with no next concept car ever announced [13]. A 2013 usability review found it hard to locate other members' profiles, which made relationships outside the site hard to sustain. Many profiles still linked to Orkut [8]. Switching costs were nil.

The content engine

  • Inspiration posts: Fiat and the agency published 281 posts of reference images and texts about what was "inspiring and feasible" (Pirelli's sensor tyre, Google's self-driving car, robotic parking) so the crowd's ideas would be realistic [1][12][13]. This is the best-documented tactic in the case: it steered quality without rejecting anyone.
  • One editor as the bridge: "A single brand content editor condensed all the information received from the crowd" and, with Fiat's engineers, distilled it into the 21 topics [1]. That was Adriano Silva, who handled content and community management for the agency and wrote the book Fiat Mio: A História de uma Revolução (2010) [8][23].
  • Video: 21 making-of episodes (1.1M+ views) plus a produced concept film, "A Common Day in Fiat Mio's Future", for which the agency designed working interface wireframes [9][10][22].
  • Newsletters, translations, Facebook Connect, iPad and mobile versions, kiosks, direct mail: the art director lists all of them as "presences" that grew as participation outran the original small site [10].
  • Member-made content was the ideas themselves. They were curated through the "Highlights" sort, votes and the editor's synthesis, and rewarded mainly by visibility [12].

People, programs and governance

The project needed cross-department work at Fiat: style centre, engineering, marketing, communications, training, HR and product [4]. One account says 10 Style Center designers interacted with suggestions [6]; another says about 800 engineers, technicians and specialists were mobilised [9]. The agency ran the site day to day and was "the link connecting Internet users with Fiat and vice versa" [1]. The agency side included the creative VP, a lead designer and art director, a UX designer, a copywriter, motion and 3D designers, and a developer [3][10].

There was no ambassador or superuser programme. Recognition was ad hoc: some top contributors were phoned and emailed, one (fourth place) was invited to visit the development centre, and others, including the first-placed member, were not. The 2013 study calls this "a lack of equal treatment" [8]. At year end, contributors received a tin shaped like Fiat parts and filled with chocolate biscuits [8]. Selection criteria for ideas were, "from the participants' point of view, vague", and "the company did not give much feedback on how contributions were developed" (translated) [8]. On the social channels, YouTube comments, many of them "pejorative", went unanswered [9].

Growth loops

Growth came almost entirely from earned media and the brand's own channels, not from members recruiting members. The agency counted coverage in 100+ publications worldwide [8], including Wired twice, Ad Age, Car Body Design and autoevolution [4][5][24][25]. Fiat used the motor show (the largest in Latin America) as the climax, the Cannes Lions 2011 main stage (where the car was displayed downstairs) as an encore [16], and exhibited the car in Turin in 2011 for Italy's 150th-anniversary celebrations [6]. It won a 2011 IDEA Bronze in Design Strategy from the Industrial Designers Society of America [26], and it lives on as a Harvard-distributed HEC Montréal teaching case [27]. Word of mouth existed ("I invited friends… everyone with the same passion", said the fourth-placed member [8]), but nothing in the product encouraged it.

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence: none published. No sales, consideration, NPS or research-cost numbers were released.

Company and agency claims: Fiat said the project "changed the way everyone at Fiat works" and "sent the whole automotive industry to the psychoanalysis couch" [1]. Ciaco: "This project contributes to Fiat's innovation and technology positioning" [8]. Adriano Silva: the brand "rejuvenated itself a lot in terms of branding with a series of influencers" [8]. In 2025, Stellantis credited Mio with anticipating smartphone integration, touchscreens, the LED light signatures that debuted on the 2016 Toro, and electrification. These are generic industry trends, so treat the claim as PR [6][7]. Fiat's former innovation staff say the project reshaped their careers and introduced recycled-yarn upholstery [28].

Third-party estimate: a 2010 marketing blogger claimed it "saved millions of reais" in consumer research, without evidence [20].

Academic: two peer-reviewed or teaching cases (TIM Review 2014 [1]; HEC case 2015 [27]) and Brazilian and Portuguese communication studies [8][9]. All take the participation numbers from Fiat and the agency.

What they did right

  1. Briefed the crowd, not just polled it. The journalists' trend report, a single carefully worded question and 281 reference posts produced 7,078 comments in phase one, against about 500 expected, and kept ideas within reach of feasibility [1].
  2. Short, timed sprints with visible results. Ten-day topic windows, then votes whose results (Precision over Sense, the paint scheme) visibly shaped the car [1][4].
  3. Radical transparency for a secretive industry. Creative Commons, the Casa Mio open house and a factory blog made the "we're listening" claim credible to press and design juries [4][14][26].
  4. A real, physical payoff on a fixed date. A concept car at the region's biggest auto show, with participants invited as Creators. That ending gave every phase urgency [1][10].
  5. A human bridge. One editor translating between "the sacred and the profane" (engineers and lay people) is the mechanism the researchers single out [1].

What they did wrong

  1. No after-life. Fiat said fiatmio.cc "will continue the open collaboration" [4][11]. In practice, real activity flatlined within a year, Twitter and Facebook stopped in July 2011 [8][9], the site filled with apparent spam from 2013, then failed, and the domain was eventually lost to an SEO site [13][21]. Consequence: the "open innovation" legacy Fiat still promotes points at a dead, then hijacked, URL.
  2. Token recognition and broken expectations. A cookie tin, plus show-invitation emails that implied a prize which never came [8]. Of six top-20 contributors interviewed in 2013, two would take part again, two only for a prize, and two never [8]. One stopped contributing after the gift arrived.
  3. Opaque selection. The researchers flag "the need to leave out some good ideas" as a source of frustration on both sides, and warn of "crowdslapping" [1]. Participants called the criteria vague [8].
  4. Uneven access. Some top contributors were invited to the development centre and others were not [8].
  5. Weak social execution. Facebook ran as a personal profile against Facebook's terms, with no Page tools or metrics. Twitter followed only 105 accounts. YouTube comments went unanswered [9].
  6. Expectation gap on the product. The car was never meant for production, and observers said so at the time ("it moved a lot of people just to be a piece shown at the Motor Show" [20]). Casual audiences still read it as proof that crowds design badly [15].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Run co-creation as a season of short sprints (open question → topics with deadlines → votes → reveal), so members always have a current task and see what their last one changed.
  2. Supply inspiration and constraints. Curated reference posts raise idea quality without rejecting anyone. Fiat's 281 posts were the hidden engine.
  3. Appoint one visible editor who synthesises crowd input for engineers and reports decisions back to members.
  4. Show the build. Making-of episodes and an open house turned an abstract survey into a story members followed.
  5. Fix a public reveal date and invite contributors in person, credited by name.

Avoid these

  1. Launching an "ongoing" community with no owner after the campaign ends. Retire it with an archive and a thank-you, or give it a standing mandate and moderation. Never leave a branded domain to spam and expiry.
  2. Hinting at rewards you won't give, or rewarding unevenly. Promise only what is certain. Recognise by contribution, consistently, with personal, car-lover-appropriate gestures (the 2013 study suggests a scale model and a letter [8]).
  3. Hiding the judging. Publish selection criteria up front and close the loop on each idea ("used", "parked because…"), or the most committed contributors will feel used.
