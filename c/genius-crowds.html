How it pays off for the company

Two-sided licensing model: members gave product ideas for free; Genius Crowds licensed the best ones to manufacturers and retailers, kept 75% of royalties and paid the inventor 25%; later it also ran sponsored contests for brands (PetEdge, Mattel). The community was the company's supply chain and its pitch to brands, not a support or retention layer around another product.

TL;DR

  • What it was: a New York startup founded by media executive CJ Kettler (formerly of Lime and Travelzoo) that ran monthly themed contests for consumer product ideas ("College Life", "Kitchen", "Pets", "Playthings"). Members submitted, commented and voted, an expert panel chose "prototype contenders", and Genius Crowds tried to license the winners to manufacturers. Inventors would get 25% of royalties (Entrepreneur, Nov 2011). It ran from 14 Oct 2010 to May 2013, on software from and with moderators supplied by Chaordix.
  • Why people came back: a fixed monthly calendar (submit, then panel review, then improve, then final vote), frequent small rewards ($25–$100 gift cards for crowd favorites and playful "participation awards" such as Teacher's Pet and Dorm Dweller), a points score and leaderboard, and a genuinely friendly core group of named regulars (RapunzeL, Myself, Deon, jimjim421, Reddawg) who welcomed newcomers and teased each other in the blog comments.
  • What the company fed it: a chatty blog written by the CEO and moderators (theme kick-offs, inspiration, "how to add an image to your submission"), regular "state of the community" numbers, member spotlights, an Ambassador program, a forum, and constant visible changes to the rules in response to member feedback (finalist rounds, blind voting, gift cards pulled and later restored, written panelist comments on every idea).
  • Biggest mistake: it promised "a shot at the shelf" and royalties on a timeline set by large manufacturers. In two and a half years, about one community idea reached retail: the Speed Bather pet-grooming tool at PetEdge in August 2012. Kettler admitted it was "one of few products that we can say moved to market" (crowdsourcing.org, May 2013). The gap between members' expectations and the actual pipeline, plus unresolved worries about voting fairness and idea theft, wore the community down, and the business never earned enough to fund the sales work needed to close it.
  • The one lesson: keep the community's core reward inside your control and on its cadence. Gift cards, recognition and feedback kept people engaged for months, but the community was built around a payoff (licensing deals with big companies) that took years and that the company didn't control. Kettler's own conclusion: "the model should be about ideas and participation, and less about creating a hard, physical product."

What the community is

Genius Crowds (geniuscrowds.com) was a stand-alone co-creation community. Unlike most entries in this catalogue, it didn't sit beside a separate product: the community was the product. Its pitch to consumers was "help create products you want to see on the store shelves. Earn $100 for crowd favorites and big royalties for genius products" (homepage, Oct 2010). Its pitch to brands was "the American Idol for retail products", a cheaper R&D pipeline that also built "a deep and loyal relationship with their customers" (Crunchbase profile).

Spaces the company ran:

  • Topic call-outs (the core). Each month had a theme plus an always-open "Open Mic" topic. Members submitted ideas with images, commented, and "loved" or voted on them (homepage, Jun 2012).
  • Supporting site features: a dashboard activity feed, follower counts, a "Genius" points score, a leaderboard and a "Genius Picks" page (FAQ, 2011; blog index, 2012).
  • Discussion forum, launched 27 Jan 2011 to replace a "Discuss Here!" topic (blog).
  • Blog (WordPress, about 150 posts from Oct 2010 to Feb 2013, judging from the Wayback CDX index), a Facebook page, a Vimeo channel and a SlideShare deck (Vimeo; SlideShare).
  • Ambassador program (Feb 2011) with bi-monthly ambassador meetings (blog; State of Genius Crowds, Jun 2011).

No unofficial spaces turned up. Members compared Genius Crowds with Quirky and Edison Nation in the comments, and outside mentions are limited to inventor blogs (Ideas Uploaded, ivetriedthat.com). Arctic Shift, the Reddit archive, returned nothing for "geniuscrowds", and Hacker News has no stories about it.

Origin and strategy

Kettler started Genius Crowds in 2010 after Lime, the healthy-living media company backed by Steve Case's Revolution, and an eight-month stint as president of Travelzoo (Entrepreneur; Obligation.org, Jun 2013). The first call-out, "College Life", opened on 14 Oct 2010 (blog, Nov 2010).

The strategy changed twice:

  1. Turnkey brand (2010–mid 2011). The original plan was to work with a Hong Kong outsourcing partner and sell products under the Genius Crowds brand, with the inventor named on the packaging (FAQ). By June 2011 Kettler was telling members that "a strategic partner we believed could prototype and manufacture some of your ideas… has not been as efficient nor responsive as we had hoped", and she had stopped picking new prototype contenders (blog, 28 Jun 2011). She bought out that partner in July 2011.
  2. "Ingredient brand" and sponsored contests (late 2011–2013). Genius Crowds repositioned to work with "retailers and multiple manufacturers and marketers" (Entrepreneur). It added a formal partnership with Chaordix to resell crowdsourcing (Oct 2011, PR.com listing), a co-branded launch for marketers in Feb 2012 (PR.com), a pet campaign with PetEdge, and a Mattel open-innovation contest for Barbie and Hot Wheels children's bikes (PR Newswire, 7 Sep 2012).

Chaordix (Calgary, a spin-off of the crowdsourcing pioneer Cambrian House) supplied the software and, at first, the community moderators. "Jaime, Brooke, and myself all work for Chaordix," moderator Derrick wrote (blog, Jul 2011).

Trajectory. Fast early growth: about 1,000 members in five months and about 2,000 in nine. After that the numbers slow. The September 2011 figure (about 100 new users a month) doesn't fit the CEO's November 2011 claim of 5–7% weekly growth, which would mean 100 or more new members a week. Treat the press claim as optimistic. The "five thousand" at closure is a registration count. The blog comment threads suggest the active core was a few dozen regulars, an estimate based on the names that recur across posts. Engagement was heavily skewed toward commenting: one member, decimoq, "posted 35% more comments than any other member" (Sep 2011). By early 2013 longtime members said "the 'spark' had gone" (Hiatus comments).

What keeps people coming back

1. A fixed contest calendar. By 2012 every call-out ran on a published schedule: Submit (2.5–3 weeks), then Expert Panel Review (3 days), then Collaborate and Improve (1 week), then Crowd Favorites, where each member had three votes and was asked "Would you buy it?" (homepage, Jun 2012; Mar 2013). New monthly themes, some timely ("Entertainment on the Go", "New Year's Resolutions", "Back to School"), gave members a fresh prompt to return to. The two-phase format itself came from members: in December 2010 the team posted "Oops. We blew it" after changing the rules without warning, and explained that a finalist round would reduce the advantage early submissions had in collecting votes and give members "an exciting horse race finish" (blog, 9 Dec 2010).

2. Frequent, small, slightly silly rewards. The founding reward table paid $25–$100 gift cards for most active voter, most active commenter, most active overall, first to reach 1,000 "Genius" points, and crowd favorites (Nov 2010). Later, monthly "participation awards" were chosen by algorithm ("most comments in one day… most comments between the hours of 11pm and 6am") and given playful titles. Kettler said this was deliberate: "We love to convey these rewards for more immediate gratification in our process (we recognize how long product manufacturing takes!)" (Oct 2011). The company also marked milestones: the 10,000th comment, the 1,000th member, and the 2,000th member, who got a $25 Amazon card on joining.

3. Status and identity. Members had a "Genius Member" rank, follower counts and a leaderboard. The September 2011 metrics post singled out new member Jim Justice ("6 ideas, 186 comments, 66 votes, 71 forum posts, garnered 16 followers… earning him a 'Genius Member' ranking"). Members called each other "Geniuses" and the site "GC", and the welcome threads are full of in-jokes ("Now, can I borrow $25?").

4. Peer help and real bonds. Comments were the main activity. Members critiqued and improved each other's ideas, and regulars made 3D renderings of other people's concepts. Kettler named this as her proudest result: "the level of commitment and also the level of solidarity as a community, which is one of the hardest things to build" (crowdsourcing.org).

5. Ties to staff. Moderators wrote in a personal voice. The interview introducing Jaime ends with "I still live with my parents… in your mom's basement" (Jan 2011). Their farewell posts drew rows of personal goodbyes. One member wrote, "I like the way you stayed involved and maintained communication with us" (Oct 2011).

6. The big carrot: royalties. Members got $250 on signing an Exclusive Product Development Agreement, a $1,500 advance once a partner placed a firm order, and then 25% of royalties (FAQ). This was the hook that justified everything else, and the one that rarely paid out.

Switching costs: effectively none. Members owned their reputation and relationships, but the ideas themselves could be taken elsewhere, and members openly compared Genius Crowds with Quirky and Edison Nation.

The content engine

  • CEO and moderator blog. Posted roughly weekly in 2011 and thinning out in 2012: four posts from April to July 2012, then gaps until two posts in February 2013 (Wayback CDX index). Formats: theme kick-offs with research prompts (the pet campaign post asked for views on tail docking and dog fighting), results posts naming crowd favorites, behind-the-scenes and "State of the community" posts with hard numbers, member spotlights ("Rapunzel from Mattoon, Illinois, population 18,000"), panelist introductions, guest posts by licensing expert Stephen Key, and trend pieces on 3D printing and the Maker Movement.
  • How-to content built from members' own work. "So, you want to add an image to your submission…" (Jul 2012) gathered tips and example renderings from members (Chooks, Myself, Robert Francois, Glenny Rams, RapunzeL, RonnieS) covering SketchUp, Blender, thrift-store prototypes and freehand drawing, and linked to a forum thread (blog). Similar posts included "What does a great submission look like?" (Apr 2011) and "Crafting a Great Submission" (Sep 2011).
  • Expert feedback as content. From January 2012, panelists wrote comments on every submission that advanced. One member's reaction: "you actually comment each submission! This is awesome" (How we select submissions, Apr 2012).
  • UGC recognition. Gift cards, spotlights and named credits rewarded members' renderings and comments. The community even crowdsourced the product tagline "Powered by the people of Genius Crowds."

Rating 2 of 5: friendly and consistent through 2011, but small, entirely text-based, and it faded when the team shrank in 2012.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. CEO CJ Kettler. Chaordix-supplied moderators who rotated often: Eric left in Jan 2011, Jaime became lead moderator and then moved to a Chaordix QA role in Sep 2011, Derrick left in Oct 2011, and Teri took over, followed by Katie in 2012. Expert panelists included Chris Zilo (Jan 2011), guest panelists (Jul 2011) and "RC" (Mar 2012, hired "to expedite the time to market"). That is at least five moderator handoffs in about two years.
  • Ambassadors. Launched 1 Feb 2011 with five founding members (RapunzeL, Myself, Zabber, Imants, Lucas), all of whom had joined within two weeks of launch. Selection was by "significant contribution and fit". The perks were a profile badge and "extended access to moderators and panelists", with no pay. Their job was to "generate buzz… anywhere on the web" and to help newcomers (blog; FAQ). Ambassadors met with staff every two months, and those meetings visibly fed into changes, including the "State of" post and the 2012 process overhaul (Jan 2012).
  • Feedback loop. Unusually open. The team asked "how do you want to be rewarded?" and acted on what members said: a reward redesign (Jan 2011), a forum (Jan 2011), a member survey (Jun 2011), blind voting tested in Open Mic and then rolled out site-wide (Jun–Jul 2011), and gift cards restored for crowd favorites (Jul 2011).
  • Rules and moderation. The FAQ asked the community to "call out" anyone gaming the votes. Members could flag ideas and comments to admins (the CDX index shows admin "complaints" queues). In February 2012 the company partnered with Creative Barcode, a QR-code scheme for marking idea ownership, to tag finalist ideas in response to theft worries (blog).

Growth loops

  • Social voting. Members recruited friends and family to vote for their ideas, which brought in signups and also caused the main fairness complaints. A panelist conceded that "friends and family influence is something that is inherently built in" and listed "Get more people to join Genius Crowds" as a way to succeed (Is Promotion, Gaming?, Jul 2012).
  • Ambassadors and referral rewards. Members asked for referral rewards in January 2011 and the company promised them (blog). No evidence was found that a referral program actually launched.
  • Press and partner announcements (Entrepreneur, American Express OPEN Forum, the Mattel and PetEdge releases) and inventor blogs and podcasts (an Ideas Uploaded podcast interview with Kettler, Dec 2010).
  • No TV or retail flywheel. Members themselves pointed to Edison Nation's PBS show and Quirky's TV-driven buzz as what Genius Crowds lacked (Hiatus comments, Feb 2013).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: one product at retail, the Speed Bather, credited to member Carla "RapunzeL" Leming and sold as PetEdge's "Master Grooming Tools Speed Bather" (blog, Jul 2012; PetEdge listing). One paid open-innovation contest with Mattel (Sep 2012). By June 2011 the company had paid $12,325 in gift cards and advances against 17 prototype contenders.
  • Company claims: "ten to twenty" corporate relationships, and the 5–7% weekly growth figure.
  • No evidence found of revenue, royalties received, funding rounds, or whether the Mattel contest produced a bike.
  • Outcome: closed in May 2013. Kettler: "As a small startup, we frankly didn't have enough resources to do the job of business development." She tried to find a larger partner (market research firms, crowdfunding platforms) "who would engage with our community of five thousand folks, and we just didn't find [that partner]" (crowdsourcing.org). Researcher Yannig Roth used it as a case study of how expensive co-creation is to run (yannigroth.com, May 2013). A later academic table lists Genius Crowds and Quirky as platforms that "ceased activity after only a few years in operation" (ResearchGate figure).

Content engine 2: a friendly but small text blog that faded in 2012. Member ownership 3: ambassadors and member feedback shaped the rules, but members had no control over outcomes or IP. Staff investment 3: an engaged CEO and panel, with moderators on loan from a vendor who rotated often. Business impact evidence 1: one product on shelves and no revenue data. Durability 1: about 2.5 years from launch to closure.

What they did right

  1. Built a real community quickly and cheaply. About 1,800 members, 2,162 ideas and 28,000 comments in eight months, for about $12k in rewards (State of GC). Members still thanked the team for "community, friendship and recognition" when it shut down.
  2. Rewards on the community's own timeline. The company openly used monthly gift cards and playful awards to make up for slow manufacturing (Oct 2011). Kettler judged gift cards "the right approach", saying they "helped to foster a close and vocal community."
  3. Radical transparency and visible responsiveness. It published community numbers, admitted mistakes ("Oops. We blew it"), and shipped changes members asked for: finalist rounds, blind voting, a forum, panelist feedback. Members kept saying "thank you for listening."
  4. Turned power users into ambassadors and teachers. The founding ambassadors were the earliest and most helpful members, and their skills (3D renderings, naming) became official how-to content.
  5. Dealt with fairness disputes with grace. After one member swept the Home Organization round in March 2011, the winner agreed to give half his gift cards to runners-up and the company publicly thanked members "for your swift and honest reaction" (Award Re-Allocation).

What they did wrong

  1. The core promise was out of their hands. "A shot at the shelf" depended on outside manufacturers. The first manufacturing partner stalled within eight months, and two years in only one product had shipped. Kettler's diagnosis: "the expectation of the community is that they will see their products on the shelves [soon] after. And that's just not realistic… when you're dealing with large corporations. That was probably the most important lesson learned." Consequence: growing disappointment, then the hiatus and closure.
  2. Silence about the pipeline. Partners asked to stay anonymous, and Kettler called it "a delicate balance" (Entrepreneur). Members selected for "the next step" said "no one was really informed what that step entailed" (ivetriedthat, Dec 2012). A contracted inventor described "several months of quiet… I knew something was wrong" (crowdsourcing.org comment, May 2013).
  3. Voting that invited gaming. Open, popularity-based voting rewarded people with big friend networks. The company swung back and forth: it removed crowd-favorite gift cards after the March 2011 sweep, brought them back with blind voting in July 2011, and was still defending the system a year later (Jul 2012). Members never fully trusted the results.
  4. Unresolved IP and economics. Ideas were posted publicly without patents, which caused "a lot of community controversy concerning idea theft" (ivetriedthat). The 75/25 royalty split was called "a complete ripoff" when product scouts charge 25% (Hiatus comments). The Creative Barcode fix arrived in Feb 2012 and covered only finalists.
  5. Community staff on loan and constantly changing. Moderators were Chaordix employees who rotated out at least five times. The blog shrank to a few posts in 2012, and by early 2013 the sense of momentum was gone.
  6. Never solved its business model. Kettler's own words: the company needed "to be part of a larger entity" and lacked business development resources. The pivot to sponsored contests (Mattel, Sep 2012) came about five months before the hiatus.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Run the community on a published calendar with a new theme each cycle and a clear finish (Genius Crowds' submit, review, improve, final-vote rhythm). Predictable rituals bring people back.
  2. Give frequent small rewards you fully control, and make them fun (named awards, milestone shout-outs), especially when the big payoff is slow.
  3. Publish community numbers and admit mistakes in public. "State of the community" posts and "we blew it" posts built trust that lasted until the end.
  4. Promote your earliest helpful members into ambassadors with a badge, direct access to staff and regular meetings, and turn their know-how into official guides.
  5. Give expert feedback on every contribution, not only the winners. Members valued panelist comments more than votes.

Avoid these

  1. Don't make your core promise depend on a third party's timeline (retail buyers, manufacturers, app-store approval, partners). If you can't deliver the main reward within a few cycles, change the reward.
  2. Don't let popularity votes decide prizes without protection against friends-and-family voting. Separate "crowd signal" from "who gets paid", and say so clearly.
  3. Don't leave the community team to rotating contractors or let the content cadence slip while the business pivots. Members notice when the "spark" goes before management does.
