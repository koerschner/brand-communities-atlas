Paper by FiftyThree and Mix

Mix (later "Public Stream"), the remix community inside Paper by FiftyThree · FiftyThree (acquired by WeTransfer in 2018; Paper now published by Evernote Corporation / Bending Spoons)

A remix network built into a beloved drawing app that hit 1M accounts in five months and was gone in three years

Shut down 21 Aug 2017; CEO said 'a relatively small number of Paper creators continue to use the Public Stream' (FiftyThree support article, 30 Jun 2017). No official Paper community exists in 2026; the app is listed by Evernote Corporation.

How it pays off for the company

Indirect only: Mix was free and meant to fight Paper's 'blank page' problem, raise engagement and feed the Pencil stylus, which by 2015 was the company's main revenue ('business model is still to sell the hardware'). No direct monetization. When hardware ended (late 2016) and the company refocused on productivity, Mix had no business case left.

TL;DR

  • What it was: Mix (launched 16 Sep 2014; renamed "People You Follow"/"Public Stream" in 2015) was a public stream built into Paper, Apple's 2012 iPad App of the Year. Anyone could post a page, and anyone else could download it with a tap, draw on top and post the "remix" back. Every page tracked what it was "inspired by", and everything posted was CC0 public domain (The Verge, TechCrunch).
  • Why people came back: a notification when someone remixed your page, a profile counting remixes instead of followers ("25 ideas inspired by 65 remixes"), and a steady supply of starting points (templates, outlines, half-finished sketches, prompts) for users who said they "didn't know how to draw" (TechCrunch).
  • What the company fed it: veteran community hires from Flickr, Vimeo and Foodspotting, six staff-curated categories, featured "Mixers" in a weekly newsletter, brand and cause campaigns ((RED) drew 4,500+ remixes), and IRL "Remixer" nights (VizWorld, FiftyThree News).
  • Biggest mistake: Mix was never tied to how FiftyThree made money, and it competed with the social web for an audience. Within a year the company pivoted to productivity and enterprise, demoted Mix in the UI, and then shut it down with six weeks' notice and no bulk export. The CEO's own reason: creators "find a larger, more engaged audience" on Instagram, Twitter and Tumblr (shutdown notice).
  • The one lesson: an in-tool sharing community lasts only if it does something the big social networks can't, and the business depends on it. Mix's real strength was remixable starting points (utility), not audience. FiftyThree measured and celebrated sign-ups (1M accounts), never retention.

What the community is

Paper was a minimalist sketching and notes app for iPad, launched in March 2012 by four ex-Microsoft founders (Georg Petschnigg, Andrew Allen, Jon Harris and Julian Walker) (Wikipedia). Its community had three layers:

  1. Made With Paper (2012–2016): a staff-run Tumblr that reblogged user art, plus an in-app "Made With Paper Stream" added in May 2013 and a custom Tumblr theme ("Paper Stacks") for creators (Made With Paper, Sep 2012; Wikipedia).
  2. Mix (Sep 2014 – Aug 2017): the main subject here. It was a layer of "open stacks of pages" above the user's private journals. In Petschnigg's words: "a stream of things you've created, a stream of people you follow, and a stream of creations you've started" (Business Insider). Users swiped down to enter it. It had three stacks (Me, Stream, Stars) and six curated categories: Learn, Sketch, Play, Write, Design and Think (Made Mistakes). A web version at mix.fiftythree.com gave every idea its own URL, but creating and remixing happened only in the iPad app (The Verge).
  3. Unofficial and off-platform: the #MadeWithPaper hashtag on Tumblr, Instagram and Twitter, fan Tumblrs such as 53paper ("I'm not associated with Fiftythree"), and power-user tutorials, notably Michael Rose's Mastering Paper series (Made Mistakes). No dedicated subreddit exists: Arctic Shift returns nothing for r/paperbyfiftythree, r/fiftythree or similar names.

Status: defunct. The Public Stream shut down on 21 Aug 2017 (shutdown notice). WeTransfer bought FiftyThree's assets on 21 Aug 2018 (WIRED). Bending Spoons bought WeTransfer in 2024, and the App Store now lists Paper under "Evernote Corporation", with no community features (App Store; Wikipedia).

Origin and strategy

  • Champion: CEO Georg Petschnigg. The idea came from his PowerPoint years at Microsoft, where "those who came up with the best PowerPoint templates within Microsoft were seen as heroes" (The Verge). Co-founder Andrew Allen led design. Senior interaction designer Paula Guntaur says she ran all of Mix's research (focus groups, feedback sessions, usability tests) and was "1 of 4 senior interaction designers working on Mix" (portfolio).
  • The problem it targeted: blank-page anxiety. "One of the biggest complaints we had about Paper was not about Paper, but about them not feeling creative… What people were actually saying is they didn't know how to draw" (Petschnigg, TechCrunch). "The first step of learning is imitation" (The Verge). The launch post was titled "No more blank pages" (FiftyThree News).
  • Link to the business model: weak and indirect. Paper became free in February 2015 when all its paid tools were unlocked (Wikipedia). The company then decided to "make the bulk of its money through the sale of its Pencil hardware" (TechCrunch, May 2015), and Petschnigg said in September 2015 that the "business model is still to sell the hardware" (The Verge). Mix therefore made money only by raising Paper engagement and, through that, selling styluses. No source ties Mix to revenue or retention numbers.
  • The strategy moved away from Mix almost immediately. The $30M Series B in March 2015 cited Mix's 1M users but was explicitly "about… bringing a new crop of tools for creative thinking… in enterprise and education" (GeekWire; TechCrunch). Think Kit (diagram tools) followed in May 2015, and Paper 3.0 in September 2015 recast the app as a notes and ideas tool for iPhone and iPad.

Trajectory: a fast sign-up spike, helped by the in-app prompt to existing users and the invite gating, then a decline that went unmeasured publicly. FiftyThree never disclosed active Mix users or posting volume. "1M accounts" means sign-ups, roughly 7–8% of Paper's downloads at the time (my estimate: 1M ÷ 13M). Hacker News barely noticed: the launch post got 17 points and 0 comments (HN).

What keeps people coming back

  • Remixes as the status currency. Each page showed a count of remixes, and profiles read "X ideas inspired by Y remixes". "It doesn't matter how many followers you have. It matters how many times your pages have inspired others" (The Verge). This was status by contribution rather than popularity, and it was a strong design choice.
  • Notification when you are remixed. "Users… will be notified whenever something they've added to is remixed" (TechCrunch). The Verge's reviewer got an email that someone had turned his Jupiter drawing into a dress. This was the main re-engagement trigger.
  • Lineage ("inspired by"). Tap and hold showed the original page, and remix chains appeared as stacks (The Verge). Members used this to ask "how did you do this?" by drawing a note on a copy and posting it back (Made Mistakes).
  • Utility hooks: templates (storyboards, wireframes, grids, coloring outlines, game boards) and a "Stars" journal that worked like any other journal (Made Mistakes). This was Mix's most durable reason to return.
  • Prompts and challenges in the Play category ("Draw a scene from your favorite movie", AMAs run through remixes, "A Day in the Life", coloring outlines) (Made Mistakes). No streaks, badges, levels or seasons existed: no evidence found of formal gamification beyond the counts.
  • Social graph: follows, plus friend import from Tumblr, Twitter and Facebook and global search (Paper 2.2, Dec 2014) (Wikipedia).
  • Recognition: staff featuring in the stream, "Mixer" profiles named in the weekly newsletter, and reblogs on Made With Paper (FiftyThree News). Members prized the features, for example "One of my Paper artwork pieces was just featured on the Paper by FiftyThree website. Smiles all round!" (Em Holmes).
  • IRL: "Remixer" evenings, such as the first LA Remixer on 15 Jul 2015 with the leather brand This Is Ground, featuring creator Mike Macadaan (FiftyThree News; This is Ground).
  • Switching costs: low. Creations were CC0 and lived on the device, and the network effect was only an audience. When FiftyThree pulled it, members simply moved to Instagram.
  • What worked against retention: intimidation. A power user wrote: "I've felt inadequate quite a few times flipping through my stream… I rarely remix other's work out of fear of ruining what they've done" (Made Mistakes). Onboarding confused some users too: "the set up of their new community, Mix, is SO confusing! #givingup" (@gaspeprincess, Oct 2014).

The content engine

  • Company-made content:
    • The FiftyThree News weekly newsletter and Tumblr featured members' work, tips of the week, product surveys with Pencil giveaways and event invites (newsletter, Jun 2015).
    • The blog The Open Studio (2015). Content lead Amy Cao: "great content shows what the community is making, and it's our job to share their story… The business objective is to help people to get to know us, instead of telling them, 'Hey use this, buy this'" (CMX, Jul 2015).
    • The interview series "53 Creative New Yorkers You Should Know About" (FiftyThree Tumblr).
    • Later, the Medium blog A Space to Create (release notes) and free Skillshare classes with Shantell Martin and Catherine Madden (Medium).
  • Member content as the product: templates, outlines and tutorials. FiftyThree seeded categories and featured "Space Covers" templates through the Public Stream as late as 2016–17 (Medium, Paper 3.4).
  • Campaigns: (RED) with Apple (4,500+ remixes), GE brain-activity art, a CreativeMornings group remix (Jul 2015), and brand contests such as the card app Felt's design contest on Mix ("We'll announce the winners this Friday!", Apr 2015) (VizWorld; @Feltapp; @03STORIES).
  • Rewards: recognition only (features, newsletter mentions) plus occasional Pencil giveaways. Creators were never paid and had no creator program.
  • Cadence decay: the newsletter archive's last snapshot shows the 26 Jun 2015 edition still on top in 2016 (Wayback Apr 2016), and Made With Paper stopped in October 2016. The content engine faded well before the shutdown.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: FiftyThree hired "serious community talent" for launch: Heather Champ (former Flickr community manager), Dr. dalas verdugo (former Vimeo director of community) and Amy Cao (former Foodspotting community and social media lead) (TechCrunch). Champ "led community development for Mix… including internal trust and safety policies and mitigation" (LinkedIn). For an app startup this was an unusually senior community bench; I score staff investment 3 because it was strong at launch and gone within about two years.
  • Programs: no ambassador, MVP or moderator program, and no member-run spaces. Curation was entirely top-down (staff-picked categories and features). Member ownership scores 2: CC0 licensing was generous, but members had no governance role and no bulk export when the service closed.
  • Feedback loop: in-app pages and product surveys ("We'd love your feedback as we develop the next generation of creator tools") (newsletter). No public ideas portal or forum existed; support ran on Zendesk.

Growth loops

  • Inside the app: existing Paper users got an in-app invitation, and a page needed only one swipe. That made the 1M accounts cheap, but they were existing customers, not new ones (TechCrunch).
  • Web: every idea had a public URL for SEO and sharing, and remix tweets carried "X's remix on Mix @fiftythree" links (many examples in 2015, e.g. @sophieverret). Non-Paper users could only look, so web visitors had to install an iPad app to take part.
  • Educators evangelized it ("will do wonders in Education", @k_shelton; #edtech posts from @JacBCreative).
  • Partner campaigns ((RED), CreativeMornings, Felt) borrowed audiences.
  • No evidence found of any measured acquisition effect from Mix.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: essentially none. FiftyThree published sign-ups (1M), reach (210 countries) and campaign counts (4,500 remixes), but no DAU/MAU, retention, Pencil attach rate or revenue tied to Mix. Business-impact evidence scores 1.
  • Company claims: "Few productivity services have grown to reach such a global scale so quickly" (VizWorld press release). Mix won the Communication Arts Interactive Annual and an Edison Bronze in 2015 (Wikipedia).
  • Counter-evidence: the CEO's own shutdown letter admitted low usage, and "FiftyThree was not profitable at the time of the acquisition" (Petschnigg via SlashGear/WIRED). Durability scores 1: under three years, with no successor.

What they did right

  1. A status metric built on contribution. Counting remixes rewarded generativity, not follower count, and suited a skill-anxious audience (The Verge).
  2. They solved a real product problem with community. Templates and half-finished pages addressed the top complaint ("I can't draw"), so the community added utility, not just a feed (TechCrunch).
  3. Zero-friction placement. Mix sat one swipe above the user's own journals, and a remix took one tap. This is why sign-ups were fast (Business Insider).
  4. Senior community and trust-and-safety staff before launch, plus a staged rollout (about 5,000 users a day) to protect quality and the infrastructure (TechCrunch).
  5. A storytelling content strategy built on members' work, not product pitches (CMX), plus cause and brand campaigns that gave members reasons to post together.
  6. An open license (CC0) that made every page legally remixable and turned Mix into "one of the largest stores of open source creativity on the web" (VizWorld).

What they did wrong

  1. No business model tied to the community. Revenue came from hardware, then from enterprise and subscription ambitions. Mix was a cost center that nobody had to defend, and when Pencil was discontinued at the end of 2016 (WIRED) it was cut within months. Consequence: shutdown in 2017.
  2. Strategy whiplash. Six months after launch the company raised money for enterprise and education. Paper 3.0 (Sep 2015) removed Mix's pull-down entry point, renamed it "People You Follow" and folded it into a grid of "Spaces" (Made Mistakes; Wikipedia). It also removed the journals metaphor that many users loved; FiftyThree promised to bring journals back in the same letter that killed the stream. Consequence: the community lost visibility and identity, and confused users.
  3. Fighting the social web for audience. "Many creators find a larger, more engaged audience by sharing publicly on Instagram, Twitter, Tumblr and elsewhere" (Petschnigg, shutdown notice). Posting was limited to iPad, and the web was view-only, which capped reach. The Verge had already warned that similar products, Mixel and DrawQuest, "ultimately failed… perhaps because they didn't provide enough standalone utility" (The Verge).
  4. Celebrating vanity metrics. The "Thanks 1,000,000!" microsite marked accounts created, while activity apparently drained unnoticed (no public active-user figure ever).
  5. Poor shutdown handling. Members got six weeks to save their posts, and FiftyThree offered no bulk export. One user asked: "Is there a way to export all ideas from mix? Or do I need to do it one by one?" (@pungme). A former FiftyThree engineer published a scraper to archive profiles (aseemk gist). Every mix.fiftythree.com and paper.fiftythree.com link broke (Made Mistakes). The promised website showcasing #MadeWithPaper from social networks has no trace: the Made With Paper Tumblr stayed frozen at October 2016 through January 2019 (Wayback).
  6. The community and content team disappeared. Headcount went from 51 (Dec 2014, with plans for 20–25 more) to 14 by 2018 (TechCrunch; Wikipedia). The newsletter and blog went quiet a year or more before the shutdown.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make status about being built upon. A count of remixes, forks or duplicates rewards generous contributors and suits beginners better than follower counts.
  2. Turn the community into starting points that fix your product's hardest moment (the blank page, the empty project). Templates are utility that general social networks can't offer.
  3. Notify creators when someone builds on their work. It was Mix's most powerful re-engagement loop.
  4. Put the community one gesture from the core workflow and roll it out in stages with trust-and-safety staff in place.
  5. Run campaigns that give members a shared prompt (causes, brand collaborations, IRL remix nights), and tell members' stories in your owned content.

Avoid these

  1. Don't run a community the business doesn't need. If it isn't tied to retention, conversion or revenue that someone reports on, it will be the first thing cut in a pivot. Measure active contributors and retention lift, not accounts.
  2. Don't compete with Instagram for audience. Make posting outward easy and keep the owned space for what only it can do (remixable files, lineage, templates). Otherwise creators leave for bigger audiences, as FiftyThree's CEO admitted they did.
  3. If you shut down, export everything and keep the URLs alive. Six weeks, one-by-one saving and broken links burned goodwill. Promising a replacement you never ship is worse.

Related communities worth adding

  • Mixel — Khoi Vinh — an iPad collage-remix social app; failed before Mix, cited by The Verge as a cautionary precedent.
  • DrawQuest (Canvas) — Chris Poole — daily drawing-prompt remix community on iPad; shut down in 2014.
  • Made With Paper Tumblr / #MadeWithPaper — FiftyThree — the brand-run UGC showcase that preceded Mix; frozen in 2016.
  • Paste by WeTransfer — FiftyThree/WeTransfer — the team collaboration product FiftyThree pivoted to.
  • Figma Community — Figma — the remix ("duplicate") model that succeeded because it was tied to professional workflows (compare).
  • Procreate Folio / Procreate forums — Savage Interactive — the iPad drawing community that outlasted Paper (compare).
  • Notability Gallery — Ginger Labs — an in-app public gallery of shared notes in a similar category.
  • Flickr community (Heather Champ era) and Vimeo community (dalas verdugo era) — community programs whose leaders FiftyThree hired.
  • Foodspotting — OpenTable — a food photo-sharing community (Amy Cao's previous role); defunct.
