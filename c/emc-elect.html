How it pays off for the company

Influencer and advocacy marketing for an enterprise storage vendor: a peer-trusted network of bloggers, partner engineers and customers that amplified launches and events, gave product teams feedback, and fed the support forum (ECN). The charter promised 'increase[d] investment and engagement with EMC'. No revenue figures were ever published.

TL;DR

  • What it was: EMC's yearly, peer-judged recognition program for people outside and inside the company (bloggers, partner engineers, customers, employees) who talked about EMC storage online. EMC announced it on 2012-11-15 with 10 "Founders" and named its first 75 members on 2013-01-15. Classes ran 2013–2016. After the Dell merger it was folded into "Dell EMC Elect" (153 members, 2017), and no 2018 class was ever named.
  • Why people came back: the title lasted one calendar year and had to be re-earned, and the people judging it were last year's members. Members got VIP lounges and private NDA briefings at EMC World, early roadmap access, travel to launches, a private forum and later a Slack. The recognition and access were the product.
  • How EMC fed it: a two-to-four-person team (Matt Brender in social marketing, Mark Browne in support-community eServices, later Sean Thulin and Dennis Smith) ran briefings, conference lounges, an "Ask the Expert" series led by Elect members, a member podcast (Tech sElect) and CrowdChats. Members wrote most of the content on their own blogs and Twitter.
  • Biggest mistake: the program lived on two people and a marketing budget line, and it never published hard ROI. When the 2016 Dell–EMC merger brought a re-org, Browne was moved to sales analytics in late 2017. By spring 2018 members were asking whether nominations had opened, and visiting bloggers were being flown in under Dell's generic "Press, Analysts and Influencers" program instead.
  • The one lesson: peer judging, one-year terms and real insider access build a loyal, self-policing advocate cohort cheaply. But unless the program is tied to measured business outcomes and owned by more than its founders, the first re-org will kill it.

What the community is

EMC Elect was a recognition and advocacy program in the mold of Microsoft MVP and VMware vExpert. Its organizers said so openly: Brender's launch post was titled "Move Over MVPs, vExpert Make Room" (itechthereforeiam, 2012-11-15). EMC defined it as "a community-driven recognition and thank you for individual's engagement with EMC as a brand over the last calendar year", judged on Engagement, Commitment and Leadership (ECN space, archived 2016).

Spaces:

  • Official: the EMC Elect space and a members-only space on the EMC Community Network (ECN, a Jive forum) (FAQ, archived 2014); the #EMCElect hashtag; an Elect lounge, private briefings and meetups at EMC World, VMworld, Cisco Live, SAP Sapphire and Oracle OpenWorld (Bayside Chronicle, 2014-05-21; CrowdChat, 2016); a Slack for current members and alumni from 2016 (CrowdChat); and the Tech sElect podcast on SoundCloud and iTunes (Apple Podcasts).
  • Unofficial: members' own blogs (GeekFluent, Bayside Chronicle, PenguinPunk, 50mu, Thulin' Around, VMtoday and others), which carried most of the program's visible output. Reddit footprint: no evidence found of any meaningful Reddit presence. The program lived on blogs, Twitter and Google+.
  • Status: defunct. The last class was 2017 (details under "What they did wrong").

Origin and strategy

  • Champions. Two employees who started out "butting heads" over competing recognition-program ideas: Matt Brender (later Broberg), coming from marketing and the social team, and Mark Browne, community manager of the ECN Support Forums in Cork, Ireland, coming from customer support. They agreed they had "the same goal: recognizing those for doing the good work they are already doing" (Influence Marketing Podcast EP009, 2017-08-17). Browne took the internal and support side; Brender took the outside audience of bloggers and tweeters (same source). Broberg's LinkedIn credits the program to a wider relaunch of ECN "with new modern social collaboration tools, new user experience, gamification, advocacy program (ELECT)" (LinkedIn). They have said they took inspiration from Dell's TechCenter Rockstars, which started in 2012 (Bayside Chronicle, 2017-02-28; vInfrastructure).
  • Stated strategy. The charter: "designed this program to increase investment and engagement with EMC within our global community – customers, partners, and EMC employees alike". The goal was "unprecedented access into EMC product and service teams" for members (Charter, archived 2016). In return, EMC told The Register it would get "a low-latency, trusted connection into the highest value influencers in our social ecosystem" (The Register, 2013-01-17).
  • Cold-start design. To avoid "who wants to be in a group that no one is in yet", the team hand-picked 10 Founders, heavy contributors on ECN and Twitter, and brought them in "at the design stage to ensure EMC Elect is defined by the community itself". Brender's plan was "Start with 10, Room for 150" (itechthereforeiam). The Founders then led the call for nominations (GeekFluent, 2012-11-15).
  • Business model link. EMC sold six- and seven-figure enterprise storage through partners. The program targeted the "micro-influencers" (Browne's term for people with under 30,000 followers) whose peer recommendations shape enterprise buying (EP009). A 2017 Dell EMC write-up argued that "a large majority of our customers turn to people they know and respect for referrals above any other source" (Dell EMC blog snippet, veddiew.typepad.com, 2017-08-17; Typepad has since closed and the text survives only as a search snippet). Support deflection was a secondary aim: members were encouraged to answer questions on ECN and to lead "Ask the Expert" threads (Bayside Chronicle, 2013-03-22).

Trajectory: demand rose every year (nominations went 270 → 375 → 450 → ~700), while the class size was deliberately held at 72–153. The program never faded from lack of interest. It stopped after the 2017 class, about a year after the Dell–EMC deal closed on 2016-09-07 (Dell).

What keeps people coming back

  1. A title you must re-earn every year. Membership was valid "for one calendar year… renewal is not automatic" and was based only on the previous calendar year's contributions (FAQ). That built a yearly ritual: nominations open (announced at VMworld in 2014) → peer judging → a coordinated "social celebration" of blogs and tweets on announcement day. Members blogged their renewals ("third year in a row"), which kept them producing all year (GeekFluent 2015). About two-thirds of each class renewed.
  2. Peer-judged status. From 2015, a panel of the previous year's Elect scored nominees on volume, frequency, relevance, technical merit and quality, with each nominee read by more than one judge. Employee scores were cut 20% and partner scores 10% to offset their privileged access. Small bonuses went to people who covered other vendors in a "balanced, even fashion" and to "connectors" who bridged communities. The judges also capped employees at 50% of a class for credibility (GeekFluent 2015). Being picked by respected peers made the badge worth more than a vendor-handed award.
  3. Insider access. FAQ perks: a certificate and gift, a directory listing, the logo, a private community, exclusive online events, priority for betas and roadmap reviews, "direct feedback mechanisms into all participating EMC product teams", and VIP treatment at tier-1 events (FAQ). In practice that meant Elect-only blogger briefings, a lounge at EMC World, data-center tours, and a roadmap meeting where one member saw "road map items that even his manager hadn't seen yet" (Thulin' Around; Bayside 2014; EP009).
  4. Paid travel and VIP treatment. Members were flown to a product launch in Milan (GeekFluent 2014). In 2016 the Elect received "Social Influencer" invitations to Dell EMC World that were treated "in the same category as main stream press and analyst media", with executive access (Victor Forde, 2016-10-23). One Australian blogger had flights and hotels for Dell EMC World 2016 and 2017 paid "via the EMC Elect program" (PenguinPunk).
  5. Belonging and identity. Members got personalized membership cards (Browne put an Irish motto and a Celtic tree of life on his to encourage others to do the same), custom shirts, a welcome kit with a handwritten-style letter that Chad Sakac singled out for praise, group "marches", charity water walks with vExperts, Cisco Champions and SAP Mentors, and "vBar crawls" at VMworld (Bayside 2013; VMtoday; Bayside 2014; X, 2017). From 2016, "once an EMC Elect, always an EMC Elect" made alumni a recognized tier (Bayside 2016).
  6. A personal bond with the staff "trustee". Browne described himself as a trustee who connected members to executives and business units. When he left, members told him "I could never truly leave" (Bayside 2017-12-22). The bond was strong, but it was also a single point of failure.
  7. Career capital. Members put the badge on résumés and business cards next to MVP and vExpert, which Brender explicitly designed for (itechthereforeiam). Partners announced members in press releases (Armedia; Xiologix).

There were no streaks, leaderboards or in-product hooks. This was a human-curated status program, not a gamified one (ECN itself had gamification).

The content engine

  • Company-produced: Elect-only bloggers' briefings at launches, so members could publish "day of" announcement posts (GeekFluent 2016); ECN blog posts announcing each class and explaining how members were selected (selection post, archived); CrowdChats, hangouts and the yearly kickoff chat (CrowdChat).
  • Member-made, company-amplified: the Tech sElect podcast, "the Dell EMC Elect community's channel", recorded mostly at conferences (Cisco Live, VMworld) with members and guests, reached at least 57 episodes by late 2017 (Apple Podcasts). Elect-led Ask the Expert threads on ECN started with Fibre Channel routing, run by Founders Allen Ward, "dynamox" and "RRR" before EMC World 2013, and wrapped up on video (Dell Community; Bayside 2013). Live tweeting and blogging from conferences filled out the rest. Victor Forde published hashtag analytics to show the Elect "made their presence felt" at Dell EMC World 2016 (Forde).
  • Rules that kept it credible: members had "no further obligations" and did not officially represent EMC; "technical preference or opinion will not be grounds for removal" (FAQ). Members' disclosures repeat that "there is no requirement for me to blog" (PenguinPunk). EMC kept the right to reuse members' content with credit.
  • Score, content engine 3/5: steady member output and a real podcast, but no structured education or how-to program aimed at the wider user base. Content followed the conference calendar.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: Brender (lead program manager on the social team; left for Intel and later Sensu) and Browne (project manager, then sole "trustee" after the merger). Sean Thulin facilitated in 2013–14. Dennis Smith joined as Principal Engagement Manager in June 2014, coming from Dell's Rockstars, and Mary Kilgallen handled logistics (Bayside 2014-06-19; Bayside 2014-05-21). At peak the team was perhaps 2–4 people (an estimate from these posts), with marketing paying for travel. Score, staff investment 3/5.
  • Governance: Founders, then the previous year's members, served as judges. Selection was transparent: published criteria, score adjustments and published nomination counts. Removal applied only to "discriminatory, hateful or slanderous" statements. Competitors' employees were eligible, while analysts and US government employees were not (FAQ). Score, member ownership 4/5: members co-designed the program, judged it and ran its podcast and Ask the Expert threads, but EMC owned the brand and the budget ("It is and always has been EMC's program" — GeekFluent).
  • Feedback loop: roadmap sessions, betas and product-manager conversations at events (e.g. feedback on MyService360 at EMC World 2016 — Think Meta). No public evidence found of specific product changes traced to Elect input.

Growth loops

  • Peer nominations as outreach: each nomination window turned members into recruiters, and self-nomination was explicitly encouraged (FAQ). Nominations grew about 2.6× between 2013 and 2016.
  • Launch amplification: briefed members published at launch time on their own blogs, with their own audiences and SEO. This is the main way the program could have reached customers. It was never measured publicly.
  • Cross-program networking: shared events with VMware vExperts, Cisco Champions and SAP Mentors (Bayside 2014). Many members belonged to several programs (Apple Podcasts description).
  • Press: The Register's launch story; Browne claimed it reached "an audience of 2.5 million" (Bayside 2013).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none found. No filings, case studies or published ROI figures. Browne said the program "yields, revenue, business growth and reputation" and has "a huge ROI", and he wrote an MSc thesis claiming "micro influencers carry far more ROI than the presumed superstars". The underlying data was never published (Bayside 2017-02-28; Bayside 2017-08-15; EP009). In the same interview Browne admitted that "communicating the value of the program was daunting at first". He advised that "sharing anecdotal successes and hard data with management… often gets overlooked".
  • Adjacent claim (unverified): "ECN members spend 250% more with EMC than non members" (2014 third-party blog quoting EMC; the page now returns an error — Vengreso). That is correlation, it covers the whole forum rather than Elect, and it is company-sourced.
  • Revealed preference: Dell kept a broader "Press, Analysts and Influencers" program for Dell Technologies World 2018 and invited at least one former Elect blogger under it (PenguinPunk, 2018). The company still valued influencer coverage; it just didn't value a separate peer-run program. Score, business impact evidence 1/5.

What they did right

  1. Solved cold start with Founders. Ten respected, already-active contributors co-designed the program and recruited the first class. It launched with credibility and 270 nominations in a month (itechthereforeiam; The Register).
  2. Credible, transparent selection. Peer judges, published nomination counts, score handicaps for employees and partners, bonuses for vendor-balanced coverage, and a 50% cap on employees. Outsiders like Stanclift still called it "one of the most elite… mostly due to the size and the process" (Medium, 2017; GeekFluent 2015).
  3. Freedom to criticize, written into the rules. No obligations, and opinions explicitly protected. That protected members' independence, and with it the credibility that made them worth courting (FAQ).
  4. Access worth having. Private briefings, NDA roadmaps and press-level conference access. Members said the briefings impressed them most (Bayside 2014).
  5. Tied to support, not just marketing. Elect-led Ask the Expert threads put expert knowledge into the support forum, giving the program a non-marketing payoff (Bayside 2013).
  6. Gave members their own channels. The podcast, Slack and alumni status shifted ownership toward members (CrowdChat).

What they did wrong

  1. Depended on two people. It started as two employees' side project. Brender left before the merger, and when Browne was reassigned "due to a corporate re-org" in late 2017, nobody took over. No 2018 nominations opened, and Founder Rob Koper wrote in June 2018 that "Dell pulled the plug… I don't believe it will survive this deathblow" (Bayside 2017-12-22; X). Consequence: the community and its badge vanished overnight. No announcement of the shutdown was found; members found out by noticing nothing happened.
  2. Never proved ROI in terms executives use. Value claims stayed anecdotal or locked in a thesis. In a merger looking for synergies, a hard-to-measure marketing line item was an easy cut (EP009).
  3. A post-merger rebrand that diluted the program. The 2017 merger with Dell TechCenter Rockstars doubled the class to 153 and widened scope to all of Dell EMC enterprise. Nominations ran for only about 17 days (Feb 28 – Mar 17, 2017) (Bayside). The program grew just as its budget and owner were about to disappear.
  4. A painful cull without warning. In 2016 the class was cut from 102 to 72 because judges felt "the excitement and direction of EMC Elect was not proceeding at the pace we believed it should". The organizer predicted people would ask "why their EMC Community champion was not selected" (Bayside 2016). The 2015 judge who wrote the numbers posts was dropped; he took it graciously but expected to lose bloggers'-briefing invites (GeekFluent). Swinging the class size up and down signaled that the rules were arbitrary.
  5. Credibility and diversity gaps. Employees made up 43–48% of classes, uncomfortably close to the judges' own 50% ceiling. The 2016 class was 93% men, and a public discussion on Google+ about it went nowhere (GeekFluent 2016). Critics in the press mocked the launch language ("If your abdominals are contracting…" — The Register). Even members called such programs "marketing programs, and not necessarily reflective of any pinnacle of particular expertise" (Stanclift).
  6. Low visibility to customers. In 2014 people commented that the Elect "don't stand out enough" at conferences (Bayside 2014-06-19). The program's value flowed mostly to members and marketing, not to the customer base it was meant to influence.
  7. All on platforms the company didn't control. Twitter, Google+, SoundCloud and Jive. After the shutdown the ECN pages moved to Dell's forum, and the history now survives mainly in members' blogs and the Wayback Machine.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Launch with hand-picked Founders who help design the program and then judge it. It fixes cold start and gives the badge legitimacy on day one.
  2. Make status expire yearly and let peers award it. One-year terms plus peer judging produce steady contribution and a yearly announcement ritual that markets itself.
  3. Publish the rubric, and handicap insiders. Score cuts for employees and partners, bonuses for balanced coverage, and caps on insider share protect credibility.
  4. Trade access for nothing in writing. Early roadmaps, private briefings and staff time work as rewards only if members are free to criticize. Put that freedom in the rules.
  5. Connect champions to support content as well as marketing. Expert-led Q&A threads create value for ordinary users and a second justification for the budget.

Avoid these

  1. One-person (or two-person) ownership. Write the playbook down, give it an executive sponsor, and have more than one staff owner, or the first re-org ends it.
  2. Unmeasured value. From year one, track the program's link to pipeline, deflected tickets or launch reach in numbers finance accepts.
  3. Silent shutdowns and erratic class sizes. If you have to shrink or stop, say so publicly, with an alumni path. Leaving members to find out from the absence of nominations burns your most loyal advocates.
