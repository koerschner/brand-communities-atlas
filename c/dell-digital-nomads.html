TL;DR

  • What it was: DigitalNomads.com was a WordPress blog-community that Dell launched on 12 Aug 2008 alongside its new Latitude E-series business laptops. It was aimed at "officeless" mobile workers and was run mostly by one Dell communications manager, Bruce Eric Anderson, the self-styled "Chief Nomad" (Neville Hobson; WordofMouth.org; Dell blog).
  • Why people came back (the little they did): to read. The draw was well-made content from big-name guest bloggers (Hugh MacLeod, Andy Sernovitz, John Jantsch, Phil Torrone, John Biggs), Big Think video interviews, and a crowdsourced whitepaper. Members almost never talked to each other: sampled posts drew 0–6 comments.
  • What Dell fed it: a hand-picked roster of "several dozen" guest bloggers, a Twitter account, a free "Digital Nomad badge", a Flickr pool and YouTube group whose member photos and videos rotated through the site banner, and a whitepaper produced through Techdirt's Insight Community (anniversary post, Aug 2009; Techdirt).
  • Biggest mistake: Dell treated a launch campaign as a community. There was no core group of members, no full-time community manager, no rituals, and (until May 2009) comments required a login. Posting fell from about 22 a month to 2–3 a month in 2009. FeverBee called it "close to death" in Oct 2009, and after Anderson left for Edelman it was "abandoned" (FeverBee; uber.la via Brave snippet).
  • The lesson: a big launch buys attention, not belonging. Unless members get to know each other, and someone's full-time job is to make that happen, a brand "community" is a content site with a shelf life.

What the community is

DigitalNomads.com ("Powered by DELL") was a WordPress site with these sections: Nomad Stories, Media Gallery, Tools & Tips, Downloads, Digital Nomad Badge, Nomad News, Nomad Blog and Nomad Profile (Wayback, Sep 2008). Registered members could comment, join the Flickr pool and YouTube group, and download a badge. Links pointed out to Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter and YouTube "Digital Nomads" spaces (Wayback, Nov 2009). A sister subdomain, whitepaper.digitalnomads.com, published the best answers from Dell-sponsored "cases" on Techdirt's Insight Community expert network (Techdirt, 4 Nov 2008).

It was an official Dell space, staffed by Dell's corporate communications and social media team. Anderson ran it, with Chief Blogger Lionel Menchaca also involved. Anderson said: "@LionelatDell and I, or any other Dell employee, are the only ones who are paid to contribute to this site" (WordofMouth.org, 19 Aug 2008). No evidence was found of any unofficial or fan-run counterpart. The large modern r/digitalnomad subreddit and the wider "digital nomad" movement have no link to Dell. Arctic Shift and Hacker News searches found a single 2008 HN submission ("Digital Nomads (new Dell blog)", 2 points, 0 comments) (HN Algolia).

Status today: defunct. The domain is now a domain-brokerage business ("DigitalNomads.com | Domain Brokerage, Branding & Advisory", live fetch on 2026-11-07).

Origin and strategy

  • Launch context. On 12 Aug 2008 Dell held a webcast press event in San Francisco, London and New Delhi for seven Latitude and three Precision mobile laptops. They offered up to 19 hours of battery life and the "Latitude ON" instant-on mode (VentureBeat). Andy Lark, Dell's VP of Global Marketing, opened by presenting "Dell's concept of the digital nomad, key to the positioning and marketing message for the new Latitudes, and announced a new but related presence on the web: DigitalNomads.com" (Hobson). Dell kept using "digital nomads" as product positioning afterwards too, for example in a 24 Sep 2008 press release (Business Wire).
  • The theory. Hugh MacLeod (gapingvoid), one of the guest writers, described it as "indirect marketing… an 'Alignment' play… The good news is, Alignment plays can be extremely effective. The bad news is, they take FOREVER to gather momentum" (quoted in Mashable, 21 Aug 2008). Dell's own framing was that it was "not about Dell, but about the broader community of individuals who call themselves digital nomads" (Bob P[earson], Dell blog, 5 Mar 2009).
  • Where it sat in Dell's social program. It belonged to Dell's 2006–2009 social media push after "Dell Hell": Direct2Dell (2006), IdeaStorm and StudioDell (2007), and the ReGeneration green community (2008). A case-study summary lists "online communities… for Dell's environmental efforts called Regeneration and technophiles called Digital Nomads" (MBA Knowledge Base, secondary source).
  • Business-model tie. The tie was weak. There was no subscription to retain, no support deflection (Dell's support forums were separate), and no product-feedback loop like IdeaStorm's. Its only business purpose was brand alignment for the Latitude line and launch buzz.
  • Champions. Bruce Eric Anderson (senior communications manager), with Bob Pearson (VP Communities & Conversations) and Andy Lark (marketing) as executive sponsors (Dell blog; Hobson).

Trajectory: activity peaked in the first four months, the period of launch PR and paid or invited celebrity contributors. Output halved in spring 2009, dwindled through the summer, and stopped in November 2009, about 15 months after launch.

What keeps people coming back

Very little. Taking the checklist item by item:

  • Rituals and cadence: none. Posts appeared on most weekdays in late 2008, but there were no recurring member events, live sessions or challenges. The only "event" was the launch-day Twitter Q&A (Hobson).
  • Progress and status: a single free "Digital Nomad badge" with downloadable "footprints" for email signatures and websites (May 2009 post). Richard Millington noted within six weeks that "the Digital Nomads badge isn't getting used much. I think that's because it's free." He proposed making it an earned "badge of honour", adding top-nomad lists, and closing the group to applicants. No evidence was found that Dell adopted any of these ideas (FeverBee, 23 Sep 2008).
  • Identity and belonging: the "digital nomad" label was a strong, aspirational identity, and the site's best idea. Content was about the topic, though, not the people. As Millington put it, "there was no content about the community… People are desperate to know what other people in the community are doing" (FeverBee).
  • Recognition: the closest thing was member photos from the Flickr pool rotating through the homepage banner, added in the Dec 2008 redesign. A member's reply, "I just slid a handful of mobile office pics to your flickr group", is about the only evidence of members responding to it (redesign post).
  • Peer support and accountability: no forum or Q&A. One "Ask Digital Nomads" post appeared (Nov 2008), but member-to-member threads never formed.
  • Parasocial ties: Anderson wrote openly and personally (his first co-working visit, his family). Guest bloggers had their own audiences, but those audiences followed the writers, not the site.
  • Friction: until 29 May 2009, commenting required logging in or creating an account. Dell dropped the requirement only after "nudging" from blogger Andy Sernovitz (May 2009 post).
  • Switching costs: none. Members kept nothing on the site (no profiles of value, no history, no reputation).

The content engine

This was the one part that worked, at least at first.

  • Celebrity and expert guest bloggers. Contributors included Hugh MacLeod, Andy Sernovitz, John Jantsch (Duct Tape Marketing), Phil Torrone (MAKE), John Biggs (CrunchGear), Chanpory Rith (LifeClever), Jay White (Dumb Little Man), Jake McKee and Shel Holtz (anniversary post; Dell blog). Formats were tips listicles ("12 Essential Services for the Digital Nomad"), gear reviews, security advice and essays. Mashable praised "very little overt Dell product tie-ins" (Mashable).
  • Video interviews. A Big Think interview series was "sponsored by Dell and digitalnomads.com", featuring Fred Wilson, Henry Blodget, Jimmy Wales, Matthew Modine and Van Jones, among others (Big Think; CDX post slugs).
  • Crowdsourced whitepaper. Dell sponsored "cases" on Techdirt's Insight Community, a paid-expert Q&A network run by Mike Masnick's Floor64. The best answers became whitepaper.digitalnomads.com posts and then "Chapter One" of a whitepaper released 5 Mar 2009. Dell billed it as "the world's first crowd-sourced whitepaper" and promised "a long book we will write together" (Techdirt; Dell blog). No Chapter Two was found.
  • Curated links: a Delicious bookmark list and a #digitalnomads Twitter hashtag (anniversary post).
  • Weakness: Millington found the content "dry" for a company of Dell's scale and wanted practical material: which trains have Wi-Fi, how much coffee buys six hours in a café, the best places in the world to work from (FeverBee, Sep 2008). By late 2009 posts were mostly guest series from small bloggers ("BusinessBackpacker", a security vendor). That suggests the curated A-list roster had moved on.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: essentially one owner, Anderson, who also had other communications duties. Three community-team members handled the launch-day Twitter account (Hobson), and Menchaca helped at the start (The Web Pitch snippet). Millington: "I'm guessing nobody had the full time job of developing the digital nomads website into a full-fledged community" (FeverBee).
  • Programs: the invited guest-blogger roster was the only contributor program. There was no ambassador, superuser or moderator program. Flickr and YouTube submissions were moderated "to ensure they meet public decency guidelines" (redesign post).
  • Feedback loop: Dell listened on site design. It shipped a Dec 2008 redesign "many of you told us you wanted" and dropped the comment login in May 2009. No evidence was found that anything flowed back into Latitude product decisions, unlike IdeaStorm.
  • Staff turnover: in 2009 Dell's social media team was cut back. A case summary says "due to the recession pressure social media team had to reduce headcount which led to the departure of key people" (MBA Knowledge Base, secondary). Anderson joined Edelman Digital as a VP. PR Week noted he "most recently worked on the company's Digital Nomads Web site" (PR Week, late 2009 per the article's Michael Brito start date of 16 Nov; exact date not verified). In Oct 2009 he was still described as "Social Media Evangelist for Dell and creator of DigitalNomads.com" (GlobeNewswire, 19 Oct 2009). The last post went up on 5 Nov 2009, so the timing of his exit and the site's death line up.

Growth loops

  • Launch PR and influencer seeding was the main loop: "nearly every top communications/business blogger either wrote for the community or wrote about the community" (FeverBee). Coverage included Mashable, VentureBeat, PR Week, Neowin, The VAR Guy and WordofMouth.org.
  • Guest bloggers' audiences brought one-off visits through cross-links and trackbacks. One example: C4 Workspace thanking Anderson for a mention.
  • Badges and footprints were meant as a backlink loop ("we'd love it if you linked your badge back to this blog :)"). There is no evidence it worked.
  • SEO was a potential loop: Dell owned the exact-match domain for a term that later became huge. Dell abandoned it before "digital nomad" went mainstream, though, and the domain was eventually resold.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none found. There were no sales, lead, retention or NPS figures. The Latitude E-series results were never attributed to the site in any filing or earnings call we found.
  • Company claims: "thousands" joined (Mar 2009) and "tens of thousands of hits a month" (Jul 2009) (Dell blog; Washington Post). For contrast, in the same period Dell reported around $6.5m in sales from its @DellOutlet Twitter offers (Guardian, Dec 2009), which shows where its measurable social returns actually came from.
  • Academic research: none found (OpenAlex search returned nothing relevant).

What they did right

  1. A strong identity, not a product. Dell built the space around a lifestyle ("business as unusual") instead of laptops, and reviewers noticed: "very little overt Dell product tie-ins" (Mashable). Dell got a good term early, before "digital nomad" went mainstream.
  2. A world-class launch. It was tied to a product event, live-tweeted (an early example of a brand doing so), and seeded with A-list bloggers. Neville Hobson called the event "a great example of integrating communication channels" (Hobson).
  3. Good early content partnerships (Big Think, Techdirt Insight Community, recognized bloggers) gave it credibility. content_engine 3: high quality but short-lived, and none of it was member-made.
  4. Some responsiveness: a redesign driven by member input, and the decision to bring user photos and videos into the site banner (redesign post).

What they did wrong

  1. Campaign, not community. The site was built to launch the Latitude line and had no long-term plan. Millington's verdict: "Dell failed to convert this attention into a sustaining community. Where was the long term planning to keep people engaged throughout the year?" The consequence was decline within months (FeverBee).
  2. No core group. Dell recruited famous writers instead of 50–100 engaged members. The famous writers brought audiences that left with them. Comments stayed in single digits (FeverBee; Wayback samples).
  3. A broadcast architecture. It was a blog with comments, with no forum, profiles or member-to-member features. Comments needed a login for its first 9.5 months. retention_mechanics 1, member_ownership 1.
  4. A free, meaningless status symbol. The badge cost nothing and meant nothing, so few used it (FeverBee, Sep 2008).
  5. Single point of failure. One part-time owner ran it. When he left during a recession-era social team contraction, it was "abandoned soon after he left Dell" (uber.la snippet). staff_investment 2.
  6. No shutdown or handover. The site sat frozen for over two years with no goodbye post, archive or redirect. Dell then let the domain go, so the content and any SEO value were lost. Its sibling ReGeneration.org was at least folded into Direct2Dell (Dell blog, Sep 2008). durability 1.
  7. Promises not kept. The whitepaper's "Chapter One of a long book we will write together" never got a Chapter Two.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Build around the identity your users want, not your product. "Digital nomad" was an aspirational label people wanted to wear. Pick a label your users would put in their own bio.
  2. Use a launch moment to seed, then hand the stage to members. Put the launch energy into recruiting and personally befriending a founding cohort of 50–100 real users, not only influencers.
  3. Make status earned and scarce. A badge only works if it is approved, numbered or tied to a contribution. Millington's "badge of honour" and closed-cohort ideas are cheap to run.
  4. Feature members, not just experts. Rotating member photos in the banner was the right instinct. Go further: member profiles, "where are you working this week" threads, member spotlights and maps.
  5. Partner for credibility (Big Think, Techdirt, known bloggers), but treat partners as fuel for member conversation, not a replacement for it.

Avoid these

  1. A community owned by one part-time person with no succession plan. Assign a full-time community manager and executive sponsorship that outlasts any one person.
  2. A blog that calls itself a community. If members can't start threads, see each other, or build a history and reputation, you have a publication. Remove friction such as login-to-comment walls on day one.
  3. Ghost-towning instead of closing. If you stop, say goodbye, archive the content, redirect the domain and keep it. A frozen site that later serves a cinema chain's pages harms the brand more than a clean shutdown.
