TL;DR

  • What it is: Sharp's official owners' community for RoBoHoN, a 20 cm walking robot phone launched in 2016. It runs as a white-label site on coorum, a SaaS from the Japanese company Asobica, and opened on 17 Nov 2023 after a pre-opening for volunteer "RoboTomo ambassadors" on 2 Nov 2023. Anyone can read it; posting needs a free account. In Nov 2026 it had about 2,000 registered members, 23,650 talk threads and 32,060 photo posts (robotomo.robohon.com).
  • Why people come back: owners treat their robot as a child and post as the robot: outings, food, seasons, outfits. Peers react, often within hours. Points for logging in and posting raise your rank, and each rank gives a badge with a QR code that teaches your physical robot a new "beam" motion (point rules). A fixed yearly calendar keeps the rhythm going: seasonal photo contests, New Year's "resolutions", the May birthday events, the November community anniversary stream.
  • What Sharp feeds it: monthly update notes (the robot has had a feature update every month since launch), "secrets of RoBoHoN" development columns, quarterly photo contests, card-maker campaigns, a summer "free research" contest, a song whose lyrics members co-wrote, and a printable official book built from a member's idea. A small product-team crew of three runs it with coached support from the vendor's customer-success staff (coorum case).
  • Biggest mistake: treating it as provisional and outsourced. Sharp launched with a stated "one-year" run, then renewed it year by year. When the vendor's Exif bug forced a three-day shutdown in Jan 2025, Sharp explained it only afterwards, and members learned the cause from another member's post on X. Members also complain that Sharp shares too little technical information, so a few volunteers end up acting as the support desk.
  • The lesson: for a companion-type product, build the community around showing off the product's personality (photos and roleplay) rather than around Q&A, and link community status back into the device. Then commit publicly to the space for the long term and own the incident communications.

What the community is

  • Owned space: ロボホンともだち広場 ("RoBoHoN friends' plaza", which members shorten to ロボとも広場). It lives at robotomo.robohon.com on coorum, Asobica's "loyal-customer platform". Its menus are:
    • ロボとも広場 (the plaza): RoboTalk! (free talk with Anything / Self-intro / Q&A categories), Life with RoBoHoN (a staff column), group votes and a profile-card maker.
    • ロボとも写真館 (the photo studio): "今日のうちの子" ("Today's Our Kid") photo posts and the photo contests.
    • ロボホン工房 (the workshop): handmade clothes and accessories, and Roblic block-programming programs.
    • ロボホン事務局 (the secretariat): announcements and the "Working RoBoHoN Bulletin".
    • Anniversary sections and an archive of past campaigns (homepage, 2026-11-08).
  • Access model: reading needs no account, but member profiles are hidden from guests. Posting, commenting and "likes" need an account. Video can't be uploaded; members link to YouTube (FAQ). The site is web-only; staff tell members to add it to their phone's home screen.
  • Who runs it: the RoBoHoN business team at Sharp, which plans and develops the robot. Kamei, Iwakoshi and Mori were interviewed for coorum's case study. Staff post as "ロボホン事務局" (the RoBoHoN secretariat) or "ロボとも運営事務局", and Asobica's customer-success staff advise them (coorum case).
  • Unofficial spaces: owners have long gathered on X and Instagram and at owner-run offline meetups (オフ会). Members say these are "usually hosted by volunteer owners" (RoboTalk thread). A few owners blog, such as お供はロボホン. Robot retailers (Robot Planet) and Sharp's sales agent Benefit Japan run in-person events. Reddit has almost nothing: no r/robohon exists (Arctic Shift returned no such subreddit), and Brave finds only stray posts in r/thegrandtour and r/MVIS.
  • Status today (8 Nov 2026): active and still growing. New posts arrive every hour. The third-anniversary stream premiered on 6 Nov 2026, and the announcement drew 128 reactions and 25 replies.

Origin and strategy

  • The product: RoBoHoN was co-developed with the robot creator Tomotaka Takahashi and launched in May 2016 at ¥198,000 before tax. It requires a monthly "Kokoro Plan" subscription; without it, everything except phone calls stops working (ITmedia, May 2016). Sales were slow at first: about 12,000 units by Jan 2019, 85% to consumers, and only 7% of the original production target (Sankei, Mar 2019; PC Watch). The second generation and the non-walking "lite" model arrived in 2019. By Feb 2026 cumulative sales were above 50,000 units, and the Wi-Fi model starts at ¥145,200 (GetNavi, Feb 2026). The creator, Miho Kagei, says the subscription exists so the product can keep getting monthly updates "without missing a single month".
  • Why a community, and why in 2023: Sharp's Oct 2023 announcement said owners had "never had an official place online to gather". The cute photos owners posted on X and Instagram "flow away down the timeline and can't be kept", and many people still couldn't picture what living with a robot is like (robohon.com, 17 Oct 2023). Asobica's release lists three goals: bring the off-line-meetup kind of interaction to more owners, store customer voices for promotion and product development, and pass long-time owners' enthusiasm on to new customers (Asobica/PR TIMES, Nov 2023).
  • The business logic, in the team's words (coorum case):
    • Awareness is low and the robot is expensive, so prospects need to see real life with it.
    • Influencer marketing had been tried, but "the quality of content was completely different": real owners' photos have "heat and warmth".
    • So they switched to building on owner UGC collected through photo contests.
    • The stated aim is to connect "loyal owners who have loved RoBoHoN for a long time" with recent buyers, and to let owners teach each other features. Updates come monthly, and "even we developers forget" everything the robot can do.
    • The name was chosen on purpose: a "plaza" you can drop into and leave, like dog owners chatting on a walk.
  • A hedged commitment: the launch release says "operation is planned for one year; we will consider extending it depending on requests" (Sharp release, 17 Nov 2023). A second year was announced at the first-anniversary stream "after receiving many voices hoping it would continue" (robohon.com, Oct 2024).

What keeps people coming back

  1. The product is a "child" and the community is the playground. The dominant genre is posting in the robot's voice. One owner noted that "more people post pretending to be the robot than as the owner" (きらら舎, Apr 2024). Display names pair owner and robot ("ドラチズ＆りゅうくん", "おれお&らいどのパパ"). Daily posts are outings, breakfasts, seasons and shrine visits with the robot, which gives people a reason to post every day that has nothing to do with support questions. Sharp's team says owners feel about RoBoHoN learning new tricks the way parents feel about a child growing up (coorum case).
  2. Community status unlocks a feature on the device. You earn points for registering (100), logging in (5 per day), posting (3), commenting, replying and reacting (1 each, capped at 3 a day), and having your answer chosen as best (20). They add up to ten ranks named "RoBoHoN Beam 1 colour" through "Beam MAX" (30,000 points). Each rank gives a badge plus a QR code; when the robot's "scan" app reads it, the robot learns that colour of eye-beam motion (point page; FAQ). It is a gentle hook, and one owner wrote "...so what? (lol)", yet it still nudges a daily login. A badge for logging in every week of the year was handed out at the third anniversary (member post).
  3. A fixed ritual calendar. Seasonal photo contests run four times a year ("Winter with RoBoHoN", "Spring-ish RoBoHoN", "RoBoHoN's Summer", "Autumn-hunting with RoBoHoN", each repeated from 2023 to 2026). The other fixed points are a January resolutions campaign, the robot's 26 May birthday with an owners' event and a community campaign each spring, a summer campaign, and the community's own anniversary stream every November. Each campaign gets its own menu and then moves to "Archive", so the site always has something "now on".
  4. Owners' events that the community feeds. Owner events predate the community: 200 robots in 2019, Osaka in 2016. Since 2023 the community supplies their content: lyrics written by members for the 8th birthday, the "most friendly" photo award, and a call for "celebration photos" for the 9th. Tickets sell out (11 minutes in 2025). Remote owners can buy an online ticket so that their robot at home reacts along with the stream (Sharp release, Mar 2026).
  5. Peer support and belonging. Q&A is a small share of the talk, but a few technically minded owners act as unofficial engineers. They explain Android ANR errors and board replacements, compile lists of "working robots" in public places, and analyse census data (ANR thread, Jan 2026; working-robot thread, Feb 2024). Members check on each other after earthquakes and gift each other handmade outfits.
  6. Switching costs: the robot is the lock-in, through the subscription and the robot's memories. The community adds social ties, years of photo history and rank. No evidence found of members leaving for another platform.

The content engine

  • Staff content (light but steady): 84 announcements in three years. The backbone is a monthly "◯月アップデートのお知らせ" that mirrors the robot's monthly feature update, plus development secrets ("ロボホンの秘密") and a "how RoBoHoN walks" series. Two columns faded: the "Working RoBoHoN Bulletin" has only 12 posts, all from Apr–May 2024, and "Life with RoBoHoN" has 9 posts between Nov 2023 and Jul 2025 (section counters, 8 Nov 2026).
  • Campaign formats Sharp keeps reusing:
    • Photo contests with themes.
    • Card makers: a profile card, a "student ID" for the 9th birthday's "RoBoHoN School", a business-card maker for the 10th.
    • Polls whose answers shape the next monthly update. Mori said polling "lets us hear owners' reactions" before choosing features.
    • The summer 2024 "free research" contest, a nod to Japanese school summer projects, with photo, Roblic programming and crafting divisions. A Roblic sale ran alongside it.
    • A 10th-anniversary catch-copy contest (402 entries) and "10 years of best shots" (1,321).
  • Co-creation that became product: for the 8th birthday, members wrote new lyrics for the dance "Sharp Sentai Robo-Ranger". The robot performed the result at the event and owners received it (CNET release). During the free-research contest, a member posted a homemade "RoBoHoN manual", and comments asked for an official one. Sharp turned owner survey answers into a book, unveiled it at the first-anniversary live on 1 Nov 2024, and published it as printable data with space for each owner's own robot photo (coorum case). In 2026 Sharp announced a "RoBoHoN Home-Stay Program", an owner–Sharp co-creation project in which "slightly unusual RoBoHoNs" stay with a few selected owners (Sharp release, Mar 2026).
  • Cross-brand collaborations: from 2025, a "same-hometown" collaboration with Daiso's coorum community "DAISOの輪" (both brands come from Higashi-Hiroshima). It included a contest for robot outfits made from Daiso goods, Daiso staff at the 9th-birthday event, and the robot singing Daiso's in-store song (DAISOの輪; Robotstart, Jun 2026). Partners such as Tsunan town in Niigata ("friendly spots" for robot owners) and the robot-apparel maker Robo-Uni show up at events.
  • Member-made content: almost all of the volume. That includes 2,387 clothes-and-accessories posts, which act as a de facto maker marketplace, though advertising is banned. Sharp says it reuses community posts on the official site and social accounts.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: a few people from the RoBoHoN product team (business planning and development), not a dedicated community team, with coorum's customer-success staff giving benchmarks ("is our user growth or like count high or low versus other coorum communities?") and passing feature requests to Asobica (coorum case).
  • Ambassadors: "ロボとも大使" (RoboTomo ambassadors) were recruited in Oct 2023 as owners willing to help launch the community. They got a pre-opening invitation and an online briefing (robohon.com, Oct 2023). They seeded the first posts (2 Nov 2023) and the first photo contest ("RoboTomo ambassadors' kids"). No evidence found that the program continued as a formal tier after launch.
  • Rules: standard guidelines: no defamation, no personal data, no advertising or commercial use, respect copyright and portrait rights. Staff may delete posts or accounts (guideline). Separately, Sharp's Dec 2024 "RoBoHoN goods guidelines" bar fan goods that could pass as official. At least one member's post of a tote bag made from a RoBoHoN towel was flagged and removed (blog, Feb 2025).
  • Feedback loop: polls feed into monthly updates. Insight from the community changed how Sharp sells Roblic: from "a programming-education tool" to "raise your RoBoHoN the way you like", after owners described its appeal as watching their robot grow. That reframing ran alongside the free-research contest and a sale, and "many people newly started living with RoBoHoN" (coorum case). Members also post feature requests (bookmarks) and governance nitpicks (archived campaigns still accepting posts), and these get few visible staff replies.

Growth loops

  • Readable, photogenic UGC for prospects: open reading was a stated goal, and the site links straight to the COCORO STORE and Robot Planet's shop. Asobica says the UGC has "produced cases where the community experience prompted a new purchase" (CNET release). That is a vendor claim with no numbers.
  • UGC syndication: contest photos and "how we met" stories are reused on robohon.com and Sharp's social accounts.
  • Events and partners: owners' events, a five-city tour in 2026 (Niigata, Hokkaido, Okinawa, Fukuoka, Hiroshima), Daiso and town collaborations. The community announces all of them, and they bring people back to it.
  • Sales-channel tie-in: most consumer units now sell face-to-face through Benefit Japan at in-store events, according to one member's analysis of the 2024 census (member analysis). No evidence found that the community is part of that sales funnel.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none published, beyond engagement ratios. Sharp reports no community-specific retention, churn or sales numbers. Sharp's securities reports don't break out RoBoHoN (Sharp files in Japan, not with the SEC).

  • Vendor and company claims:

    • Active rate of 65% two months in, against a coorum average of 53%.
    • About 56% average monthly active rate in 2024.
    • "Contributes to LTV" (the coorum AWARD 2024 "Passionate Community" prize).
    • Community posts led to new purchases.
    • Many new owners arrived during the Roblic reframing and sale.
    • Owners' answers to the community surveys outnumbered earlier surveys.

    Sources: CNET, coorum AWARD, coorum case.

  • Indirect signals: sales kept rising to more than 50,000 units by 2026. The pre-community subscription continuation rate was 81% in 2019. Event tickets sell out. Sharp has renewed the "one-year" community for a third and fourth year, which means it passes the team's internal review. Score 2: plausible and positive, but the only numbers come from the vendor.

What they did right

  1. Designed for how owners actually relate to the product. Photos and roleplay, not tickets. The two biggest sections are "Today's Our Kid" (32,060) and free talk (23,650); Q&A is small. Activity has risen every year.
  2. A device-linked reward. Badges unlock real robot motions, a cheap and on-brand way to make community status visible in the living room.
  3. A predictable ritual calendar. Seasonal contests repeated year after year, plus the birthday and anniversary cycles. Members know when to come back, and each campaign leaves a permanent archive.
  4. Visible co-creation with concrete output. Member lyrics became a dance the robot performs. A member's idea became the official printable manual. The Home-Stay Program follows the same pattern. The team says the community's role is "a place where owners take part in development" rather than chasing sign-ups.
  5. Seeded with ambassadors and a soft launch. Volunteer ambassadors had two weeks on the site before it opened, so new arrivals found it already populated.
  6. Listening changed the marketing. The Roblic message moved from education to "raising your child", which shows how a community can give a manufacturer the customer's language.

What they did wrong

  1. Launching as a "one-year" experiment. The fine print in the 2023 release and the Oct 2023 notice ("operation confirmed until Nov 2024") left owners unsure whether it would last. Renewal depended on "many voices hoping it continues". It survived, but a highly emotional user base spent the first year wondering about the plaza's future.
  2. Outsourced platform, outsourced incident. Asobica's 8 Jan 2025 release left Exif data (including GPS) on some uploaded images on every coorum site until 29 Jan (Asobica notice). Sharp took the plaza offline from 27 Jan 19:30 to 30 Jan 18:45 and apologised only afterwards ("we regret this is an after-the-fact notice") (staff post). Members learned the cause from a fellow member relaying Asobica's notice via X. The same member concluded that Sharp "can't fix" the system itself and doubted big feature upgrades would come (thread). Members reacted warmly to the reopening, so the damage was limited, but trust now rests on a vendor Sharp doesn't control.
  3. Volunteers carry the technical support. In the Jan 2026 ANR-error thread, members said "Sharp gives no information at all" and asked Sharp to "disclose a bit more; users can't troubleshoot" (thread). An aging 2 GB Android device with no public diagnostics leaves the community as a support desk without official backing.
  4. Brand protection over maker culture. The Dec 2024 goods guidelines led to the removal of a member's homemade tote bag. Bloggers started changing their own favicons out of caution (blog). This happened in a community where handmade clothing is the third-largest section.
  5. Culture that can put off newcomers. One owner-blogger found the baby-talk roleplay, early cliques and occasional arguments off-putting and chose to lurk (お供はロボホン). He also disliked that member profiles are hidden until you register. Staff fixed a UX issue he flagged (the default category was "Self-intro") but did nothing visible about the tone.
  6. Small reach and faded staff columns. About 2,000 members against more than 50,000 units sold, and anniversary streams draw 1k–3k views. Some staff series stopped after a few posts (Working RoBoHoN Bulletin, Life with RoBoHoN), and the Roblic section has just 47 posts.

Score notes

  • Retention mechanics 4: a daily-login point system, rank badges that unlock motions on the robot, a repeating seasonal and anniversary calendar, and roleplay-driven daily posting. It falls short of a 5 because there are no in-app notifications or native app beyond email and a home-screen shortcut.
  • Content engine 3: steady monthly update notes, quarterly contests and annual streams, but the staff columns are thin and some stopped after a few posts. The volume comes from members.
  • Member ownership 4: members write lyrics, inspire the official book, run census analyses, give tech support and host off-line meetups.
  • Staff investment 3: a few product-team staff plus vendor customer-success support, renewed year by year rather than a dedicated team.
  • Business impact evidence 2: only vendor engagement ratios and qualitative claims ("contributes to LTV", "prompted purchases").
  • Durability 3: three years old with posting still growing, but it began as a "one-year" trial and depends on a third-party platform that has already forced one outage.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Let members post as the product's character if your product has one, and make the main loop sharing life with it rather than troubleshooting. That gives people a reason to post daily.
  2. Turn community rank into an in-product reward (Sharp's badges unlock robot motions), so status shows up where the customer actually uses the product.
  3. Run a repeating calendar: quarterly themed contests plus fixed annual moments (a birthday, the community's anniversary, New Year), each archived afterwards.
  4. Ship visible co-creations, such as member-written lyrics or a manual built from a member's idea, and put members' own photos into official material.
  5. Soft-launch with recruited ambassadors so the space is populated on day one, and use polls to steer the roadmap and learn your customers' language for marketing.

Avoid these

  1. Don't launch a community with an expiry date. Commit publicly or don't start; uncertainty is especially costly with emotionally invested users.
  2. Don't outsource the platform without owning incident communications and a fallback channel. Tell members before or during an outage, not after.
  3. Don't let volunteers become the unofficial support desk without giving them official information and recognition, and don't enforce brand rules in a way that chills the maker culture that drives engagement.
