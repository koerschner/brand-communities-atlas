TL;DR

  • What it is. NatWest (then part of the RBS Group) ran two public customer spaces in a row. The first was the Ideas Bank (January 2012), an open comment wall under its "Helpful Banking" Customer Charter. The second was NatWest Communities, a Lithium forum with its own Ideas Bank (autumn 2013 to 22 December 2016). RBS ran a smaller copy of each. NatWest's closure page says 28,000 people joined and wrote 16,000 posts and 700 ideas [2]. That confirms the size in the "about 30k customers" rumour [1]. The rumour's claim that negativity caused the closure is plausible but unproven: NatWest gave no reason.
  • Why people came back. Almost all of them came back about their own problem: a blocked card, a payment that never arrived, an app gap. A small core of regulars (RG, John-Jay, IB3) came back to push ideas and hold staff to account. A rank and kudos ladder existed, but there was no belonging in the hobbyist sense that a fintech forum has.
  • What the company did to feed it. It hired three to five named community managers, promised a staff reply to any post that had no member answer within 24 hours, put status labels on every idea ("Coming Soon", "Sorry, we can't do this right now"), posted "You said… we listened!" threads when an idea shipped, ran expert Q&A sessions and kept a blog of fraud and budget tips [3][17][18][19].
  • Biggest mistake. It opened a public wall without planning for a crisis or a backlog. The 2012 Ideas Bank was live during the June 2012 RBS/NatWest IT meltdown and became a national news story of customers who couldn't buy food [10][11][12]. Later, the forum's ideas board recorded slow delivery: a payee-reference request ran for three years [20], and an Android Pay request drew 248 angry comments [22]. Staff could pass ideas on but couldn't commit the product teams.
  • The lesson. An ideas portal is a promise. If you can't change the product at the pace the board demands, members turn it into a public record of what you haven't done. Give the community team real authority over the roadmap, or don't open the board.

Origin and strategy

The Customer Charter (2010). After the state rescue in 2008, the RBS Group relaunched NatWest and RBS around "Helpful Banking" and published a 14-point Customer Charter. Point 14 was "We will actively seek your thoughts and suggestions on how we can become more helpful… a new Customer Listening Programme" [9]. The 2012 Ideas Bank sat inside the Charter section of natwest.com, next to the annual Charter "Results" [5].

Ideas Bank v1 (2012). Its owner was Chris Popple, Managing Director, Digital Channels. In April 2012 he posted a note thanking customers for "taking the time to post a suggestion on our new Ideas Bank". It listed the ideas being worked on: more mobile apps, 24/7 web chat, interest rates on statements, references on payments, and spending graphs [5]. The house rules show the design: 260 characters per post, software screening before publication, moderation after it, and no complaints ("follow the complaints procedure"). Members granted NatWest "sole ownership" of any ideas, with no compensation [7]. In practice it was a suggestion box with public voting. It was never designed for conversation.

NatWest Communities (2013). In the autumn of 2013 NatWest moved to Lithium, the main enterprise community platform of the time. The forum had three starter boards and a welcome from "Fergus your community manager" [3, Nov 2013]. It was framed as peer support: "a place for NatWest customers to share experiences, knowledge and help each other". It sat in the Support Centre beside FAQs and web chat, so its job was mainly support deflection plus feedback [3][16]. The Ideas Bank moved inside it as a Lithium idea exchange [18].

Origin of the rumour. On the Monzo forum, on the day in September 2018 that Starling closed its own forum, a member repeated a colleague's account. NatWest had "launched a consumer forum… then they closed them down after something like 30k customers joined it, because of the negativity" [1]. The size checks out: NatWest's own figure is 28,000 members [2]. The cause doesn't: no source found states why NatWest closed it (see "What they did wrong").

Trajectory: slow organic growth over about three years, with posting peaking in 2015-16. Readers far outnumbered posters: thousands of guests against a handful of logged-in members at any time, which shows that most traffic came from search. For scale, NatWest Group had about 19 million customers [UK Finance profile, see 31], so 28,000 members is about 0.15%. Wayback captures of forum URLs ran at 300 to 660 a month through 2016 and then fell to 52 in November and 10 in December [30]. That points to a live site until the end, not one that had already died.

What keeps people coming back

  • Your own problem, answered (the main driver). The busiest boards were Online & Mobile Banking (5,725 posts by Dec 2016), Current Accounts (3,104) and Ways to Bank (2,381) [3]. People came with a card, login or payment problem. The guarantee was: "if a message hasn't had a response by another member within 24 hours, the Community Team will spring into action!" [3, 2015-16]. That is a support queue with peer answers on top, and most visits ended when the problem was solved.
  • Voting and status on ideas. Ideas earned kudos, and staff gave each one a visible status (New Idea, Under Review, We're looking into this but need more time, Coming Soon, Accepted, Duplicate, Sorry we can't do this right now) with a named reply [18]. Members could watch "their" idea move. This was the closest thing to a ritual, and also the main source of frustration (below).
  • Closing the loop. "You said… we listened!" posts (for example payee references, January 2015) quoted members' Ideas Bank comments back to them and announced the feature [19]. Monthly online-banking release notes ("Online Banking – July Update") invited comment and drew 44 replies and 30,000 views [20].
  • Status ladder. The standard Lithium ranks (New Member, Getting Started, Beginner, Fledgling, Part Timer, Regular, Emerging Talent), "Top Kudoed Authors" on the homepage, and accepted "Latest Solved Topics" [3][20][21]. The same names topped the kudos list for two years (RG, John-Jay, IB3) [3], a sign of a small core of superusers. No evidence was found of a formal superuser or champion programme, perks or meetups.
  • Ties to staff. Members named community managers in their posts ("@SallyE and @MichelleK do as much as they can") [21]. Regulars negotiated service levels directly with staff (see Member voice). This was the strongest belonging mechanism, and it was tied to people who could not deliver product change.
  • Switching costs. None. A bank forum creates no lock-in, and the Android Pay thread shows members openly using the forum to announce they were moving banks [22].

Score, retention mechanics: 2/5. It had the standard Lithium gamification and idea statuses, but no rituals, cohorts, events or identity beyond a few regulars.

The content engine

  • Community blog (from about 2014): fraud and scam alerts ("Phishing Scams Rise by 20%", "Tips for Cash Machine Safety"), seasonal money pieces (freshers, Christmas shopping, "Budget 2016: what it means for you"), member savings stories ("My Saver Story") and mobile release notes [24]. The cadence looks to have been a few posts a month.
  • Expert Q&A sessions (2014): timed sessions with specialists, such as "ISAs – what is the new ISA allowance all about?" (12 Aug) and "Phishing – how can you stay safe online?" (20 Aug). One topic was a sensitive product change: "0% Card Tricks – why have we removed teaser rates?" [17].
  • Product release notes and consultations: online-banking monthly updates, "We need you! What do you think about natwest.com" and calls for feedback on how credits appear in accounts [3][20].
  • Member-made content: nothing beyond answers and ideas. There were no templates, guides or wikis, which is unsurprising for a bank, where the "product" can't be extended by users.

Score, content engine: 2/5. The content was competent but generic consumer finance; nothing made the forum a destination.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. Named community managers appear in the archive: Fergus (2013), SallyE (registered January 2014; 4,207 posts by May 2015), MichelleW (January 2014; 1,668 posts), MichelleK, KellyY and RH_NW, plus ImranRBS on the RBS forum [3][17][19][21][25]. That suggests a team of about three to five at any time. Chris Popple (MD, Digital Channels) sponsored the 2012 Ideas Bank [5]; no executive sponsor is visible for the 2013-16 forum.
  • Rules. Strict for a regulated firm: no personal or confidential information, no complaints ("we can't resolve your complaints on the Community"; complaints went to a separate hub), UK residents only, and NatWest's right not to publish any post [3][7].
  • Feedback into the product. Staff passed ideas to named internal teams ("I've gone back to our Online Banking Team…", "all your comments are being passed onto the mobile team") and reported back [18][22]. Sally said that tracking down the right owner "takes a bit of time… in a big organisation" [21]. In 2015 the regulars pushed for, and Sally accepted, a policy of a NatWest reply to every unanswered post, a status on every idea, and monthly updates on ideas with more than five kudos [21].
  • Superusers. No formal programme was found.

Score, staff investment: 3/5. The forum had a dedicated, visible, responsive team with a published SLA, but no evidence of authority over the roadmap or of a budget for events and programmes. Score, member ownership: 2/5. A few regulars shaped its norms, but members owned nothing.

Growth loops

  • Search. This was the real loop. With 1,600 to 2,600 guests online against 2 or 3 members [3], most visitors arrived from Google on a specific question. Members noticed that Googling "Android Pay NatWest" returned the forum's own pages, and that NatWest then removed the official pages [22].
  • Support-centre placement. The forum was linked as "Join in the conversation" and "Lightbulb moments" from the Support Centre [16], so it recruited people who already had a problem.
  • Social and staff referrals. The @NatWest_Help Twitter team sent people to the Ideas Bank (one blogger was told to post his hold-music complaint there) [13].
  • No acquisition loop. No evidence that the forum brought in new customers. Its public archive of unhappy threads probably worked against acquisition.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none found. No deflection rate, NPS change, retention figure or cost saving was published.
  • Company claims. (a) The April 2012 Popple note credits customer ideas for 24/7 web chat, interest rates on statements and work on payee references and spending graphs [5]. (b) "You said… we listened!" credits the Ideas Bank for editable payee references, live in January 2015 [19]. (c) An agency case study for RBS (SapientRazorfish, Campaign, January 2017) says that the decision to roll out Touch ID was "driven in part by customer comments on RBS' Ideas Bank, the bank's online community forum" [26]. That is an agency claim and not independently verified.
  • Counter-evidence. In July 2015, six months after "we listened", members were still asking for the same payee-reference change: "requested for the last 3 years… zero out of 100 for taking notice" [20]. Either the shipped feature didn't match the request or members didn't find it.

Score, business impact evidence: 1/5. There are only anecdotes of ideas shipping and one agency claim.

What they did right

  1. Clear separation of jobs. Every page said what the forum was not for (complaints, urgent account issues) and linked to the right channel [3][7]. That is the right pattern for a regulated firm.
  2. A published service level. The 24-hour staff fallback meant that no question went unanswered, and regulars praised the improved "response policy on frequency and out of hours cover" [3][21].
  3. Visible statuses on ideas, written by named people. Even "Sorry, we can't do this right now… due to system constraints" is better than silence, and members could see their ideas move [18].
  4. Closing the loop in public. "You said… we listened!" posts quoted members' own words back to them [19]. This is the best practice, and the forum used it.
  5. Asking the power users how to run it. Sally's "If there were no limitations what would you want to see?" produced a concrete governance charter (a reply to every post, a status on every idea, monthly progress on popular ideas), and it was adopted [21].
  6. A closure page with real numbers. Rather than letting the site disappear, NatWest published a short thank-you page with lifetime statistics and where to go next [2]. Many companies don't.

What they did wrong

  1. Opening an anonymous, unthreaded public wall with no crisis plan (2012). When RBS Group's batch-processing failure froze accounts in June 2012, the Ideas Bank on NatWest's own website became the outlet for national anger. ITV, the Telegraph and the Guardian quoted it directly ("I'm disabled and in wheel chair… we have no food left") [10][11][12]. The bank's only answer on the page was a pasted apology from CEO Stephen Hester [5, 25 Jun 2012 capture]. Consequence: the open feed was gone by January 2013 and replaced by a more controlled "new Ideas Bank" [8].
  2. An ideas board the company couldn't serve. The product teams moved at the speed of a big, heavily regulated bank. A three-year payee-reference request [20], a direct-debit visibility idea refused "due to system constraints" [18], and Android Pay missing at its UK launch in May 2016 while Nationwide, HSBC and Lloyds supported it [22] all left a permanent public record of "not yet". Members said so: "how can you defend the indefensible… to Nat West we really dont matter" [21]. Consequence: the forum's most active threads were about switching banks.
  3. Community staff with no authority. The managers could only "pass it on" and "keep you posted" [18][22]. A member reported getting the Android Pay date from the Chief Executive's Office complaints team, not from the forum [22]. That told everyone that the forum was not where decisions were made.
  4. Too many relaunches and splits. In four years: v1 Ideas Bank (2012), v2 Ideas Bank (2013), a Lithium forum with a third Ideas Bank (2013), separate RBS instances, a 2014-15 restructure of the boards [3][8][25]. Each move reset the members' history.
  5. Quiet closure with no reason given. A banner went up in mid-December 2016 and the forum closed on 22 December 2016, just before Christmas. Content was not kept read-only, and 16,000 posts of peer answers, the search asset, were lost [2][3][4]. The "Closing the Community" thread (458 posts on its board by then) was not archived, so members' reactions are lost too [3]. Why it closed is unknown. The closure page gives no reason [2]. The timing fits NatWest's move to automated help: its Cora chatbot launched in 2017 and answered "more than 200 basic banking queries" by early 2018 [28]. The negativity claim in the rumour [1] fits the tone of the archive but has no source.

Score, durability: 1/5. The forum lasted about three years and closed with no successor public space.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Publish a reply guarantee. "If no member answers in 24 hours, staff will" keeps a peer-support forum credible while it is too small to answer itself [3].
  2. Give every idea a status and a named reply, including honest "no" answers with the reason [18].
  3. Close the loop in public, using members' own words, when an idea ships ("You said… we listened!") [19].
  4. Let your superusers write the service charter. Ask them "if there were no limitations, what would you want?" and adopt the answers [21].
  5. Say on every page what the space is not for and route those cases (complaints, account-specific issues) elsewhere with one click [3][7].

Avoid these

  1. Don't open an ideas board faster than you can ship. Every unshipped popular idea becomes a permanent public grievance and a thread about switching to a competitor [20][22]. Match the board's scope to what your product team will actually commit to, and give the community lead a seat in roadmap decisions.
  2. Don't run an open, anonymous wall on your homepage without a crisis plan. When something breaks, it becomes the press's quote source [10][11][12]. Have a pinned incident thread, staffing, and a way to switch the space to incident mode.
  3. Don't relaunch repeatedly or close quietly. Four platforms in four years, then a pre-Christmas shutdown that deleted all the content, threw away the community's history and its value in search [2][3][8].
