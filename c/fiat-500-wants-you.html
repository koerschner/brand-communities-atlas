TL;DR

  • What it is: fiat500.com's "500 wants you", launched 3 May 2006, exactly 500 days before the new Fiat 500 was due. Fiat and its agencies (ARC Worldwide / Leo Burnett Torino) invited the public to "co-design" the car through configurators, contests and votes. By May 2008 it had 120,000 registered members from 206 countries and about 9 million unique visitors [1][2][3].
  • Why people came back: something new arrived every one to three months (13 site releases carrying 28 activities). Each release was a contest or toy aimed at a different niche: designers, ad creatives, musicians, tattoo artists, expectant mothers, spy photographers. Members also got rewards: the first official images, and the right to book one of 500 "500 wants you edition" cars [2][4][5][6].
  • What the company fed it: an agency team of about 25 named people, plus Fiat's head of design, Roberto Giolito, who treated the data as "rough diamonds". A real-time back end showed members' choices to Centro Stile and the product managers, and some of those choices (white, black and red paint, chrome trim, the Italian-flag stripe, a pearl-white launch colour, a glass roof) shipped on the car [2][7].
  • Biggest mistake: it was built as a campaign with an end date. Once the car launched, the owner spin-off "500 loves you" (April 2008) was gated by VIN and run on bespoke ASP pages. Owners drifted to fan forums and Facebook: the noticeboard's last post is dated November 2010 and the whole thing was later switched off. The community's showpiece, the 500 "community edition" cars, also arrived in autumn 2007 with the wrong badges, which caused a small but public revolt [8][9][10].
  • The one lesson: co-creation can make a launch: 57,000 orders in three weeks and the 2008 CMO of the Year award for Luca De Meo [11][12]. But the community it gathers will leave once the "design" job is done unless you hand it a durable owner role and a home it controls.

What the community is

"500 wants you" was an interactive, multilingual microsite at www.fiat500.com, also reachable at 500wantsyou.com [13]. It ran from 3 May 2006 into spring 2008 and gathered a registered "community" around the unreleased car [1][2]. It was not a forum: members did not talk to each other in threads. They configured the car, entered contests, voted on each other's entries, uploaded media, and received previews and privileges. Its spaces were:

  • Concept Lab I (May 2006) and II (Dec 2006): Flash configurators built on two photos of the 2004 Trepiùno concept. Users chose paint, wheels, graphics and accessories, including deliberately fake ones such as a safe, a fridge and a food warmer, used to test what people actually wanted [7].
  • Contests and toys: a Designboom design competition (€5,000 for each of three categories), a homepage redesign kit for web designers, "500logia" (a crowd-written encyclopedia of the old 500), a mascot contest (a €3,000 gold ingot), the Jingle Machine, "Speak 500" (how "500" is pronounced in more than 50 languages), the Sticker Art Contest, the Baby Boom contest, the "500 wants youR ADV" ad contest, the "Hai visto 500?" geotagged spy-photo hunt, the "500's way" webcam e-cards and the YouTube "500 Air Drive Championship" [4][5][6][14][15][16].
  • Member privileges: a members-only world premiere of the car's images on 20 March 2007 [17]. Online booking of 500 "500 wants you edition" cars opened on 8 May 2007, only for registered members [5][18]. The 4 July 2007 launch show was streamed live on the site [19].
  • "500 loves you" (April 2008): an owner social network. Owners entered their car's VIN to get a personal page that worked as a blog, plus friends lists, messaging, a noticeboard for meetups, a "500 Wiki" for rating features, and "search by number plate" to find the owner of a 500 you had seen [8][9][20].
  • IRL: the 500 PicNic on 5 July 2008, the car's first birthday, held in about 13 to 15 cities with MTV. Fiat said the idea of a first owners' meeting "came from the people themselves" [8][21].

Fiat retired the platform completely. The 500 loves you pages redirect from 2012, fiat500.com was redirecting by January 2014, and 500wantsyou.com later became an unrelated insurance spam site [13][22].

Origin and strategy

Fiat was coming off the Stilo flop. Luca De Meo, then Fiat brand and commercial chief, told Reuters in May 2006: "We are trying to do everything that is contrary to what we did with the Stilo" [23]. The press release that launched the platform framed it as co-creation: "For the first time in the history of our company, and perhaps of the motor industry as a whole, a car will be created by the public and for the public!" Contributions "will be assessed and possibly used during the actual development" [1].

According to its digital creative director, Matteo Righi, the platform served three internal customers [2]:

  1. Centro Stile (design): a source of preference data, "so as not to disappoint expectations".
  2. Marketing and product managers: a way to "fascinate people" and test against competitors.
  3. Leo Burnett (positioning): a way to revive "a myth absent for 50 years" while "doing without traditional advertising in favour of the web".

Righi says the objective "was not the final sale, but the collection of suggestions about the new car" [4]. In practice it was the launch's demand engine. Champions: De Meo (credited by the press for the "unconventional" tools [12][24]), Giovanni Perosino (head of communication), the internet managers Stefano Stravato and Luis Cilimingras, and Roberto Giolito, head of Centro Stile, who "spurred us on" [2][7].

Relationship to Fiat Mio (verify flag): the coordinator's note calls this the "direct predecessor" of Fiat Mio. Both are Fiat co-creation projects, and 500 wants you came first (2006 vs. 2009). But Mio was run by Fiat Brazil with a different agency (AgênciaClick Isobar). I found no source that links the two teams or names 500 wants you as Mio's model. "Precursor within Fiat" is supported; "direct predecessor" is not.

Trajectory: steep growth tied to the launch countdown, then a peak in registrations in May 2008 (120,000). After that, activity fell away, and the owner layer was close to silent by 2009. Some outlets also cite "3 million" contributors [30][31]. That figure appears to mix visitors with participants, so treat it as marketing.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A ritual of releases and countdown peaks. The team shipped a new release every one to three months as a deliberate "communication peak". Release dates followed the launch narrative: 500 days, 365 days (Sep 2006), "nine months" (Dec 2006), the premiere (Mar 2007), the platform's first birthday (May 2007), the countdown (Jun 2007), launch day (4 Jul 2007) [4][6][32]. "None of the initiatives was ever removed," so the site "grew together with its users" [4].
  2. Long-tail niche targeting. Righi says they "adopted Chris Anderson's Long Tail theory": each release targeted a different passion group (cooks, decorators, tattoo artists, graffiti writers, illustrators, tuners, DJs, filmmakers, expectant mothers, paparazzi). The aim was that "everyone was involved, not just car enthusiasts" [4].
  3. Status through competition and the crowd's vote. Many contests had a popular-jury track, so members came back to vote. The ad contest gave one prize to the professional jury's pick and one to the "500 wants you jury". Both winners got a 500 and a trip to Cannes, where they acted as the community's "correspondents" [5]. The Sticker Art finalists were chosen by users, and the winner got a gold ingot and had a design produced as a real sticker [6][33].
  4. Insider rewards. Members saw the official images before any other media "as an exclusive reward for community members only" [5][17]. Only members could reserve the 500 wants you edition [18]. Belonging bought scarce things.
  5. Visible influence. Fiat repeatedly said the car had been "created by the people". The most-picked colours and trims went into the range, and the rock genre, the most popular choice among the 15,000 user jingles, was used in the TV spots [7][28]. Seeing your input ship is the strongest reason to return to a co-creation space.
  6. Weak peer ties, and the main gap. Until 500 loves you arrived in 2008 there was no member-to-member discussion layer. The peer bonds that formed (regional meetups, owner clubs) grew on cinquecentisti.com, nuovafiat500.it and Facebook. Members advertised those spaces on Fiat's own noticeboard [9][20].

Score justification: retention mechanics 3/5. The cadence, voting and insider rewards were excellent for a fixed-length campaign, but nothing was built to sustain peer bonds or a habit after launch.

The content engine

Everything was agency-made, interactive and Flash-heavy. The output, in order:

  • Configurators as research tools. Concept Lab I/II fed a real-time preference back end used "exclusively" by the agency, Centro Stile and product managers. Concept Lab II focused on interiors, mixing real accessories with fake ones to test appetite. The 500 Video Configurator (July 2007) rendered the user's choice, out of more than 500,000 combinations, as a video [7].
  • Toys and contests for each niche (see above), each with a gallery that made member work the content.
  • Live media: the "Welcome 500" launch streamed on 4–5 July 2007, watched by more than 100,000 people online, plus "500 Wrooom" live video (Jan 2008) [4][19][34].
  • Localization into 23 languages [2], and a newsletter that fan forums abroad discussed [35].
  • Scale of investment: a credits list of about 25 named people, plus musicians and a 3D studio. One feature, the "500's way" e-card, took "51 people, 10 days" [2][16].

Score: content engine 4/5. It was highly inventive, frequent and multilingual. It loses a point because the content was campaign-bound and was deleted afterwards. Righi himself complains: "why don't companies and agencies leave online projects that are still studied in schools and universities?" [7]

People, programs and governance

  • Team: ARC Worldwide Torino and Leo Burnett Torino. Client service director Carmen Momo ran data analysis and presented it to the client; digital creative director Matteo Righi; technical director Gianluca Mori; content managers Raffaella Ramondetti, Barbara Berardi and Cristina Gilli; plus project managers, developers and copywriters [2][7].
  • Feedback loop: this was the program's strength. The back end tracked choices in real time. Giolito saw the data as "rough diamonds extracted from an immense mine", and Righi's verdict is "FIAT wasn't pretending" [7]. Spontaneous suggestions (pearl white, the Sky Dome glass roof) are said to have been adopted [7]. Academic work by Filieri & Alguezaui (2012) uses the case to study why consumers co-create, and summarises that Fiat learned preferred colours and accessories and adjusted pricing and production [36].
  • No superuser or ambassador program, and no moderators in the forum sense. Recognition came through contest wins and featured galleries.
  • Governance problem flagged by researchers: Cucco & Dalli (Economia & Management, 2008) describe the case as a balance between retro-marketing, collaborative innovation and community management. They say the last of these "can take on critical traits and probably constitutes the most delicate element of the whole project" [37].

Scores: member ownership 2/5, because Fiat and the agency set every agenda and members chose from menus. Staff investment 4/5: a large, senior agency team and the head of design engaged for two years, though no dedicated community managers stayed on after launch.

Growth loops

  • Earned media from each release. Every release came with a Fiat press release and wide coverage in the Italian and international press (Repubblica, Quattroruote, NYT, Designboom, Dezeen) [17][18][38].
  • Partner audiences: Designboom's design readership, ad schools (through the Cannes prize), MTV for the PicNic, and YouTube for Air Drive [5][15][21][26].
  • Sharing features built in: you could email your Concept Lab car to a friend, and the e-cards and playable banners spread on their own; the banners won an ADCI silver [14][16][28].
  • Scarcity: the members-only edition and the members-only premiere turned registration into a waiting list [17][18].

Evidence of business impact

Hard numbers (third-party or Fiat):

  • 57,000 orders by 26 July 2007, three weeks after launch: "practically the entire sales target estimated for 2007" [11].
  • 112,000 customers in 70 countries by May 2008; 80% chose top-trim Lounge or Sport versions, a mix consistent with the personalisation-led positioning (correlation only) [8].
  • The 500 wants you edition (500 units) sold out online in under 500 minutes [27].

Company and agency claims:

  • Booz & Company named De Meo 2008 CMO of the Year, explicitly citing "the '500 Wants You' interactive website where customers were invited to participate directly" [12].
  • 1 gold, 2 silvers and 7 nominations at the ADCI Awards, plus an Interactive Key Award [2][25]. Leo Burnett's global leaders featured the case in their book Humankind [2].

What is missing: there is no measure separating the platform's effect from the retro-icon product, the TV campaign and the 4 July spectacle. I found no retention or repurchase data, and the community's later decline suggests it did little for long-term loyalty. Business impact evidence: 3/5.

What they did right

  1. A finite job with a clear story. "Help build the car in 500 days" gave each visit a purpose and a deadline [1].
  2. The data reached real decision makers. A live back end went to the head of design, and visible adoptions (colours, chrome, flag stripe, glass roof, rock music) made the co-creation claim believable [7][28].
  3. A release cadence tied to narrative peaks (500/365/270 days, premiere, birthday, launch), so press coverage and member visits rose together [4][6][32].
  4. Niche-by-niche recruitment. Contests aimed at non-car passions (music, tattoos, cooking, parenting) widened the funnel well beyond car enthusiasts [4]. Baby Boom alone drew about 4,000 mothers [5].
  5. Scarce, honest rewards for members: the first look and the first cars [17][18].
  6. Building the owner layer early. The team planned the move from "co-create the car" to "co-create its world" (brandertainment) and then to an owner network with IRL picnics, instead of going dark at launch [8][16].

What they did wrong

  1. A campaign, not an institution. The platform was staffed and paid for as an agency campaign. After spring 2008 nothing new shipped, the tools were taken offline, and the domain was folded into Fiat's brand site. The 120,000 members had nowhere official to go [2][7][22].
  2. The community edition went wrong (Aug–Oct 2007). Buyers of the 500 wants you edition found their cars carried "Opening Edition" plates, the Cappellini special sold at the Milan furniture fair. They also heard of a €250 charge for plates, and some editions were numbered while others were not. Members wrote protest emails to Fiat and to Quattroruote, and one poster told others how to file a complaint with Italy's competition authority (AGCM) [10][39]. The community's flagship reward became a grievance.
  3. The owner network was walled in and late. 500 loves you came 9 months after launch, required a VIN, and offered blog pages and a noticeboard on bespoke ASP pages, just as Facebook groups took off. Within weeks owners were using it to point each other to the Club Nuova Fiat 500 forum ("we are over 2,000 members"), cinquecentisti.com and Facebook groups. The last post is dated 14 November 2010 [9][20][40].
  4. No member-to-member layer during the high-energy phase. Interaction ran member→brand, so the strongest bonds formed on fan sites Fiat didn't run [9][20].
  5. Archives destroyed. The configurators and galleries were taken offline. Even the agency lead complains that a much-studied case can no longer be seen [7].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give the community a job with a deadline and a story. "Help us build X before launch day" is a strong reason to return; plan your releases around the countdown.
  2. Ship visible proof that input mattered. Name the features that came from members. Credibility here drives participation more than prizes do.
  3. Run a regular drop cadence (every 4–12 weeks), each drop aimed at a different niche. It recruits new segments and gives old members a reason to come back.
  4. Reward members with scarce access, such as first looks and first units, not just merchandise.
  5. Pipe community data straight to product owners in real time. The back end shared by the agency and Centro Stile is the bit most worth copying.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let the community die with the campaign budget. Before launch, decide who owns the community afterwards, what its new purpose is, and which platform it lives on.
  2. Don't fumble the members' flagship reward. One mislabeled special edition turned your most loyal buyers into complainants on public forums.
  3. Don't build a walled owner garden late, on a weaker platform than the fan sites. Meet owners where peer bonds are already forming, or support the fan clubs instead of competing with them.
