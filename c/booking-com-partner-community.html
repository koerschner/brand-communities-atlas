How it pays off for the company

Support deflection and onboarding for millions of accommodation partners who could hardly reach human support, plus a broadcast and listening channel (announcements, AMAs, webinars, feedback threads). Booking.com never published deflection, retention or revenue numbers for it.

TL;DR

  • What it was: Booking.com's official forum for accommodation partners (hotels, B&Bs and holiday-rental hosts). It started in 2017 as the "Partner Forum" on the Zendesk help centre, moved onto the new Partner Hub around 2019, was renamed the "Global Partner Community" in August 2021 when regional spaces launched, was rebuilt as a "new Partner Community" in September 2022, and was shut down later. A "Partner Community Closure" announcement page existed, and by November 2026 every community URL returns 404 (closure page title in search index; Using the Partner Community, 2022).
  • Why people came back: need, not belonging. Partners couldn't easily reach Booking.com support, so they posted in the forum hoping another host or a moderator would help. The tag counters say it all: in one 2022–23 snapshot, "urgent" carried about 14,700 posts while "tips" carried 237 (US community snippet).
  • What Booking.com fed it: named Community Managers (Brooke, then Sergei, then Didem and Megan), a moderation team, Booking.com employees acting as "Community Ambassadors", points/levels/badges and Top Contributor lists, AMA threads with product managers, webinar announcements, Traveller Review Award threads, and links into the Click. conferences and Click. Magazine.
  • Biggest mistake: it was a peer forum bolted onto a support system that couldn't answer the forum's main question ("why is my property closed / where is my payout?"). By its own rules, moderators "can't provide specialized support" and couldn't see reservations (guidelines, 2023). Members read deleted posts and a four-day "Unanswered" tab as image management. Many quit trusting it, and the company eventually closed it, as it had closed its consumer Travel Communities in June 2023.
  • The one lesson: a business-to-business community can't make up for missing support or a strained commercial relationship. If members' money is at stake and they can't get answers, they won't treat your forum as a community. They'll treat it as a complaints desk, and the knowledgeable regulars will leave.

Unlike Airbnb, Booking.com never built a host identity. It has no Superhost-style ambassador network and no local host clubs. The forum sat inside a content portal (the Partner Hub), next to Help articles, Click. Magazine and "Solutions" upsell pages.

Origin and strategy

The company's own 2024-era announcement started "Since 2017 we've built our Partner Community with one main purpose in mind: to provide you, our partners — from every corner of [the world]…" (updates and announcements, snippet). The first Zendesk-era welcome post called it "created just for you to connect with other Booking.com partners", with "weekly updates in the Forum Announcements topics" (Just joined? thread, 2018).

The business logic was support deflection and onboarding at enormous scale. At the end of 2024 Booking.com listed about 4.0 million properties, of which about 3.5 million were homes and apartments (Booking Holdings 10-K 2024). In 2025 it gave Traveller Review Awards to 1.71 million partners (Booking.com press release, 27 Jan 2025). Most of these are small independents who struggle to reach a human. The forum's welcome copy said so directly: "If you find the Partner Help Area… not answering your questions, try either search… community results" (Sergei, Oct 2020).

It also fitted Booking.com's partner-relations push. "Growing Together" (2020) promised "to listen more, and to show you exactly how we're acting on your feedback" (Growing Together; Click. Magazine). That push came while hoteliers were fighting Booking.com over parity clauses. The EU Court of Justice ruled those clauses anti-competitive in September 2024, and more than 15,000 hotels joined a collective damages action in 2025 (HOTREC, Aug 2025; Guardian, Aug 2025). The forum was always a meeting place for parties in a commercial dispute, not for fans.

The community sat in Booking.com's "Digital Partner Experience" organisation. The community manager Megan Waters later became "Lead – Content Strategy in the Digital Partner Experience team" (LinkedIn). Another manager there described "digitally manag[ing] millions of accommodation partners via Booking.com's Partner Hub" through "scalable self service solutions" (same source). In other words, the community was one self-service channel among several. No executive sponsor was found.

Reading the numbers. Even the largest tag held only about 23,000 posts over five-plus years, against 1.7 to 4 million partners. Active participation was almost certainly well under 1% of the partner base (an estimate). Drupal node IDs in page metadata, which count all site content and not only forum posts, rose from about 60,000 (Oct 2020) to 73,000 (Aug 2021), 105,000 (Dec 2022) and 122,000 (Aug 2023). That suggests the site kept adding content at roughly the same rate through 2023, with no sign of a breakout. The ratio of "urgent" to "tips" says more than the totals: this was a help-desk queue, not a knowledge-sharing community.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Livelihood dependency and missing support (the main driver). Thread titles such as "Impossible to contact booking.com" (US, May 2022) and "It is impossible to contact Booking" (Aug 2021) recur throughout (1, 2). A partner wrote in 2023: "you have to contact the community in the hope that someone can help you" (Jasleen thread). This brought people in, but it doesn't build attachment: once the problem is solved or seen as hopeless, the member leaves.
  2. Gamification (2019 onward). Five levels earned by contributions, 8 badges (for example "Conversation Starter: write 5 posts", "Contributor: write 5 comments", "Snap: upload your profile picture", "About You: add your property bio"), "surprise notifications" on unlocks, and a weekly Top 5 Contributors list (2019 explainer; 2022 guidelines). The 2022 relaunch kept points, levels and badges and added following, @-tagging, thumbs up/down and preset hashtags (how-to). One member explained it to another: "The Badges are actually just to show other people how Active you are in the forum. You technically gain nothing through it" (What's in a badge?, 2019). There were no perks, no status on the public listing and no access to staff.
  3. Recognition rituals. Top Contributors moved from weekly (2019) to monthly lists (2023–24). Staff wrote thank-you posts that tagged regulars: "During the first half of 2023, I've seen many of you making invaluable contributions… I'd like to tag some of you to express gratitude" (snippet). Community managers also redirected new askers to named veterans ("I believe that @Yesenia Campos Valdovinos has recently shared some suggestions…", TRA 2023 thread).
  4. Seasonal hooks. Every January the Traveller Review Awards brought award-kit and "how do I add my award photo" threads. Summer-readiness posts ("New here? Here's how you can make the most of this summer", Aug 2023) gave the year a rhythm (1, 2).
  5. Email notifications for replies, likes, followed users and removals (how-to).

What was missing: no streaks, no cohorts, no host identity or name, no local meetups for small hosts (Click. targets "strategic leaders & key accounts"), no badge that showed up where guests could see it, and no switching cost. Partners could leave the forum without losing anything.

The content engine

  • AMA threads with product managers. Examples include "Ask Me Anything: Guest Reviews with Tarundeep Singh" (questions closed 21 July, top-10 answers posted 25 July, probably 2022) and "Availability with Majid Himi" (top-10 answers on 20 February 2023). The rules were strict: "Questions regarding individual claims, specific guests and reservation numbers will be removed" (reviews AMA; availability AMA). Taking ten curated questions in writing is a cautious, low-cost format compared with live sessions.
  • Webinars. An Events and webinars category with sub-sections (Webinars, Previous webinars, Workshops, Calendar of events). In 2023 there were roughly monthly free webinars, often on property-page optimisation, plus market trend sessions and financial training recordings, announced by the Community Manager (category; Aug 2023 webinar; financial training).
  • Company announcements (product updates, the "Booking.yeah" Super Bowl ad, research recruitment for 45–60-minute Zoom interviews) in a dedicated category (snippet).
  • Engagement prompts in the Zendesk era, such as an "April Photo Challenge: your most popular piece of furniture" with a winner announced (snippet).
  • Around the forum: Click. Magazine, the Partner Hub newsletter (which promised "partner community discussions"), the "Booking.com Basics" YouTube series (2024) and Click. conference videos (newsletter; Basics video, July 2024). Booking.com's education output is substantial, but it lives in Help and Click. Magazine rather than in member-made content.
  • Member content was answers to support questions plus occasional tips (welcome rituals, review management). No templates, guides or member showcases were curated or rewarded beyond Top Contributor mentions. The guidelines reserved the right to reuse member content "in our publications or marketing campaigns" (guidelines).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: Brooke was the community manager in the Zendesk era (thread). Sergei was Community Manager (2019–2021, posting in English and Italian), with Ilaria on the Italian side (1, 2). "Alex from the Partner Hub team" ran the 2021 rename. Didem and Megan were "Community Manager" in 2022–24 (community team tag), backed by "our moderation team" (See you in 2023!).
  • "Community Ambassadors" were employees, not members. For example: "I've been working at Booking.com as an Account Executive, in the Melbourne office… I'm also pleased to be a Community Ambassador here" (June 2023, thread). There was no member superuser program, no member moderators, and no perks or path for veteran hosts. The consumer-side Travel Communities, by contrast, did run volunteer "Travel Ambassadors".
  • Rules: a three-strike policy, no sharing of reservation or personal data, no bug reports (send them via the Extranet), and a warning that "you're talking to other partners like you, not Booking.com support teams". Most importantly: "Privacy laws prevent community moderators or advisors from having access to your property or reservation information. They can't provide specialized support" (guidelines, 2023). The 2017 terms already said Booking.com "may, but is not obligated to, monitor or review" posts and disclaimed accuracy (Zendesk guidelines).
  • Feedback loop: staff said they "share your feedback and suggestions with our internal teams" (TRA thread). AMAs and the Advisory Board were the only formal channels. No public idea portal or "you asked, we built" log was found.

Growth loops

There were few. The forum required an Extranet login, so it only reached existing partners and did nothing for acquisition. Its public pages did rank in search for long-tail Extranet questions ("Temporary closure of property", "Close dates"), which presumably saved some contacts with support (unmeasured). The only "referral" was onboarding: new-partner pages pointed to the community. The 2022 relaunch removed the regional and topic structure some members used ("Topic headings never relate to anything I want to ask/say. Very limited choice", Sept 2022).

Evidence of business impact

No hard evidence found. Booking Holdings' filings don't mention the Partner Community or Partner Hub. An EDGAR full-text search for "Partner Hub" in 10-Ks returns no Booking hit (EDGAR). No deflection rate, partner NPS or churn figure was published. The closure itself is the strongest signal: after roughly eight years and at least three platforms, Booking.com decided the forum wasn't worth running. That fits a wider pattern. Its consumer Travel Communities went read-only on 1 June 2023 and closed on 30 June 2023 (closure notice snippet). The 10-K stresses Gen AI investment "to benefit internal productivity and improve the consumer and partner experience" (10-K 2024), and AI-assistant support appears in partner-facing products (affiliate support example). It's plausible, though unconfirmed, that self-service and AI replaced the forum.

What they did right

  1. Staffed it with named people. Community Managers wrote welcomes, holiday sign-offs and redirects, and tagged experienced members into threads. That was the forum's warmest feature (See you in 2023!).
  2. Put product managers in front of partners. The AMAs with reviews and availability PMs addressed the topics members cared about most, reviews above all.
  3. Recognised helpers publicly. Top Contributor lists and thank-you posts that tagged names were cheap and fitted a peer-help forum.
  4. Localised a little. The Italian community (Italy has the most Booking.com award winners: 207,730 in 2025) and the US regional space showed awareness that hosts want peers in their own market and language (press release).
  5. Wrote clear guidelines and explained moderation (three strikes, email on removal), better than many vendor forums.

What they did wrong

  1. Built a peer forum where the members needed support. The commonest posts were account closures, payouts, invoices and verification, which peers can't fix and moderators were barred from looking up. Result: unanswered "urgent" threads and anger aimed at the forum. "If questions are not answered by the community then the booking.com help team should answer them… As booking.com is also a 'partner' then they should also actively get involved" (Sept 2022).
  2. Created a censorship perception from day one. "You are not permitted to discuss booking.com in any way other than positive" (Zendesk era), and later "some of my posts just get deleted and i get no communication as to why" (2022) (1, 2). Members compared it with LateRooms' forum, which "fade[d] away… for the very same reason".
  3. Treated feedback as a dead end. Members asked repeatedly about systemic problems, such as calendar sync causing double bookings ("dozens and dozens of reports in this community"), and got community-team replies with no product follow-through (Aug 2021).
  4. Re-platformed it about three times in five years (Zendesk to Partner Hub around 2019, rename in 2021, rebuild in 2022). Each move reset habits and brought login problems ("it says I am invalid… like the rest of Booking.com, it's just not up to the standards it should be", 2022).
  5. Covered too few languages. There were only three (British English, American English, Italian) for a partner base concentrated in France, Spain, Germany, Greece and Asia. The guidelines suggested Google Translate (guidelines).
  6. Hid unanswered questions. "Under the 'Unanswered' tab it only lists the last 4 days unanswered questions. I assume this is to avoid the embarrassment" (Sept 2022).
  7. Gave members no ownership. The ambassadors were employees, badges had no value, and there was no host identity. Nothing made a veteran host want to stay once their own problem was solved.
  8. Closed it with no visible migration path. The closure page has itself been deleted, and no archive or replacement community is linked from the Hub (as of Nov 2026).

Score justifications. Retention mechanics 2: points, badges and top-contributor lists existed, but people returned out of need, with no rituals, identity or switching cost. Content engine 3: AMAs, monthly webinars, Click. events and Click. Magazine were real but mostly happened outside the forum. Member ownership 1: the ambassadors were employees, members had no roles, and posts were removed without explanation. Staff investment 3: named Community Managers, a moderation team and several re-platforms, though staff couldn't touch accounts. Business impact evidence 1: no published figures. Durability 2: about eight years on three platforms, then closed with its archive removed.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Bring product owners in with a fixed, bounded format. Ten curated questions with dated answers is cheap, and it signals that someone with authority is listening.
  2. Name your community staff and let them act as connectors. Tagging the veteran who already solved a problem works better than a staff answer and builds status for that member.
  3. Recognise helpers publicly and regularly (weekly or monthly top contributors, tagged thank-you posts), but give the recognition real value: early access, a direct line to staff, or visibility customers can see.
  4. Organise by market and language where members' problems differ by jurisdiction (taxes, regulations, payouts).
  5. Use seasonal moments (awards, peak season) as recurring reasons to post and share.

Avoid these

  1. Don't use a peer forum as a substitute for support. If the top tag is "urgent" and moderators can't see accounts, add a staffed escalation path (as Amazon's Seller Forums at least partly did) or don't open the forum.
  2. Don't let moderation look like reputation management. Explain every removal, publish unanswered counts honestly, and keep a visible "you said, we did" log, especially when members are also commercial counterparties.
  3. Don't keep re-platforming, and don't close without a goodbye. Each migration lost habits and members. Removing the archive and the closure notice also throws away years of search-indexed answers.
