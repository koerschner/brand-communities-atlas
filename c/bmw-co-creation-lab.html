Now

Defunct. BMW now rents crowds case by case (Local Motors 2012, Startnext 2014, HYVE Crowd c. 2019–20)

How it pays off for the company

Cheap outside ideation for R&D and predevelopment (mobility services, interior personalisation, luggage space); a standing pool of pre-qualified co-creators and lead users to recruit for later projects and workshops; consumer research (it profiled its own participants); and, by the agency's own account, a channel for 'embedded marketing and branding campaigns' (~1.4M Facebook contact points from one contest). No revenue, cost, retention or NPS figures were ever published

TL;DR

  • What it is: a BMW Group ideas platform built and run by the agency HYVE. It launched with an urban-mobility ideas contest in March 2010, was declared "permanently established" in September 2010 as "the central platform for all customer integration projects for the BMW Group", and hosted a third and final contest (car trunks) in May–June 2013 [1][3][9].
  • Why people came back: the next contest, not the Lab itself. Each contest offered jury prizes (up to a Munich trip or €5,000), prizes for the "most active members" scored by a points-based Activity Index, public like/would-use votes, and a member wall for messages. A small core moved from contest to contest: the Portuguese designer "Marguis" was most active member in 2010's first contest, most active again in the second, and won the 2013 trunk contest [12][15][16][9].
  • What BMW fed it: contest briefs, a design configurator, juries of BMW managers and outside experts, workshop invitations, and in 2013 eleven BMW engineers trained by HYVE to coach entrants. There was almost no content beyond that: no blog, newsletter archive or progress reports on what happened to the ideas [4][9][16].
  • Biggest mistake: promising a permanent relationship but delivering episodic campaigns. Only three contests ran in four years, with a gap of more than two years between the second and third. Pages for finished contests kept inviting people to "join the contest and win great prizes" a year after the deadline, and the hub's front page showed the 2013 contest as "the latest project" until at least 2015 [17][25].
  • The one lesson: contests are good at producing short bursts of activity, and points tables produce plenty of activity of mixed quality. A co-creation community only lasts if there is a steady supply of projects and members can see what became of their ideas. BMW's own partners named exactly that problem in 2010 ("Relationships to participants were mostly nourished only during projects"), and the Lab never solved it [6].

What the community is

The BMW Group Co-Creation Lab was a web hub at bmwgroup-cocreationlab.com. It described itself as "a virtual meeting place for individuals interested in cars and all related topics, who want to share their ideas and opinions on tomorrow's automotive world" [24]. It ran on HYVE's IdeaNet software (footer: "© 2010 HYVE", later "powered by HYVE IdeaNet") [12][28]. The hub had an About page, a public grid of member avatars ("co-creators"), a login shared by all projects, and links to whichever project was running. Each project lived on its own site or subdomain with the same parts: Home, Submit, Ideas, Community, Contest and "My Area" [12][14][28]. The interior and trunk sites also had Chinese-language versions [14][28].

Three projects ran under the Lab's name:

# Project Dates Site
1 "Tomorrow's Urban Mobility Services" idea contest 2 Mar – 14 Apr 2010; jury 4 May; winners 17 May [11] bmwgroup-ideacontest.com
2 "BMW Group Interior Idea Contest" (personalising car interiors, with a colour/trim configurator) 8 Sep – 13 Oct 2010, extended to 27 Oct [3][4] interior-ideacontest.bmwgroup-cocreationlab.com
3 "BMW Group Trunk Idea Contest" (luggage space for X models and the MINI Countryman) from 17 May 2013, about one month [9][25] trunk-contest.bmwgroup-cocreationlab.com

The Lab came out of a decade of BMW open-innovation work. The Virtual Innovation Agency (VIA) started in 2001 as a portal where companies and individuals could submit technology ideas, and @BMW was still pointing people to it in 2019–20 [31][35][36]. The BMW Customer Innovation Lab launched in June 2003 (www.bmw.de/innovationlab). It used MIT-derived lead-user and toolkit methods to collect ideas for ConnectedDrive telematics and driver-assistance features, and 1,045 users took part [32][33][34]. The co-creation literature's "BMW Customer Innovation Lab" (the name used in the Ducati report) is this 2003 project, the Co-Creation Lab's direct ancestor.

No unofficial space was found. Searches of owner forums turned up only announcement posts. The E46 Fanatics post for the interior contest, written by a one-post account with a tracked referral link, drew 2,300 views and no replies [37].

Origin and strategy

The strategy was written down unusually clearly, because the agency and BMW's project lead presented it at conferences. In ESOMAR's Innovate 2010 talk, Gregor Jawecki and Volker Bilgram (HYVE) and Dr. Philipp Wiegandt (BMW Group) explained that BMW's earlier co-creation work (a netnography on sustainable energies, the urban-mobility contest, the 2003 Customer Innovation Lab, a lead-user project on "BMW on the internet") each used a different group of people. That meant "high efforts for identification and getting in touch with individuals", and "relationships to participants were mostly nourished only during projects" [6]. The Lab was meant to fix this. It would be "a central hub for various projects", "a meeting place for different user groups", "ongoing and openly accessible", "focused on end-consumers", and explicitly "NOT … a social media site" [6].

HYVE's Michael Bartl framed it as moving from projects to a "programme": "It is crucial to think of co-creation as a strategic programme rather than as a 'just in time' outsourcing of innovation tasks… characterized by continuous collaborative relationships with users." He promised that "the history of user interactions is displayed and communicated on the platform" and that the methods would cover idea contests, toolkits, virtual concept tests and lead-user applications [5][29][30].

The business case, as stated in the slides [6]:

  • For BMW: "bundling of co-creation expertise", "enduring relationships with users", "deep knowledge about co-creators", "easier sourcing of participants", "external communication and co-creation roadmap".
  • For members: "information on upcoming projects", "convenience (e.g. single sign on)", "appreciation as co-creators", "possibility to suggest own topics".
  • Marketing: "The intense consumer-company dialogue can also be used for embedded marketing and branding campaigns."

Wiegandt is the internal champion on record (he also sat on the interior jury). In 2013 the trunk contest was led by Dr. Matthias Meyer, "Leader Innovation Process", who HYVE later described as "Founder and Head of BMW Startup Garage" [9][46]. The Lab had nothing to do with subscriptions or support deflection. It was an R&D and market-research instrument wrapped in community language.

Trajectory: two contests in 2010, then silence for 31 months, one contest in 2013, then nothing. Total registrations (about 4,300 across a decade of projects) are small next to the brand's reach. Engagement was intense but short: in four or five weeks the interior contest produced an average of 24 evaluations and 12 comments per member [8].

What keeps people coming back

1. The next contest. Nothing ran between contests. The urban-mobility site told visitors after the jury that "the platform will remain online and we would appreciate if you would keep inspiring each other" [13]. In practice, activity after a deadline came down to a few stray ideas getting a couple of votes each [18].

2. Extrinsic prizes, tiered by quality and by activity. Contest 1 offered modest prizes: a portable navigation unit, an iPod touch and a MINI messenger bag, plus "most active member" awards [12]. Contest 2 offered a BMW Weekend Activity Package in Munich (travel, two hotel nights, a "meeting with developers of BMW's Research and Innovation Centre", a BMW Premium Tour), weekend rentals of a 6 Series/Z4 or 3 Series/MINI convertible, and watches, sunglasses and wallets for the three most active members [15]. By 2013 prizes were in cash (€5,000, €2,500, €1,500, plus €1,000 of BMW Shop vouchers for the most active) and a Munich workshop with BMW experts [9]. The prize budget grew over time, which suggests BMW found it needed more money to bring people back.

3. The Activity Index (points and a visible score). Each action earned points: an idea 10, a design 5, a comment 3, "leaving a message to another member" 2, a vote 1. Members could see their own and others' totals in "My Area", and the leaders won prizes [15]. It worked as a volume driver. The top interior member, "Iulian", joined on 11 Sep 2010 and by 3 Oct had 136 ideas, 525 evaluations and an Activity Counter of 2,814 [19]. It also produced filler. Profile walls are full of copy-paste "Hey! Welcome! Feel free to visit and comment my profile! Good luck! ;)" and "visit my works and comment… thanks :)" messages, each worth 2 points [19][20][22].

4. Peer recognition and a little belonging. Ideas carried public "I like this idea / I would use this" percentages [11]. Designs with the most likes went into a "hall of fame gallery" [15]. Winners were named with jury quotes [16]. A recognisable group (Marguis, natali, bonibom, bordo_boy, Oliver D., Xtal, HD) commented on one another's work across the contest [19][20][21][22]. Some norms of generosity appeared too. One member wrote: "I apologize for not seeing your idea 'Run Safe' before submitting my idea… you had the idea first, so you deserve the credit. I have edited the description to give you credit and have asked BMW to remove my idea" [19].

5. Access to BMW people. In 2013, eleven BMW employees were "trained in 'community management'" by HYVE and "provided support and guidance to the community". The welcome page promised that "BMW experts are also part of this community and will give as much feedback as possible" [9][28]. A HYVE community manager ("matthias athyve") posted greetings on new members' walls in 2010 [22]. The prize everyone actually wanted was face time in Munich.

6. Weak identity, deliberately. The Lab called members "co-creators" and showed them in an avatar grid [24]. But because BMW ruled out a "social media site" [6], there were no forums, groups, events or local chapters, and nothing to belong to when no contest was running. HYVE's survey of participants found 98% joined from "attachment to the brand", 62% hoping their idea would be realised, 60% to connect with BMW's innovation team, and only 41% to meet other car enthusiasts. Participants were 97% male and 54% owned a BMW Group car [6].

There were no switching costs and no in-product hooks. This was a website, not part of owning a BMW.

The content engine

Content was minimal, and that is the point to take away here. BMW and HYVE produced:

  • Contest briefs with categories (urban mobility: Mobility in general, Parking, Electric cars, Networks & Communication, Applications), timelines and evaluation rules [11].
  • A design toolkit: the interior contest's configurator, which produced 1,253 member designs [4][8]. Member-made content was the whole product.
  • Winner and jury pages with short jury quotes [12][16].
  • Press releases at launch and at results, picked up by BMW blogs [1][2][3][4][45].
  • Agency thought leadership: conference decks (ESOMAR 2010, KnowTech 2011), a WARC paper, a 2010 paper and a 2013 book chapter [6][7][8][29][30]. This content was aimed at the innovation-management world, not at members.

There was no newsletter archive, blog, video, "where are they now" update or report on which ideas BMW pursued. The 2013 case study says only that the top ideas went "to the BMW internal predevelopment department for further exploration" and that "multiple ideas generated in these projects have been developed and realized by the company", without naming any [9].

People, programs and governance

  • Operator: HYVE AG (Munich) designed the method, hosted the software and did community management. BMW supplied topics, jurors, experts and prizes [9][12][41].
  • Juries: contest 1 had BMW strategy and customer-foresight managers plus an Arthur D. Little director [2]. Contest 2 had BMW design and vehicle people (Wiegandt among them) and Prof. Peter Vink [16]. Contest 3 had "five BMW top managers" [9].
  • Expert coaches: the 2013 cohort of eleven BMW staff trained by HYVE [9].
  • Rules: registration was required to see ideas on the later sites [21]. Terms gave the organiser usage rights. Ideas could not be withdrawn: a new member who uploaded a broken design asked "How can I delete my design?" and another member replied "is too late now" [23].
  • No superuser program. The heaviest contributors were rewarded only through the most-active prizes. Members could supposedly "suggest own topics" [6], but no member-proposed project ever appeared.
  • Feedback loop: one-way. Ideas went in, a jury picked winners, and selected ideas went to predevelopment. Members never heard what happened next.

Growth loops

Recruitment was paid and pushed rather than viral: a BMW Group Facebook push, press releases, banners, emails and posts on owner forums. URLs carried tracking parameters such as referrerSource=fb, =banner, =forum and =mail [6][14][37]. The single sign-on was supposed to turn each contest's sign-ups into a standing pool for the next ("easier sourcing of participants") [6]. The repeat winners show this partly worked. But growth never compounded: total registrations rose from about 1,800 (Nov 2010) to 2,239 (Oct 2011) to 4,296 (2013, counting every project back to 2003) [6][8][9]. Ideas were hidden behind registration on later sites [21], so there was no SEO effect.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: output metrics only (ideas, designs, evaluations, time on site, Facebook reach) [8][9]. HYVE's KnowTech deck reported them under two headings, "product development perspective" and "marketing perspective", which shows how BMW was asked to judge the Lab [8].
  • Company and agency claims: "considered a great success by all parties involved" (trunk contest); "It is great to see a community partnering with us to solve problems of the future mobility" (Matthias Meyer, BMW) [9]. A contest-1 juror said the ideas "added innovative and valuable input to the topics we are already working on and confirmed us that the overall direction we are following leads into the right direction" [12]. That is a validation role, not a source of new direction. A second statement on the same page, which the archived layout places under Arthur D. Little's Marc Winterhoff, admitted "the submitted ideas were very close to existing services or service innovations, which are just being implemented" [12].
  • Product outcomes: none publicly traceable. The trunk winner, "Fits or not fit?", was an app to check whether a box fits in your car [9]. No shipped BMW feature has been credited to the Lab.
  • Academic attention: strong relative to its size. It appears in HYVE-authored papers [29][30], in a Polish case study pairing it with VW's People's Car Project [31], and in later work on how to measure idea contests [43]. The Lab's main lasting product may be HYVE's case material.

What they did right

  1. They named the real problem. The 2010 diagnosis that relationships died between projects, and the "programme, not project" framing, were correct and ahead of their time [5][6].
  2. Shared identity across projects. One login and one profile let the same people return. Marguis's run from 2010 to the 2013 win is the clearest proof that a standing pool beats starting from zero each time [12][16][9].
  3. They profiled their contributors. The participant survey (motives, skills, BMW ownership, 84% saying "my peers often ask me for advice") is a model for knowing who your co-creators are [6].
  4. They put BMW staff in the conversation. Training eleven engineers to coach entrants in 2013 raised both quality and engagement: 26.9 evaluations and 14 comments per member over one month [9].
  5. They rewarded quality and participation separately. Jury prizes for ideas plus most-active prizes kept non-winners involved and produced unusually high evaluation counts [8][15].
  6. Prizes tied to the brand experience. A trip to Munich to meet developers is cheap for BMW and very valuable to fans. The 2020 HYVE-run winner called his BMW visit "priceless" [41].

What they did wrong

  1. "Permanent" in name only. BMW announced "the permanent establishment" of the Lab in September 2010 [3], then ran nothing for 31 months, one contest in 2013, then nothing again. Consequence: the standing pool BMW wanted cooled off between contests, and by 2013 even the case study counted registrations "since 2003" [9].
  2. Zombie pages. Closed contest sites kept saying "Join the contest… and win great prizes!" into October 2011 [17]. The hub showed a 2013 contest as current until at least May 2015 [25]. By 2016 the public avatar grid showed what look like spam accounts [26]. A neglected brand space is worse than a closed one.
  3. Gamification that rewarded noise. Points for messages and votes produced walls of "visit my profile" spam and polite but empty feedback ("nice", "great work"). One member complained: "I saw many drawings and even many successful tests, but nobody has the courage to criticize here" [15][19][20].
  4. Members never saw what happened to their ideas. There were no progress reports and no credited features. Nearly two thirds of participants (62%) came hoping their ideas would be realised [6], so this silence undermined the main reason people took part.
  5. Ruling out social features. "NOT a social media site" [6] left people nothing to do between briefs. ESOMAR's own closing slide warned that "new governance and incentive models may be needed" to manage thousands of consumers [6]. None arrived.
  6. Fragmenting instead of consolidating. After 2013 BMW ran crowd projects on other companies' communities: Local Motors' Forge (2012, nearly 3,500 submissions) [38], Startnext's crowdfunding "Mobility Experience Challenge" (2014) [39][40], and HYVE Crowd contests ("Making mobility hassle-free", 247 participants; "Mobility and Energy Services 2030") [41][42]. Each campaign grew someone else's community instead of BMW's own. One researcher even describes hyvecrowd.com as a project that "began as BMW Group Co-Creation Lab" [44].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. One identity across every project. A single login, profile and history turns each campaign into a deposit in a pool you can come back to, instead of a fresh recruiting cost.
  2. Profile your contributors early. Survey motives and skills so you know who your lead users are and why they came (here, brand attachment and hope of seeing their idea built).
  3. Put your own experts in the thread. Trained staff giving real feedback lifts idea quality and engagement more than bigger prizes do.
  4. Reward quality and participation on separate tracks, and make the top reward access (a workshop with the team, a visit), not just merchandise.
  5. Write the strategy down and say it publicly. The "programme, not project" framing let BMW and HYVE line up internally. Pair it with a published schedule of projects.

Avoid these

  1. Don't promise "permanent" if you're funding campaigns. If projects come one at a time, say so, and retire or archive each site cleanly when it ends.
  2. Don't score raw volume. Points for messages and votes buy spam and empty praise. Weight points by what peers or staff rate as useful, or don't score social actions at all.
  3. Don't let ideas disappear. Report back on every shortlisted idea, credit contributors by name when anything ships, and show the members' "history of user interactions" you promised.

Open questions

  • How many people took part in more than one contest? Only anecdotal evidence (Marguis, bonibom, natali) was found.
  • Did any Lab idea ship in a BMW or MINI product? Neither BMW nor HYVE names one.
  • Why the 2011–2012 gap? Possibly budget, a change of champion, or a move to Local Motors and other external crowds. No source explains it.
  • What were the "seven projects since 2003" HYVE counts [9]? Netnography and lead-user studies are named in [6], but there is no complete list.
  • What did Bartl et al.'s 2013 chapter conclude in hindsight [30]? It is paywalled and was not read.

Related communities worth adding

  • BMW Virtual Innovation Agency (VIA) — BMW Group — a 2001 portal for outside technology ideas, still referenced by @BMW in 2019–20; the long-running B2B/B2C idea intake behind the Lab.
  • BMW Customer Innovation Lab — BMW Group — a 2003 lead-user/toolkit portal for ConnectedDrive (1,045 users); the "Customer Innovation Lab" cited next to Ducati's Tech Café. Could merge into this entry.
  • BMW Mobility Experience Challenge — BMW Group with Startnext — a 2014 crowdfunding-plus-idea contest for car apps; studied in Jovanović et al. (2017).
  • HYVE Crowd (hyvecrowd.com) — HYVE AG — a cross-brand crowdsourcing community (BMW, Bombardier, VW, Swiss utilities) that took over BMW contests after the Lab; an agency-run "rented crowd" model.
  • Audi Virtual Lab — Audi — early-2000s online co-design of infotainment; the sister German-auto co-creation project (also Bartl/HYVE).
  • NIVEA Invisible for Black & White co-creation — Beiersdorf with HYVE — c. 2011 netnography and co-creation behind a launched deodorant line; a HYVE case that did end in a shipped product (per HYVE's own decks).
  • BMW × Local Motors Urban Driving Experience Challenge — BMW Group and Local Motors — a 2012 contest on the Forge, with nearly 3,500 submissions (partly covered under local-motors).
