TL;DR

  • What it was: a free blogging platform and social network for Salon readers at open.salon.com. Private beta in spring 2008, public beta 24 July 2008, official launch 11 August 2008. Staff edited a "cover" from member posts and promoted the best to Salon.com. It was a 2009 National Magazine Award finalist for interactive feature. It had about 35,000 bloggers by November 2009, and Salon closed it in March 2015 [1][2][7][13][22].
  • Why people came back: to be read, and possibly to be published. The cover, Editor's Picks, a 48-hour "Top Rated" list and the chance of a slot on "Big Salon" made a ladder for aspiring writers. Comment-for-comment reciprocity and friendships turned it into a tight social group with its own rituals [1][5][24][28].
  • What Salon fed it: at the start, a lot. Two cover updates a day, a themed slot for each weekday, weekly "Open Call" prompts, "Open Chat" member interviews, and about 100 member pieces a month moved into the magazine by early 2011 [1][5][8][22].
  • Biggest mistake: Salon never re-platformed it. In 2011 Salon.com moved to WordPress, but Open Salon stayed on "too old and creaky" software on servers in Sacramento. Spam bots took over in late 2012, the cover sat frozen for weeks, and regulars boycotted to a member-run fork. Salon then let it drift for two more years and closed it with 14 days' read-only access [9][10][19][13].
  • The lesson: a "write for us and we might feature you" community runs on the editor's attention and on platform upkeep. Once promotion stops, the deal has nothing left in it for members.

What the community is

Open Salon ("OS" to its members) was "part blogging platform, part social network." Anyone could sign up without an invitation and start a blog, a profile page, a friends list and private messages [2][5]. What set it apart from Blogger or WordPress was the cover: a home page "like a real-time magazine cover" that Open Salon editors programmed from member posts, next to automatic "most-read" and "highest-rated" lists. In the beta, "a new issue goes up every evening at 8 p.m. ET; we update the cover every morning at 11 a.m. ET" [1][2]. Salon said from the start that "we'll frequently choose the best posts to feature on Salon" [2].

Official spaces: open.salon.com (also opensalon.com) and its cover, Editor's Picks, Popularity and Most Recent views [6]. Promotion slots on Salon.com and Salon's topic pages from the November 2009 redesign [7]. An Open Salon Facebook page run by staff [38].

Unofficial spaces: Our Salon (oursalon.ning.com), a member-built Ning network with blogs, a "Writers Block," a paper.li "magazine" and a shop. It was live by June 2012 and took in most regulars during the December 2012 boycott [10][26]. Orphans of Open Salon, a Facebook group where the Open Salon editor himself posted status updates while he couldn't log in to his own site [10]. Later, a Medium publication called The Refugees of Open Salon and Our Salon's own Medium account [27][26].

Status: defunct. Logins stopped on 9 March 2015 and the site went dark on 31 March 2015 [13][21][22]. Salon Media Group stopped publishing altogether on 5 November 2026 [36].

Origin and strategy

  • Lineage. Open Salon descended from Salon Blogs (2002), which co-founder Scott Rosenberg started on Radio UserLand. That program "had sort of fallen into suspended animation because the technology platform and company that we'd partnered with were moribund." Rosenberg prototyped a successor in 2006–07 and then left, and Kerry Lauerman and his team built and launched it [3][4]. Lauerman's company bio credits him with founding "Salon's blog network, Open Salon." He became editor-in-chief in November 2010 [34]. Before Open Salon came Salon's forum Table Talk (1995–2011) and automated letters to the editor (late 2005). By 2008 letters drew "almost 10 percent of our monthly readership," which convinced editor-in-chief Joan Walsh that readers were Salon's "best asset" [2].
  • The pitch to members: "Open Salon gets rid of traditional gatekeepers, and makes our smart, creative audience full partners in Salon's publishing future… you can use our audience to build your own audience" (Joan Walsh) [2]. Walsh told CJR that member blogs "aren't merely sidebars. They're the main show" [4].
  • The pitch to investors was blunter. The FY2008 10-K, written before launch, says: "Management believes Open Salon will attract and retain unique users, increase advertising inventory and lower its incremental editorial costs." Salon also planned to spend its leftover NBC and Rainbow barter ad credits promoting it [14]. Salon was losing money: FY2008 revenue was about $7.5M against $10.9M in operating costs, as an Open Salon blogger noticed and CJR reported [4].
  • Monetization for members was a tip jar. Tippem, built with Revolution MoneyExchange, let readers tip writers with no fees, and each new member got a $10 credit to give away [2][4][5]. Rosenberg expected more reward schemes to follow [3]. No evidence found that any did. The FY2010 and FY2013 10-Ks say Open Salon let readers "generate advertising revenue," but no revenue-share program is documented anywhere else [17][20].

Trajectory: launch in mid-2008, fast growth to a peak in 2009–2011, a technical collapse in late 2012, a long tail of loyal posters with almost no staff curation in 2013–14, and closure in 2015. No traffic numbers for Open Salon itself were ever disclosed. The 10-Ks only call it "a reliable source of content and audience traffic" (FY2010, FY2011) [17].

What keeps people coming back

  1. A promotion ladder. Members could climb from rating to Top Rated, then to Editor's Pick, then to the Open Salon cover, then to Salon.com ("Big Salon" in member slang) [5][24]. The FAQ explains: "Every day Open Salon's editors select among the Editor's Picks to build a smart, fun and entertaining home page. Content selected to be a good match for Salon.com may be spotlighted there as well" [5]. A Salon slot was a real payoff. John Manchester had three OS pieces run on Salon, one with "90,000 page views," and they led to bylines at Talking Writing and The Good Men Project, "even a paid gig! ($300)" [24]. Joy-Ann Reid, later an MSNBC host, cross-posted her blog to Open Salon in 2008 and "was immediately a huge hit" (Joan Walsh) [25]. She was on the cover in May 2009 [6].
  2. Lightweight status signals. A thumbs-up "Rate" button, a "Top Rated" list over the past 48 hours, the Editor's Pick badge and an activity feed that refreshed every five minutes with "posting, commenting, joining, and rating." Each member could appear in the feed once per window, so it represented members "equally" [5].
  3. Fixed rituals. Each weekday in the beta had a themed cover slot: Monday travel photo (tag Roadie), Tuesday food (Foodie), Wednesday personal narrative (True Story), Thursday Open Call response, Friday pet photo (Aw), weekend Big Story roundups [1]. The Open Call was a standing staff prompt on the cover. Members answered by tagging posts "opencall," as with "Open Call: How I am affected by Prop 8" in May 2009 [5][6]. From May 2009, Open Chats were five-minute staff interviews with members [22].
  4. Reciprocity and friendship. "Commenting at Open Salon was of course a game – you comment on your favorites, and they'll comment on you. But it was more than that… I made friends there. A few I met in person" [24]. Members built their own rituals: an "OS Book Club" account (May 2009) [6], and comment threads that turned into group fiction "like the pirate ship" [28]. The Medium refugees describe "a few hundred writers and poets and photographers and cooks and comics and geeks and provocateurs" who "'met' on an invisible ship of friends and fools" [27].
  5. Identity. Members called themselves "OSers," the parent site "Big Salon," and the place "OS." The CJR described the tone as "up-market," "surprisingly polite and courteous… a tone that Salon staffers have tried very hard to cultivate" [4][24].
  6. Switching costs came from friends, followers and archives, not from the product. When the platform failed, the core group moved together, which shows the social graph mattered more than the software [10][26].

The content engine

  • Staff-made content was mostly curation. Twice-daily cover programming, Editor's Picks, themed slots, Open Calls and Open Chats [1][5][22]. Walsh and Lauerman wrote launch and welcome posts and kept favorite-blogger blogrolls [2]. Salon's Broadsheet blog featured OS women writers the week of launch [39].
  • Member content became Salon's product. In the November 2009 redesign, Open Salon posts sat inside Salon's topic pages, with "as much real estate… reserved for Open Salon as for the site's original articles" [7][32]. By FY2012 Salon's 10-K says lifestyle personal essays "often… are plucked from Open Salon," and that OS reader-writers "contributed heavily" to Salon series such as "Interview with my Bully," "Pinched," "My Brilliant Second Career" and "Made" [18]. Promoted pieces ran under Salon.com URLs tagged "open2010", for example Manchester's essay at https://www.salon.com/2010/06/05/building_my_father_s_coffin_open2010/ [24].
  • Onboarding existing bloggers. An RSS/Atom import let people cross-post an outside blog automatically [5]. Salon editors asked favorite bloggers to cross-post and "nudge[d]" them when a post was worth running [25].
  • Cadence fell with staff. By 2012 one person, Jacob Sugarman, was both "Open Salon Editor" and cover editor [35]. In June 2014 the curated cover showed posts from mid-to-late 2013 while the automatic Top Rated list was current [11].

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: Kerry Lauerman (founder, 2008–10, then EIC), Joan Walsh (EIC and public champion), unnamed Open Salon editors, and later Jacob Sugarman (about 2012 to 2016 per his profile) [34][35]. In October 2011 Salon added "an additional site manager" to keep OS running [9]. Hard numbers on team size: no evidence found beyond about $0.1M a quarter in added personnel cost at launch [15].
  • Moderation: member flagging sent posts to "the editorial staff of Open Salon." There was a trainable spam filter for private messages, and bloggers could delete comments or turn them off on their own posts [5]. There was no ambassador, moderator or superuser program (no evidence found).
  • Feedback loop: launch posts invited bug reports ("It's still in beta… let us know") [2]. Member feedback on Tippem showed up as public posts. Donna Sandstrom wrote that tipping made inviting friends feel "like… an Amway party" [4]. No evidence found of a formal ideas channel.

Growth loops

  • Brand halo plus promotion. "You can use our audience to build your own audience" [2]. A Salon.com feature could bring tens of thousands of readers (90,000 page views in Manchester's case), which pulled in new writers [24].
  • Invites: "Invite friends" links throughout the site [5].
  • Paid and barter marketing: NBC and Rainbow ad credits set aside for the launch [14][16].
  • Press: Techmeme, CNET, ReadWriteWeb and CJR at launch, partly because of the tipping angle [3][4][33]. One member later said she found OS through the film Julie & Julia. That is probably a mix-up: Julie Powell's original blog ran on Salon's earlier blog program, but the memory still shows the Salon brand pulling in writers [28].
  • SEO from tens of thousands of public posts: plausible but unmeasured (no evidence found).

Evidence of business impact

  • Company claims (10-Ks): its audience "has grown consistently since its launch, and is expected to contribute to revenues in fiscal 2010" (FY2009). It "has become a reliable source of content and audience traffic" (FY2010–11). "Open Salon attracts unique users, resulting in increased advertising inventory and lowering its incremental editorial costs" (FY2012). Salon was "seeking less costly sources of content, including… popular articles and blogs from Open Salon" (FY2012–13) [16][17][18][20].
  • Hard numbers: none specific to Open Salon. For context, Salon's advertising revenue fell from $5.43M (FY2008) to $5.20M (FY2009) to $2.97M (FY2010) while Open Salon grew [17]. Salon's total investment was small: $95K of capitalized software, all of it by March 2009, and "no amounts were capitalized in fiscal years 2010 through 2015" [20][21].
  • Recognition: National Magazine Award finalist, 2009, interactive feature [22][23].
  • Verdict: Open Salon delivered cheap content and some traffic. The company never showed that it made money and stopped investing in it the year after launch.

What they did right

  1. Curation on top of open publishing. Anyone could post, but editors set the cover. That curbed the noise problem of open platforms and made recognition feel real. The ASME finalist nod and CJR's praise of the "civilized tone" back this up [4][22].
  2. A real reward: publication. About 100 member pieces a month went into the magazine, and members' writing careers grew from it [8][24][25].
  3. Rituals from day one. Weekday themes and Open Calls gave newcomers something easy to write about and gave regulars appointments to keep [1][5].
  4. Seeded with the right people. About 1,300 hand-invited beta users and invited cross-posting bloggers set the tone. Rosenberg credited Flickr's lesson that "the initial conditions of any online community really do set its course for years to come" [1][4][25].
  5. Low friction. No invitation, instant blogging, RSS import, profiles, friends and messages [2][5].

What they did wrong

  1. Underfunded the platform. Only $95K of capitalized development, and none after FY2009 [20][21]. When Salon.com moved to WordPress in October 2011, OS was promised a move "eventually" [9]. In late 2012 the database migration failed because "the software is simply too old and creaky," and OS was still on its own servers in Sacramento while Salon.com ran on AWS [10][19]. Result: a spam-bot flood, broken logins, a cover that couldn't be updated, and a boycott.
  2. Let the promotion ladder go quiet. OS pieces were "frequently featured on the front page of Salon in the first two years… but eventually disappeared" [22]. By 2014 the curated cover was months out of date [11]. Result: members lost their main reason to write there. Wikipedia sums it up as "editorial neglect" [23].
  3. Talked to members off-site and in private. During the 2012 crisis the editor posted updates in the members' Facebook group because he couldn't log in to OS. Members couldn't even link to the fork without their posts vanishing, and some suspected Salon tolerated the spammers for money [10]. Result: lost trust, and the core moved to Our Salon.
  4. A monetization design that didn't fit. Tippem required a Social Security number through a third-party wallet [5]. Members disliked it ("an Amway party") and critics expected it to "cheapen the material" [4][33]. Money mostly moved between members, as CJR noted [4]. It quietly disappeared (no evidence found of an announcement). Meanwhile Salon ran members' free work as its own lifestyle content, and members noticed ("I have made roughly 150 dollars from writing" after publication "on the main site at Salon") [29][18].
  5. Shut it down abruptly. "Due to the changing landscape of blogging" [13], logins were cut on the day of the announcement, and members got 14 days to view their content with no clear export [13][22]. This repeated Salon's 2011 deletion of Table Talk (see the salon-table-talk report). A later member recalls being given an archive download URL, so some export may have existed [30].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make recognition a ladder with a real top rung. Peer ratings, then staff picks, then the flagship channel. Open Salon's promotion to Salon.com was its strongest retention hook [5][8][24].
  2. Run fixed weekly prompts (themed days, Open Calls) so newcomers always have something to post and regulars have appointments [1][5].
  3. Seed with a hand-picked beta and set norms early. The first 1,300 members set a tone that lasted for years [1][4].
  4. Make it easy to bring existing work in (RSS import, cross-posting) so established creators can join cheaply [5][25].
  5. Show who is active (activity feed, friends, messages) so writing feels social, not solitary [5].

Avoid these

  1. Don't launch a community on a one-time build budget. Plan to re-platform and fight spam for its whole life, or plan its exit from the start [9][10][20].
  2. Don't let the promise lapse. If the deal is "write well and we'll feature you," stopping the features breaks the deal even if the site stays up [11][22].
  3. Don't treat members' work as free inventory without sharing value or giving them their data. Leave a real export path and a long notice period when you close [13][29].

Score notes

  • Retention mechanics 3: a strong promotion ladder, ratings and weekly rituals, but nothing beyond them, and the ladder faded after about 2011.
  • Content engine 3: early on, twice-daily curation, prompts, member interviews and Salon series built from member work. It shrank to one editor and a cover months out of date.
  • Member ownership 4: members wrote all the content and built their own book club, group fiction, a Facebook group and a working fork.
  • Staff investment 1: $95K of capitalized software and nothing after FY2009. The site was never migrated, and one editor was left by 2012.
  • Business impact evidence 2: only qualitative 10-K claims and an ASME finalist nod, with no Open Salon numbers.
  • Durability 1: about four healthy years, broken in late 2012, closed in March 2015.
