TL;DR

  • What it was: American Express OPEN's small-business site, launched in 2007 at openforum.com. It started as a companion to live networking events, picked up Jive discussion boards, then the Idea Hub expert blog and the cardmember-only Connectodex directory (2009), a LinkedIn-powered advice exchange (2013–14), and finally became a straight publication. Amex renamed it Business Trends & Insights in Nov 2018. Today the same URLs are "Amex Business Intel" (brandpublishing.com.br; live site).
  • Why people came back: useful, non-promotional expert advice on cash flow, marketing, hiring and social media, published 50+ times a week by 200+ named contributors (Roen, 2013). Peer interaction was always the weaker draw: outsiders described it as "more articles and less community" (Ask MetaFilter, 2011).
  • What Amex put in: staff "OPEN Hosts" who seeded and moderated threads, a network of paid and influencer authors (Federated Media's Guy Kawasaki, Henry Blodget, Mashable), national TV ads for the community itself (2009–10), LinkedIn login, a mobile app (2010), and live events such as CEO BootCamp.
  • Biggest mistake: Amex never committed to member-to-member value. It rebuilt the participation layer every two to three years (boards → Connectodex → advice exchange → none), gated networking to cardmembers, and in time let editors and influencers stand in for the community. When distribution moved to social platforms, nothing held members on the site, and the brand was retired.
  • The one lesson: an expert content engine can bring a million monthly visitors and build brand preference. A community still needs a reason for members to talk to each other that the content alone doesn't give them, and that has to be designed in from day one and kept stable.

What the community is

OPEN Forum was American Express OPEN's (the small-business card division's) owned site for small-business owners. It ran from 2007 to 2018: at openforum.com, then from about 2014 at americanexpress.com/us/small-business/openforum, then at /en-us/business/trends-and-insights after the Nov 2018 rename (brandpublishing.com.br, 2019). Origin and strategy

  • The business thesis. "We already have a large part of the pie so our biggest opportunity is with small business growth: if they grow, we grow" (Mary Ann Fitzmaurice Reilly, SVP Partnerships & Business Development, OPEN; Fast Company, Jul 2010). Amex already led small-business cards, so OPEN Forum's job was loyalty, share of wallet and preference, not top-of-funnel volume.
  • Research-led. OPEN's semi-annual Small Business Monitor found "nearly 60% of customers... found this new social media world really challenging" and "only 13% were tapping into social networking because they didn't know how" (Fast Company). Director Jason Rudman described owners as having a "high relationship IQ" but a "low social media IQ" (ZDNet, 2009).
  • From exclusive to open. Roen's 2013 deck describes "OPEN in 2006: Exclusive Access & Insights" ("Inside the Entrepreneurial Mind" events) becoming "OPEN Forum 2007: Access & Insights for All" (Roen, FinanceConnect 2013).
  • Stated objectives (2009): "Engage business owners in a new set of experiences that increase loyalty, value perception, and relevance of our brand... attract partners... create additional compelling benefits for Cardmembers and convert prospects" (Rudman, ZDNet).
  • The 2009 relaunch was a recession play. "It was no longer about the sell message, small business owners trying to survive were looking for information" (Rudman, Chief Marketer). Crispin Porter + Bogusky was the agency.
  • Long-term commitment, no ROI test. "We didn't go into this lightly and we didn't go into this as a test... Don't just dip your toe into the water" (Fitzmaurice Reilly, Fast Company).
  • Champions: Marcy Shinder (VP, announced the 2007 launch; SlideShare summary), Fitzmaurice Reilly, Jason Rudman (Director, 2009–10), Scott Roen (VP Digital Marketing & Innovation, 2010–14), Courtney Colwell (Director of OPEN Forum and content marketing, ~2015–17; Contently, 2016), and later Clayton Ruebensaal (EVP Global B2B Marketing, Business Class era; Adweek).

Trajectory: the boards were thin from 2007 to 2008. The 2009 relaunch then grew traffic roughly fivefold to more than a million UVs a month, which held from about 2010 to 2017. That traffic came for content. Participation peaked at about 15,000 directory profiles, which is roughly 1% of monthly visitors. I found no public traffic figures for the post-2018 successors.

What keeps people coming back

  • Useful content, not rituals. The real retention driver was a reliable flow of practical advice: 7 topic areas in 2009, later Customers, Leadership, Marketing, Money and Technology, at 50+ pieces a week. I found no evidence of streaks, seasons or weekly live rituals, apart from in-person events.
  • Parasocial ties to experts. Named columnists (Guy Kawasaki, Anita Campbell, John Jantsch, Barry Moltz, Penelope Trunk) carried their audiences with them. "Featured contributor" modules linked straight to their Connectodex profiles (Wayback, 2012). Amex said the authors drove earned traffic: "leveraging our authors, which have a tremendous following" (Chief Marketer).
  • Staff hosts. In 2007–08, "OPEN Host" Heidi, Jane, Scott, Tim and Melissa started most threads and replied in person. In one 2008 thread Heidi coached inventor Ron Komorowski on his marketing photo over several posts and pointed him to another member's advice (Wayback).
  • Status and recognition (light). Posts had 1–5 star ratings and post counts, and members had "Professional" or "Member" badges (2008). The 2013 advice exchange let members "rate each other's helpfulness" (Marketing Insider Group). "Open Forum frequently selects people from Connectodex and profiles them on the site" (Chief Marketer), so exposure for your business was the reward.
  • Gated identity as a switching cost. Only OPEN Cardmembers could build a Connectodex profile, "market their business, find vendors and build relationships" (Finextra). One reviewer said it gave members "the feeling of being in a special club" (MediaShower).
  • Product hooks: a mobile app (2010; 10-K 2011) with push notifications (Exhibitor), email digests, and links from the cardmember account navigation ("Powerful Connections: Grow your business network at OPEN Forum").
  • IRL events: OPEN Forum CEO BootCamp (Chicago, women's editions with the SBA), SCORE Speed Coaching, and NYT Small Business Summit tie-ins (bizjournals; GovEvents; Amex investor deck, 2011). Event talks were recycled as articles.

Score, retention mechanics 2/5: aside from a gated directory, staff hosts and star ratings, I found no designed habit loop, progression or peer accountability.

The content engine

  • Curated expert network. Federated Media supplied the first big names in 2009 (Finextra). By 2013 there were 200+ contributors, and Roen's deck says "Curating Expertise Became Critical to Scale" and "Reciprocal Altruism works" (Roen, 2013). Contributors were often small-business consultants who got exposure in return (for example the Franchise King, writing since 2008: thefranchiseking.com). By 2026 bylines read "Amex Business Intel™ Freelance Contributor".
  • Formats: articles, infographics (Federated Media commissioned, e.g. Susannah Brinkley), syndicated video (MSNBC's Your Business, 2012), Crash Courses with VERI, Tumblr curation, and longform docu-series such as The Journey: Buchi Kombucha, filmed over three months (Contently; Adams Wood). In the Business Class era it added celebrity owner videos (Patti LaBelle, Nick Offerman) and "15 Stories" (CMI).
  • Topics chosen by demand. Colwell: "what's there, is there because real business owners asked for it". Input came from customer service, client managers, search, social and members (Entrepreneur).
  • No product pitches in the classic era. Commentators called this the core of its credibility (Mark D Hogan, 2013), though one 2011 reader noted "a minority of the articles are over-promotional" (MetaFilter). The 2026 hub openly promotes products.
  • Member content was used as a funnel. The 2013 relaunch had "UGC... selected by editors to maximize engagement" and was "Leveraging UGC to drive membership" (Roen, 2014). In 2016 Colwell aimed for "content as community", with more pieces "directly from the site's core audience" (Contently).

Score, content engine 5/5: it is widely cited as the benchmark B2B brand publication, produced at high volume and quality for more than a decade.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: a Director of OPEN Forum reporting into OPEN digital marketing, which a VP ran. Roen "manages a team charged with the strategy, development, and management of" OPEN Forum (LinkedIn FinanceConnect). A 2007–08 host team of at least five named moderators ran the boards. Agencies and partners included Crispin Porter + Bogusky, Federated Media, Percolate, VERI, Contently (2016) and dentsu mcgarrybowen (2021). Score, staff investment 4/5: a large, sustained budget including TV, but it went mostly to content rather than community managers.
  • Superuser program: none formal. Connectodex "power users" such as Fanminder CEO Paul Rosenfield, who reportedly found "partners, customers, investors and employees", were held up as examples (Rudman, via SocialMediaCouncil snippet). Expert contributors were in effect the ambassador tier.
  • Rules: Community Guidelines, Report Abuse links and hosts (2007). There was a help@openforum.com address.
  • Feedback loop: content topics, not product. Usage was measured monthly by UVs, repeat visits, time on site and Connectodex enrollments, plus brand-impact tracking from Q4 2008 (ZDNet). Amex mined its Small Business Monitor survey to decide what to build.

Growth loops

  1. Earned and social sharing: 85% of traffic was non-paid. "Readers share with their own social graphs, bringing back more visitors... saves marketing dollars" (Roen, 2011).
  2. Contributor audiences: authors' own sites and blogs brought 21.8M impressions to OPEN Forum in 2009 (Chief Marketer).
  3. SEO from a deep archive of evergreen how-to articles. This is inferred: no traffic split was published.
  4. Paid amplification of the community itself: national TV spots at the July 2009 launch, and a second wave six months later that quoted real member questions ("Ask a question. Find an answer. Join the conversation at openforum.com") (The Financial Brand, 2010).
  5. Partner distribution: LinkedIn login and co-marketing ("LinkedIn Critical to our Scale & Engagement"), FedEx's sponsored "The World" Idea Hub topic, and startup partnerships.
  6. Spin-off movement: Small Business Saturday (Nov 27 2010) came out of the same OPEN team and was credited at the time to "American Express OPEN Forum, the group that organizes the branding" (Inc. snippet, 2012). Its Neighborhood Champions ambassador program still runs (Main Street America).

Evidence of business impact

  • Company-reported brand lift: Roen's 2011 deck says "OPEN Forum Drives Brand Metrics" for product consideration, "differentiated products & services" and likelihood to recommend. The charts aren't legible in the public copy (Roen, 2011).
  • NPS: businesses that used Connectodex "reported significantly higher net promoter scores" (HBS case 712-447, Piskorski & Chen, 2012/2013, via search snippet; HBS listing). This is self-selected, so it is correlation, not causation.
  • Marketing efficiency: 85% non-paid traffic (company claim). At the 2017 CES booth Amex used an OPEN Forum app push and recorded "29 percent more card applications than the previous year", but that came from a whole event program (Exhibitor).
  • Filings: the 10-K listed OPEN Forum among OPEN cardmember benefits every year from 2009 ("community-driven Web Site") to 2016 (10-K 2009; 10-K 2016). The 2011 10-K and the Feb 2011 investor-day deck name its mobile platform as a 2010 highlight. I found no revenue, retention or churn figures tied to it.
  • Third-party research: Forrester case "American Express OPEN Capitalizes On Small Businesses' Craving For Community" (2011, paywalled; listing). Awards: 2010 IMA, second place for Internet-based loyalty.

Score, business-impact evidence 3/5: consistent company claims and an HBS correlation, but no hard financial attribution.

What they did right

  1. Built on research and kept the mission. The site answered a measured pain point (social media and growth know-how) with a stable mission: "helping these businesses do more business" (Entrepreneur).
  2. Kept product pitches out. That earned the trust a bank voice lacks. In Colwell's words: "it's not us answering it. It's not our voice" (Contently).
  3. Borrowed credibility at scale. The 150 → 200+ expert network produced output and distribution together (21.8M referred impressions in 2009).
  4. Kept marketing the community after launch. The second TV wave six months in was rare in finance and grew traffic (Financial Brand).
  5. Tied it to the card. The gated Connectodex, featured-member exposure and 10-K benefit listing made it part of the card's value, and Connectodex users scored higher on NPS.
  6. Was early on new channels. @openforum was Amex's first Twitter account in 2009, followed by Tumblr in 2011, a mobile app in 2010 and native social video by 2016.

What they did wrong

  1. Staff-seeded boards never reached critical mass. In 2007 most threads were host prompts with zero replies (Wayback). Amex answered by rebranding the site as a blog in 2008 rather than fixing the reason to post. Consequence: content became the product and members became readers.
  2. The participation layer was replatformed every 2–3 years. Jive boards (2007), blog (2008), Idea Hub plus Connectodex (2009), LinkedIn login (2010), a LinkedIn-powered advice exchange in "private beta" (2013), a content-first redesign (2015). The 2014 Connectodex page admits it had been "nearly a year since we took the previous content-rich OPEN Forum and integrated the networking capabilities of the Connectodex" (search snippet; page now 404s). Consequence: every relaunch reset member history, and I found no evidence that profiles or Q&A archives carried through.
  3. Gating limited the network. Connectodex was cardmember-only, which gave a "special club" feel but capped networking value at around 15,000 profiles against more than a million visitors. LinkedIn login (2010) and "powered by LinkedIn" (2013) then outsourced identity to the very network members could use without Amex.
  4. Experts and editors took the members' place. "We got really heavy into content over the past several years... Now we're rethinking, 'What does that idea of community mean again'" (Colwell, 2016). Editors picked the UGC. Outsiders saw "more articles and less community" (MetaFilter, 2011).
  5. It gave up the home base to social platforms. "It's unrealistic to expect people to click through from wherever they are to come to your site" (Colwell, 2016). That was rational for reach, but it removed the last reason to keep member features on site.
  6. The brand was retired and the hub drifted toward product promotion. OPEN Savings ended in June 2018 (Doctor of Credit), Amex phased out the OPEN brand, and OPEN Forum became Business Trends & Insights (Nov 2018), then Business Class (2020), then Amex Business Intel. The 2026 home page leads with card and banking pitches (live site), the opposite of the original no-pitch rule. Even CMI notes that "OPEN Forum was a good name given its community mission, but Business Class works well for the current mission" (CMI).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Start from measured pain and write a mission you won't change. OPEN surveyed its customers twice a year and kept "help businesses do more business" for more than a decade.
  2. Recruit an expert contributor network that brings its own audience. It solves content volume, credibility and distribution at once. Pay them in exposure as well as money, and feature them prominently.
  3. Ban product pitches from the community surface. Let the brand benefit by association ("blur the sell"); trust compounds.
  4. Keep marketing the community after launch, using real member questions in the ads.
  5. Make membership a visible customer benefit: list it with the product perks, give members exposure (featured profiles), and measure NPS for participants against non-participants.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let staff hosts and editors stand in for peer value. If members only show up to read, you've built a publication. Design a reason to post (peer answers, deals, introductions) and measure reply rates, not page views.
  2. Don't replatform the participation layer every few years or outsource identity to another network. Keep profiles, reputations and archives continuous.
  3. Don't gate the network so tightly that it can't reach critical mass, and don't later swing the hub to product promotion. Both erode what made the space worth visiting.
