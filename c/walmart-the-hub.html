How it pays off for the company

A back-to-school apparel promotion: it was meant to close Walmart's 'trend gap' with teens (Target was winning more teen apparel spending), collect wishlists and registration data, and source user-made TV ads. It never had a retention or subscription role, and no sales impact was reported

TL;DR

  • What it is: "The HUB (School Your Way)," a Walmart and Sony back-to-school site launched in July 2006 at schoolyourway.walmart.com. It copied MySpace's look (teens aged 13-18 made profile pages called "Hubster pages," with photos, a song and a short video), but underneath it was a sweepstakes-style contest: the prizes were Sony gear and having your video turned into a Walmart TV commercial. Entries closed Aug 5, 2006, and the site was shut by early October (official rules, Wayback; Forbes).
  • Why people came back: almost nothing brought them back. Members could not message, email or befriend each other. All content waited for approval, and every registrant's parent got an email. The only built-in reasons to return were "vote for a page or video once per day," a wishlist of Walmart products, and a style quiz (participation rules; Ad Age).
  • What Walmart fed it: scripted videos of four actors playing teen "Hubsters" (a fashionista, a Texas football player, a skateboarder and an R&B singer), fashion "Hot List" lookbooks with add-to-wishlist buttons, a preset menu of songs, and big Sony prizes. Seven companies are named in the rules (Ad Age; rules).
  • Biggest mistake: it borrowed the look of a social network but removed what makes one social: peer-to-peer contact, self-expression and ownership. It also seeded the site with actors presented as real teens. Teen testers asked "Are these real kids?" and called it "corny," and the trade press called it "phony in tone and style" (Ad Age; Bob Garfield, Ad Age).
  • The lesson: a time-boxed promotion dressed up as a community fails as both. If you want a community, let members talk to each other and own what they make, and never fake the members. If you want a campaign, call it one and run it where your audience already is (in 2006 Hitwise found more than 3% of Walmart.com's traffic already came from MySpace) (Forbes).

What the community is

The HUB was a Flash-and-PHP microsite, branded "Wal-Mart - The HUB (School Your Way)," at schoolyourway.walmart.com (the rules give the entry URL as www.walmart.com/SchoolYourWay). Registered users, called "Hubsters," could:

  • build a one-page profile: a headline, a short text, photos, a "My Song" pick from a preset menu, and up to 2 minutes of video. Items from Walmart's catalog were pre-inserted from a "style category" chosen at signup;
  • browse "Hot List" and "Hot Trends" fashion pages ("Looks for the Guys," "Girl's Fashion") and add items to a wishlist "for purchase now or later";
  • take a style quiz and a survey;
  • vote for other pages or videos once per day;
  • search Hubsters by page name.

Sources: the FAQ, the Hot List page and the rules, all archived (text excerpts saved in sources/walmart-the-hub/site-captures.md).

There was no messaging, commenting, friending or emailing between users. A Walmart spokeswoman told the Guardian that "the Hub was not intended as a social networking site and hubsters will not be able to communicate with fellow entrants by email," and called it "more of a brand positioning thing than a sales tool" (Guardian, Jul 20, 2006). So it was really a UGC contest gallery, not a community. The press, and Walmart's own MySpace-style framing ("express your individuality and see how other people express theirs"), made it read as a social network, and it has been remembered that way since (Mashable; Guardian, 2012; Livemint, 2014).

Who ran it: the sponsor was Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. The rules list the "Contest Entities" as Sony Pictures Entertainment, GSD&M (Walmart's Austin ad agency of record), Tribal DDB, Leroy & Morton, Skycastle Entertainment (whose copyright is in the page footer, suggesting it produced the site) and Sullivan Compliance Company (contest administration) (rules; Ad Age). No community manager, moderator team or Walmart owner was ever named publicly. Walmart "declined to comment" to Ad Age.

Unofficial spaces: none were found. No fan forum or subreddit existed (Reddit was a year old). The conversation about the HUB happened entirely among critics: Ad Age, Mashable, CNET, Slashdot, the Guardian, the Motley Fool and marketing bloggers.

Status: defunct since about Oct 2, 2006.

Origin and strategy

  • Business problem: teen apparel. Ad Age framed the site as "an attempt at closing the trend gap Wal-Mart now faces as Target wins more teen-apparel dollars." Youth-marketing consultant Irma Zandl said "Wal-Mart really needs this to work," citing "increasingly bad feedback from teen girls about Wal-Mart in contrast to Target — especially Wal-Mart's apparent lack of cleanliness, messy layout and lack of stylish attire" (Ad Age, Jul 2006). CNET read it the same way, as a bid "to win some cool quotient away from Target" (CNET, Jul 20, 2006).
  • Moment: summer 2006, at the peak of MySpace mania (News Corp had bought MySpace in 2005). Big brands were rushing to "social" and UGC. The same year Chevrolet's Tahoe ad-builder backfired, and Ad Age noted that Walmart reserved the right to edit the winning commercial, "obviously hoping to avoid the fate of Chevrolet's Tahoe."
  • Context inside Walmart: 2006 was the year of Walmart's internet "war room." Edelman ran its online PR, and the same months brought the "Wal-Marting Across America" fake blog (exposed in October 2006), paidcritics.com and the Wal-Mart Facts site (Guardian; ClickZ, Oct 19, 2006; Reason, Jul 17, 2006). The HUB was run by GSD&M, not Edelman. Still, reporters bundled it with those efforts as one more example of Walmart faking authenticity online.
  • Tie to the business model: acquisition and brand positioning for the back-to-school season, plus first-party data. Registration asked for first and last name, a parent's email, date of birth, full address and phone number. The wishlist turned browsing into a purchase list. Entrants assigned Walmart "ownership, copyright, and all other rights" in their pages and videos, giving the company a pool of free ad material (rules). Retention and LTV were never stated goals.
  • Champion: no individual executive was ever named. No evidence found.

Trajectory: spike, then nothing. Archive captures cluster in the launch week, when critics were poking around, and almost no new profiles appear after July. Profile IDs run from about 10,041,521 to 46,841,873, but they look like composite or random identifiers, not sequential counts, so they say nothing about how many people signed up (inference). Most reports say "10 weeks" (RetailWire; Network World). Ad Age says "less than three months." Counting from the Jul 10 contest start, it was about 12 weeks. ClickZ's "launched this August" is wrong.

Was it "killed," or did it just end? Forbes conceded "it's certainly possible that Wal-Mart always planned on shutting down the site after the back-to-school season". The rules did tie everything to an Aug 5 deadline and an Oct 6 cutoff for winners-list requests. As a contest, it ran its course. As a community, the coordinator's "failure" call holds: nothing was built to outlive the contest, the attempt was ridiculed, and the Motley Fool faulted Walmart both for the launch and for a "boneheaded" shutdown that locked out registrants and dumped them on the generic Walmart.com home page (Motley Fool, Oct 5, 2006).

What keeps people coming back

Almost nothing. Each of the usual mechanisms, in turn:

  • Rituals and cadence: none. It was a single 4-week entry window (Jul 10–Aug 5) followed by judging.
  • Progress and status: a daily vote ("You may only vote for a specific page or video once per day") was the only recurring action, so the only reason to come back was to vote for yourself or a friend. Winners were picked by judges on Style, Originality, Personality, Relevance to School Your Way, and Composition. Votes counted toward a pool of "semi-finalists," so status depended on the judges, not on peers (participate page).
  • Identity and belonging: a coined tribe name ("Hubsters," Wal-Mart's ideal of a hipster per Ad Age's photo caption) and a tagline. Critics mocked both as "painfully uncool."
  • Recognition: prizes, plus the promise of seeing your video "made into a TV ad," aired on cable and in Regal Cinemas for the grand prize (prizes). A real hook, but a one-time one.
  • Peer support and accountability: impossible. There were no messages, comments or email, by design.
  • Parasocial ties: the only "people" with personality were the four actor-teens, and audiences decoded them as fake almost immediately.
  • In-product hooks: the wishlist and the style quiz, both of which fed Walmart's commerce, not member-to-member ties.
  • Switching costs: zero. Profiles were locked once submitted ("Once submitted, you cannot change your Video or Page"), they could not be moved to MySpace, and Walmart owned them.
  • Friction against returning: every piece of content sat "pending approval," and Ad Age saw "'pending approval' notes dominate pages already created." A minor's page could vanish if a parent objected ("WHY IS MY PAGE MISSING? Your parent or guardian may not have given their consent"). The site required Flash 8 and JavaScript.

Retention-mechanics score: 1/5. The only loop was daily voting, and the design actively prevented social ties.

The content engine

  • Company-made content: four scripted "teen" video vignettes on the home page, played by actors "positioned to appear authentic" (Ad Age), plus seeded example pages like "Beth's Backyard Club" ("I'll school my way by looking hot in my Wal-Mart clothes to school to catch a cute boy's eye...."). Ashley's "Tap into Fashion" page ("Shopping will be my number ONE hobby this fall") was quoted by CNET and Garfield as the site's voice. The production cost was visible: Garfield noted the actors were "filmed at God-knows-what-expense, with God-knows-how-many grownups milling around the set."
  • Commerce content: "Hot List" lookbooks ("Get This Look!": Olive Green Mash Tee, Rugged Vintage Jeans, Light Camo Back Pack) with add-to-wishlist forms (archived page).
  • Music: user-uploaded music was banned unless original. Every page instead picked "My Song" from a short preset list (archived pages show Chris Brown's "Yo (Excuse Me Miss)," Cheyenne Kimball, Martina McBride, Miranda Lambert, Matisyahu, Matt Kearney).
  • Member-made content: a profile page and an optional 2-minute video, guided by templates, "samples," and Walmart merchandise pre-inserted into the page. Rules banned trademarks, logos, copyrighted music, last names and any alcohol, tobacco, drug or firearm reference. Walmart-branded apparel was explicitly allowed. Archived member pages are mostly one or two lines, such as "CheerChick03: i luv 2 cheer!", a wrestler's "Make Life a Constant Party," or "School In Space... floating pencils and all!" Some read as genuine teens. Others, such as the near-identical "DJ STINA ROCKS!" and "XTINA Rockin' to the Beat!" pages praising "hip clothes from Wal-Mart," read like seeded copy (inference from the archive).
  • Rewards: Sony VAIO laptops, HD Handycams, $500 Sony Style certificates, an LA trip with a studio tour, and a TV spot.
  • Cadence: none after launch. No newsletters, events, or new content drops were found.

Content-engine score: 2/5. It had real production budget and prizes, but the content was inauthentic, front-loaded and one-off.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff and roles: an agency roster (GSD&M, Tribal DDB, Leroy & Morton, Skycastle, Sullivan Compliance) plus Sony, with no named community staff. Moderation was pre-publication review of everything. The rules also gave parents an opt-out email, with silence by Aug 27 counting as consent. That differed from how the press described it ("need their parents' consent"): in practice it was a negative-option notice.
  • Ambassadors or superusers: none, apart from the paid actor-teens.
  • Rules: a long prohibited-content list. Commentators pointed out that the list made authentic teen expression, let alone a critical take on Walmart, impossible: "a subversive, ironic ad by a savvy teen on how her dad's hardware shop closed down after the retail goliath rolled into town would likely be 'otherwise objectionable'" (Ad Age).
  • Feedback into the product: no evidence. The site launched to immediate ridicule and nothing was changed during its run.

Staff-investment score: 2/5. A lot of agency money went into production and compliance, but none into people who would host or nurture a community.

Growth loops

  • Designed loops: prizes as the acquisition magnet, daily voting (which pushes entrants to recruit friends to vote), and the promise of a TV ad. No referral, sharing or embed tools were documented.
  • What actually spread: criticism. Slashdot ("Walmart Tries to Emulate MySpace," Jul 19, 2006), Mashable ("WalMart Clones MySpace, Badly"), CNET, the Guardian, the NYT's online column ("appears to be essentially an advertising vehicle that encourages teenagers to create commercials for the retail chain"), Seth Godin and others (Slashdot; NYT, Jul 22, 2006). The site's lasting reach is as a case study in marketing textbooks and listicles, still taught years later (Learning from Big Boys, 2008; Quizlet MKTG course set).
  • The missed loop: Forbes and Mashable both pointed out that Walmart already had a MySpace profile and real MySpace referral traffic (>3% of Walmart.com visits). Their advice was to sponsor promotions, layouts and widgets there rather than build a walled copy.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. No sign-ups, entries, traffic, wishlist conversions, sales lift or cost were ever published. None of the sources found here tie it to Walmart's filings or earnings calls. SEC filings were not searched, because a promotion this small would not be material.
  • Context data: Hitwise: more than 3% of Walmart.com traffic came from MySpace in September 2006 (via Forbes). Mintel estimated teens spent $153B in 2006 (via Learning from Big Boys).
  • Reputational impact: ClickZ bundled the HUB with the Edelman fake blog and the "ToyLand" nag-your-parents site as a run of October 2006 "online flubs" (ClickZ). CIO magazine cited it in "How Wal-Mart Lost Its Technology Edge" (Oct 2007) (CIO). A dissenting view: Goodby Silverstein's Hashem Bajwa thought "these will just be things that pass very quickly" (ClickZ).

Business-impact-evidence score: 1/5.

Durability score: 1/5. It was built to last one season and lasted one season.

What they did right

  1. Recognized the teen-apparel problem and tried a new channel quickly. Zandl called the "'we media' attempt terrific" in principle, and the Fool's Rick Munarriz later said he faulted Walmart for giving up too fast, not for trying (Fool, Oct 17, 2006).
  2. Took child safety seriously. The age gate, the personal-information bans, pre-moderation and parental notices anticipated the safety panic around MySpace. Seth Godin argued that "some people want a clean, well-lit, orderly environment, even online" (Seth's Blog, Jul 2006), and Mashable conceded that "there's nothing wrong with trying to build a safer social network" (citing Piczo and Multiply).
  3. Offered aspirational, creator-flavored rewards. A real TV spot and a meeting with an industry professional are a stronger UGC incentive than a coupon.
  4. Protected the brand from a Chevy-Tahoe-style hijack. It worked: no anti-Walmart UGC ever surfaced. But the cost was the whole point of the site.

What they did wrong

  1. Fake members. Actors scripted as real teens ("positioned to appear authentic") on the front page, plus pages that read like ad copy. Consequence: the first teens Ad Age showed it to asked "Are these real kids?", and "fake kids" became the story (Techdirt via CIO). Because it ran at the same time as Walmart's Edelman astroturfing scandals, it made Walmart's credibility problem worse.
  2. A social network with the social removed. There was no member-to-member contact at all. Garfield: it "reneges on its implicit promise to be a Hub for networking." Tim Stock (Scenario DNA): "Teens aren't searching for what a company deems relevant, but what they deem relevant… You can't own it."
  3. Over-control. Everything was pre-approved, a parent was notified for every registrant, and logos, trademarks and copyrighted music were banned, but Walmart apparel was allowed. "Think MySpace stripped of content, striped with pending-approval notices…" (Melanie Colburn via the Guardian).
  4. Members' expression was really ad copy. The rules asked entrants "to consider how Wal-Mart helps support their personal style," and took all rights to what they made. Members could see that their pages were the product.
  5. Coined-cool branding. "Hubsters," giant pencils, "School Your Way." Mashable: "marketing executives trying to get down with the kidz."
  6. The wrong host for the audience. A Walmart.com subdomain for teens who shopped elsewhere ("goes to Wal-Mart only when her mom does," Ad Age). The Fool suggested a standalone site "with very loose corporate affiliations."
  7. A careless shutdown. Registrants were cut off without notice and redirected to the Walmart.com home page (Forbes, Fool) or, per ClickZ, to the Gillette-sponsored Soundcheck page. No archive or export was offered, and no winners showcase was found. By 2009 the same hostname was serving an unrelated GameStop promo (Wayback).
  8. No learning carried forward. Walmart's next teen and student play, the "Roommate Style Match" Facebook group (Aug 2007), was quickly overrun by anti-Walmart posts (CIO). Walmart's more credible later efforts (Elevenmoms, 2008) relied on real, independent bloggers (Social CRM, 2009).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Let members reach each other. The minimum viable community is member-to-member contact (comments, replies, follows). Safety can come from moderation and reporting, not from removing the social layer.
  2. Seed with real people, disclosed. Recruit genuine early users, or clearly labeled staff and creators. Never present actors or employees as peers.
  3. Give creators ownership and a stage. Real-world showcases (a TV spot, a featured slot) are strong incentives. Pair them with members keeping rights and attribution, and with a showcase that outlives the contest.
  4. Go where the audience already is. If a big share of your traffic already comes from a platform (MySpace in 2006, TikTok or Discord today), feed a presence there before building a walled copy.
  5. Name the format honestly. If it is a seasonal campaign, run it as one, with a clear end and an archive or winners page. Don't sell it as a "hub" people will live in.

Avoid these

  1. Astroturf. Fake members are discovered almost immediately, and the discovery becomes the story, compounding any existing trust deficit.
  2. Control that makes self-expression impossible. Pre-approving everything, banning everything except your own logo, and requiring members to say how your product expresses them turns community members into unpaid copywriters.
  3. Ending without warning or an off-ramp. Cutting off registrants and redirecting them to a generic home page burns whatever goodwill the effort earned.
