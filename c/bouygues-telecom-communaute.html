Peak

Self-reported by community manager Emmanuel Gay (undated, c. mid-2010s): ~30 core volunteer members, 5,000 responses a month, 300,000 customer-service contacts avoided a month, 1.4M monthly unique visitors, 85% of traffic from search engines (LinkedIn, via search snippets)

Now

Official forum closed to new posts around March 2025 and to reading by April 2025; /s/forum redirects to the help home and old threads redirect to a customer login (checked 2026-11-07). Peer help survives only on unofficial sites, e.g. Bbox-Mag forum: 2,616 members, 28,970 posts (Nov 2026)

How it pays off for the company

Support deflection and SEO: certified answers fed the FAQ, the forum drew search traffic, and Bouygues' own community lead claimed 300,000 avoided contacts a month. It also served as a recruiting pool for the Comité-Clients and as a soft sales channel (advisers pitched upgrades in threads). Bouygues replaced it with FAQ, Assistant Bbox diagnostics, the Beety chatbot and AI tools.

TL;DR

  • What it is: Bouygues Telecom's official customer help forum. It started in September 2008 as an "entraide" Q&A built by the agency Human to Human, was relaunched in January 2012 as the Forum Woobees on Dimelo, later became the plain Forum d'assistance, moved to Salesforce around 2021-22, and was closed in March-April 2025. The whole archive went with it [1][7][31][45].
  • Why people came back: to fix a problem. A small core of 10 to 30 "Top Contributeurs" stayed for points and levels, "certified answer" stamps from staff, contests with smartphone prizes, and a route into the company's Comité-Clients customer panel [10][11][14][32].
  • What the company did to feed it: paid advisers certified every good answer, and those answers fed the FAQ [1][3]. Employees from every department ("Woobees") answered questions and made how-to videos [6][8]. The company ran a 75-person social-care team (2015) and took the panel's members behind the scenes and in front of top management [15][14].
  • Biggest mistake: the ending. Bouygues closed the forum with no public notice and took down 16 years of solved threads, even for reading. Long-time customers said the forum was a reason they had stayed with Bouygues [31].
  • The lesson: a staff-certified peer-support forum is a cheap, durable asset (its community lead claimed 300,000 contacts avoided a month). Treat the archive as a permanent asset, and give volunteers a path to real influence, such as panel seats and access to executives, not just badges [32][14].

What the community is

Bouygues Telecom is France's third or fourth operator (14 million customers in the mid-2010s) [38]. Its official community was always support first. Customers asked questions about mobile, Bbox (home internet and TV) and billing. Other customers answered, and Bouygues customer advisers "certified" the right answer or completed it [1][3]. It went through four phases:

Plate-forme d'entraide Sep 2008 to Jan 2012 entraide.bouyguestelecom.fr, built by Human to Human Mobile internet first, Bbox ADSL from 22 Oct 2008; votes and a member ranking [1][2]
Forum Woobees Jan 2012 to c. 2015 forum.bouyguestelecom.fr on Dimelo Paired with "Woobees" staff experts on Facebook, YouTube and @woobees [7][8][9]
Forum d'assistance Bouygues Telecom c. 2015 to c. 2021 same URL, Dimelo/RingCentral Woobees brand dropped (@woobees "moved" to @bouyguestelecom on 30 Sep 2015) [17][24]
Salesforce forum c. 2021-22 to Mar-Apr 2025 assistance.bouyguestelecom.fr/s/forum Part of the Salesforce-based help site; closed in 2025 [30][31]

Around the forum Bouygues built several adjacent programs:

  • the Woobees, volunteer employee experts (Dec 2011) [6];
  • B&You (July 2011), a low-cost, online-only brand whose support ran on peer help [18][19][20];
  • the Comité-Clients panel (Dec 2014 onward) [14];
  • a Howtank peer-chat test (Nov 2017) [22];
  • Bouygues Telecom Friends, paid local helpers recruited through Mila (Jul 2017) [21].

Unofficial spaces matter as well. Bouygues engineers personally answered on lafibre.info around 2010-12 [5][13]. Bbox-Mag's forum remains the main fan-run help space today [35]. Reddit hardly matters: r/Bouygues has 1 subscriber and 18 posts [42].

Origin and strategy

The 2008 launch was openly about deflection plus brand image. Jean-Luc Gonzalez, Bouygues Telecom's internet director, said nearly half of consumers now had a "web reflex" and searched online before calling support. The forum was meant to "complete" the help centre with an interactive Q&A. Certified Q&As would "then enrich the FAQ", and the space would also guide prospects who were still choosing an operator [1][3]. He called exchanges between internet users "strategic for us" and said they fit "the image of proximity of the brand" [3]. At the time Bouygues was marketing itself as "n°1 of customer relationship in mobile" [1].

The 2012 Woobees relaunch came out of the social-care team, which ran a two-month pilot of community managers and advisers in October 2010. That team found that community managers were pinging in-house experts every day. The fix was to let those experts (app, network, box, legal, marketing) answer the public directly [6][15]. Emmanuel Gay, head of "community customer relations", owned the program. He describes building the promise "At Bouygues Telecom, there is always a community of enthusiasts to help me" [32][25]. His former manager credits him with recruiting and running communities, the relationship charter, adviser training, tools (Viavoo, Dimelo, Netino moderation), answer libraries, the assistance forums and the customer committee [33].

The strategic context explains why the forum mattered and then stopped mattering. From 2012 Bouygues' turnaround (1,500 plans cut to 14, "growth first, client first") focused on cutting churn rather than buying customers, built on a Salesforce 360° customer view [38]. By 2024-26 Bouygues was investing in AI instead: a Google-powered sales assistant (Sept 2024), the Beety chatbot, the Assistant Bbox diagnostics, and the agent-assist tool "Iris", used daily by 6,000 reps and built on a knowledge base of 500 articles [37]. The forum was closed while that shift was under way [31].

What keeps people coming back

  • Need-driven visits, by design. Most traffic was one-off: people searching a problem and landing on a solved thread. 85% of traffic came from search engines [32]. This was a help desk that let regulars build status, not a hangout.
  • Points, levels and visible rank. Every profile showed a level and a points bar (for example "Niveau 2, 837 / 2000") [12]. The 2013 leaders were at levels 5 and 6 with 25,000 to 41,000 points [11]. By 2018-21 regulars and staff both appeared as "Niveau 4, 5000 / 5000", so the visible ladder looks flattened or capped after the redesign (inferred from page captures) [24][28].
  • Certification stamps. Threads carried labels: "Réponse expert certifiée", "Réponse utilisateur certifiée" (staff endorsing a customer's answer) and "Réponse conseiller approuvée", plus "Question star" for featured questions [12]. That was public recognition from the company itself.
  • Contests. The Challenge du Forum Woobees (28 Nov 2012 to 28 Jan 2013) gave 10 Samsung Galaxy SIII phones (€649.90 each) to the members with the most "best answer" or "certified answer" votes [10][11].
  • Status that turns into influence. The "Top Contributeur" title appeared in members' signatures, for example "Kev, Client Bouygues, Top Contributeur et Membre de la Communauté Bouygues sur Howtank" [23]. It led to real seats: five of the 11 founding Comité-Clients members (2015) were "Tops Contributeurs" chosen by participation score. The committee meets experts and the executive committee and votes on which Fondation Bouygues Telecom charity projects get support [14][26].
  • Named, human staff. Advisers signed with first names (Anaïs, Manu, Cédric, Murielle on the forum; Céline and Claude on @woobees), and volunteers had ongoing relationships with them [12][24][28][17]. Bbox-Mag's farewell thanked the advisers "who followed and accompanied the top contributors to certify the best answers" [31].
  • IRL and insider access. Comité-Clients members were invited inside the company [14][26]. Bouygues also hosted 200+ customers of 10+ years at six customer-relations centres one Saturday, 14 October (year not stated) [39].
  • Switching cost. For some members the forum was itself a retention reason: "c'est un des motifs (et non des moindres) qui m'ont fait rejoindre et revenir chez Bouygues" (it was one of the reasons, and not the least, that I joined and came back to Bouygues) [31].

The content engine

  • The forum as the FAQ's supply chain. From day one, solved and certified Q&As were meant to be turned into FAQ entries [1][2]. In effect the community acted as an R&D lab for the knowledge base. Today that knowledge base (500+ articles) powers Iris and the chatbots [37].
  • Woobees how-to videos. At the Dec 2011 launch, employee experts posted "dozens of videos" on YouTube answering frequent questions, such as setting up email on a smartphone [6][7]. The official YouTube channel now presents itself as "Tutos & Assistance", with step-by-step videos.
  • Staff-posted alerts and pinned threads. Examples include "[Alerte phishing en cours] Soyez vigilants !" and "Ne communiquez jamais le mot de passe de votre compte-client" [24]. Staff also posted resolution notices for outages, such as the F3411 TV outage in Nov 2020 [29].
  • Blog and Mag. The Comité-Clients blog (2014) became Le Mag de Bouygues Telecom, which now publishes marketing, safety and parenting content, not community content [14][39].
  • Member-made content. Members' long diagnostic threads were the main member-made content. No member wiki, templates program or creator program was found ("no evidence found"). That contrasts with Orange's TopMembre-written "Trucs et astuces" wiki.

People, programs and governance

  • Dedicated certifying advisers from launch [1][3]. By 2015 the social team had 75 people, with a rule that community managers handle general questions and switch to a trained customer adviser as soon as the customer's file is needed [15][16]. Bouygues used Dimelo/RingCentral Engage from 2011 to route forum, Facebook and Twitter requests, with up to 3 conversations per adviser at once and a 1-hour response target on social media [25].
  • Woobees: volunteer employees "from all of Bouygues Telecom's trades (technical, marketing, sales, network)" [8]. This is an internal expert program, not a customer MVP program.
  • Top Contributeurs: the customer superuser tier (about 30 active, per Gay [32]). Perks: points and level, certified stamps, contests, Comité-Clients eligibility. No published charter, NDA or insider-preview program was found ("no evidence found").
  • Comité-Clients: launched Dec 2014 with 11 members (5 Top Contributeurs plus 6 drawn by lot from 5 regions, including one business customer) on one-year terms, renewable once [14]. In 2019 it had 20 members chosen from 250+ applicants, balanced by gender, age, region and tenure [26]. It was renewed in May 2022 with roughly 30 customers of 10+ years' tenure, and Bouygues calls it "unique in the telecom sector" [27]. Feedback also flowed through a "relais qualité" that sorted advisers' improvement ideas and sent them to the relevant departments [16].
  • Moderation: outsourced to Netino [33]. Top contributors also policed tone, sometimes harshly: in a March 2020 thread a top contributor called a critic "le troll" and told him his account had been reported as spam [28].
  • Paid peer help (Mila Friends): a different model. Vetted enthusiasts who passed a test did paid home visits for Bouygues customers. It started in Nice (Jul 2017), reached 10 cities in Oct 2017 and 26 cities as "Mila Friends" later [21][46].

Growth loops

  • SEO. This was the main loop. Search share of traffic rose from 60% to 85% and uniques from 800k to 1.4M a month (self-reported) [32]. Prospects were an explicit audience from 2008 [1].
  • Prospect conversion inside threads. Staff answers sometimes doubled as sales pitches. In the COVID thread, an adviser offered a B&You customer extra data and then "Bonne nouvelle ! … vous êtes éligible à la bbox fibre … Intéressé ?" [28]. An adviser also pointed a member to the fibre referral (parrainage) offer [24].
  • B&You's community-first model used peer support to keep the low-cost brand cheap to run [18][20].
  • No referral program tied to forum status was found ("no evidence found").

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none published. Bouygues' annual reports and the Salesforce material found here say nothing about forum deflection. No academic study was found.
  • Insider claim (strongest available): the community manager claims "300,000 monthly contacts avoided to customer service" from about 5,000 monthly answers [32]. For scale, the 2015 Bordeaux centre alone took about a million calls a month [15]. If true, the forum absorbed a meaningful share of contact volume. This claim is undated and unaudited.
  • Proxy: Bouygues kept the forum staffed for about 16 years and built a governance body on its volunteers. Its replacement shows the same underlying goal, deflection: FAQ plus AI that "outperforms a supervisor" [37].
  • Score justifications:
    • retention_mechanics 3: a solid gamified Q&A with real status rewards, but the community existed only around solving problems.
    • content_engine 3: forum-to-FAQ pipeline and Woobees videos, but almost no member-authored content.

What they did right

  1. Company-certified peer answers (2008, early for France). Customers answered, advisers certified, and certified answers became FAQ entries [1][3]. This gave both speed and accuracy, and its "certified by Bouygues" labels later became standard in French telecom forums (SFR's community uses "Réponse certifiée par SFR").
  2. Pulling in-house experts into public support (Woobees). Instead of community managers relaying questions, network, app and legal experts answered and made videos themselves [6][8]. Customers on unofficial forums rated this kind of access highly: "je suis admiratif du support que tu apportes … Ce serait tellement génial si tous les FAI pouvaient avoir des employés comme toi" (I admire the support you give … it would be great if every ISP had employees like you) [5].
  3. A real status ladder, up to governance. Top Contributeurs could earn seats on the Comité-Clients, with access to the executive committee [14]. That reward cost little and was not cash.
  4. Clear lane separation. Community managers took general questions and switched to customer advisers once the customer's file was needed. Private follow-up went to verified email [15][28].
  5. Search as a growth engine. By the community lead's account, the forum became the main search-traffic magnet for support questions [32].

What they did wrong

  1. They deleted the archive. In 2025 the forum closed to new questions and to reading. A Bbox-Mag member wrote on 20 Apr 2025: "C'est non seulement fermé pour les nouveaux topics mais également pour la consultation de l'historique! … Serait ce une forme de censure? Encore un service en moins alors que les tarifs ne cessent d'augmenter." (It's closed not only to new topics but to reading the history! … Is this a form of censorship? Yet another service gone while prices keep rising.) [31]. Old thread URLs now go to a customer login page [45]. That loses the SEO value, the knowledge and goodwill. A Bbox-Mag regular with inside contact says content was not the reason for closing [31].
  2. They gave no public send-off. The only farewell found was written by a fan site's founder, who thanked the top contributors and advisers. No Bouygues announcement was found [31].
  3. They churned brands and platforms. Entraide became Woobees, then Forum d'assistance, then Salesforce, with tool changes in 2012, c. 2015 and c. 2021-22 [7][17][30]. Each move reset the brand, and the late migration preceded the shutdown. Early tooling was fragile too: in 2011 a customer could not finish registering on the entraide platform for weeks [5].
  4. The status ladder lost its top. Leaders sat at levels 5 and 6 (out of 40,000 to 75,000 points) in 2013, but by 2018-21 everyone senior showed "Niveau 4, 5000 / 5000". It looks as if the ladder was compressed, which removes long-run goals for veterans (inferred from captures) [11][24][28].
  5. Volunteers were left to absorb anger. During the 2020 price-rise waves, top contributors defended Bouygues and traded insults with angry customers. One member wrote "les top contributeurs me semblent être de sacrés bip" (the top contributors strike me as real bleeps) [28]. When volunteers act as unofficial company defenders, they hurt the brand.
  6. Support and sales got mixed. Advisers tacked upsells onto complaint threads [28]. On the Comité-Clients blog a commenter asked whether its members were "des cousins du PDG ou des salariés de bouygues ?" (cousins of the CEO or Bouygues employees?) [40].
  7. They experimented without follow-through. Howtank chat (2017), Mila Friends (2017-18) and B&You's "débrouillez-vous entre vous" (sort it out among yourselves) model [20][21][22] drew customers away from the forum. No outcome data was found for any of them.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Certify, then harvest. Let users answer, have staff mark the correct answer publicly, and pipe certified answers into your docs. The community becomes your knowledge base's R&D lab [1][3].
  2. Put real experts in the room under their first names. Volunteer employee experts (the Woobees model) and named advisers build trust that anonymous support accounts don't [6][12].
  3. Turn top-helper status into influence. Reserve seats on a customer advisory panel for top contributors, with meetings with executives. It costs little and means far more than badges [14][27].
  4. Time-boxed contests tied to quality. Bouygues rewarded "best" and "certified" answers rather than raw volume, with a clear set of rules [10].
  5. Design for search from day one. Solved threads with clean URLs were the main growth and deflection engine [32].

Avoid these

  1. Never delete the archive when you shut down. Freeze it read-only, keep the URLs, and thank the volunteers publicly. Otherwise you lose SEO, knowledge and your most loyal customers' goodwill in one move [31][45].
  2. Don't make volunteers your defence force. Train and protect them, and have staff handle policy or pricing anger. Volunteers fighting critics damages the brand [28].
  3. Don't rebrand or replatform repeatedly, or mix upsells into support threads. Each migration and each pitch inside a complaint costs trust and continuity [7][28][30].
