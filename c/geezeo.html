TL;DR

  • What it was. Geezeo (2007 to 2009) was a free social personal-finance site from two Massachusetts founders, Peter Glyman and Shawn Ward. It combined CashEdge account aggregation with user-created Groups, shared Goals that updated themselves from linked accounts, an anonymous Money Confessions board piped to Twitter, and from 2008 Expert Groups run by personal-finance authors. It reached about 20,000 users by March 2008 and "more than 100,000" by late 2009 (WaPo; CU Times).
  • Why people came back. Low-effort, emotionally honest participation (a one-line confession, anonymous if you liked), goals that tracked progress automatically, SMS balance checks, and repeated sweepstakes that gave extra entries for coming back ("Keep coming back to Geezeo to gain entries to The Great Geezeo Bailout!", blog, Feb 2009).
  • What the company did to feed it. A near-daily irreverent blog (a puppy mascot interviewing students, a fake advice columnist named "D. Expert"), a paid content strategy director, campus "Brand Executives," a "Rock Star" power-user group, named experts with their own groups, and syndicated content from investor TheStreet.com.
  • The biggest mistake. The community never got dense. In February 2008, the biggest of about 80 groups had 111 members and most had 1 to 15 (groups page). A reviewer called the groups "spread too thin." The goals and transaction tools were also buggy, and when TheStreet passed on buying the company in April 2009, Geezeo stopped feeding the community, closed registrations, and let the blog fill with spam.
  • The one lesson. Geezeo's users told it that 90% "would rather get it within their own financial institution" (American Banker, Mar 2009). The community's lasting value was as market research and a proof point for selling white-label software to 300+ banks and credit unions, not as a destination in itself.

What the community is

Geezeo began as DebtFolio, a credit-card debt analyzer launched in alpha in October 2006, and was rebuilt as "a more comprehensive personal finance manager not limited to debt" in early 2007 (blog, Jan 31 2007). The site went live in summer 2007 (American Banker says July; Today.com says "end of May") (AB, Nov 2007; Today.com, Aug 2007). Its owned community spaces were:

There was no significant unofficial community. The only Hacker News mentions are passing comparisons with Mint and Wesabe, and the Arctic Shift Reddit archive found no Geezeo threads (searched Nov 2026).

Origin and strategy

The founders pitched social finance as behavior change for the "plastic generation" of under-35s who had abandoned checkbooks for debit cards and become "detached from their finances" (Glyman, WaPo, Mar 2008; Hartford Business, Feb 2009). Glyman likened the site to Weight Watchers or Jenny Craig (NY Daily News, Mar 2008), and Geezeo's own homepage quoted the New York Times line that such sites "act like Weight Watchers for money" (homepage, Feb 2009). Ward framed the community as emotional, not advisory: "People don't like to talk about money, especially when they have money problems... it's a way to not feel alone. Not a whole lot of it is advice. It's more about experiences" (WaPo p.2).

The business model tied the community to revenue: members would rate financial products, and Geezeo would earn referral fees when peers opened accounts based on those ratings ("The twist on it is we'll have a Geezeo user recommendation rating", AB, Nov 2007). Ads arrived in May 2008, framed to members as "Those little ads keep Geezeo free for you to use" (blog, May 7 2008). Funding was about $1M from friends, family and one angel (Hartford Business, Feb 2009), then TheStreet.com: $1.2M for about 13% in April 2008 with an option to buy the whole company at a $12M equity value, plus $650K more in October 2008 for 18.5% (TheStreet 10-Q, May 2009). The community's internal champions were the two founders, content strategy director Katie McCaskey, and early engineer Theron Parlin.

The pivot came in three steps. In January 2009 Geezeo shut its referral service because recommending rival banks "conflicts with its new business model." In February 2009 it launched white-label PFM for banks and expected 80% of 2009 revenue from financial institutions (AB, Mar 2009). When TheStreet let its option lapse in April 2009, Geezeo went all in on banks, and by December 2009 it was "no longer accepting new registrations to its Web site" (Hartford Business, Dec 2009).

Two caveats. First, the January 2009 traffic spike came while TheStreet.com was cross-promoting Geezeo across its network and Geezeo was running cash sweepstakes, so it measures marketing reach more than community health (estimate; Compete panels were noisy below six figures, as Finovate itself noted). What keeps people coming back

  • Low-friction confession as a ritual. Money Confessions asked for one line, public or anonymous, from a box on the dashboard. Real examples ranged from "whoops, i overpaid my credit card" to "I'm 30k in debt brought on by my depression and anxiety... my husband has no idea" (WaPo). In 2009 the blog ran a Sunday "Geezeo Confessions and Conversations" roundup that reflected the recession back to members ("My wife just lost her job, 60% of our monthly income gone overnight", Feb 1 2009). This was the feature the press remembered.
  • Self-updating goals. Because accounts refreshed nightly, goal progress moved without any input from the member, and others could join the same goal. Shared goal pages were thin, though: a reviewer found only two participants on one of the biggest goals, and found that joining sometimes failed to register (PFSR review, 2008).
  • Mobile check-ins. SMS balance checks from day one, and from December 2007 "text 'Geezeo Clothes' to 41411" to see the remaining budget in a category (blog). This was a daily-utility hook, not a social one.
  • Identity niches. Groups were organized by age, art, college, city and life stage ("Quarter Life Crisis," "Starving Musicians," "Boston on a Budget"), and Geezeo's staff and campus ambassadors seeded college groups.
  • Experts as a draw. From October 2008 the Expert Groups gave members a named person to ask, such as Stockpickr founder James Altucher ("ready to answer your questions on everything from stocks, to personal finance, to entrepreneurship", blog) and Miami Herald "Frugalista" Natalie McNeal (blog).
  • Extrinsic return incentives. These included a $500 gas contest (summer 2008), "Win 3 months of mortgage, $1,000 in investment money" (homepage, early 2009) and The Great Geezeo Bailout ($6,000 cash plus TradeMonster, Lending Club and Identity Guard prizes, entered by registering or logging in, March 2009) (blog).
  • Switching costs. Mostly data, not relationships: linked accounts, tags and budgets. No evidence was found of streaks, badges, levels or leaderboards.

The content engine

  • A daily, deliberately irreverent blog (blog.geezeo.com, Nov 2006 to Jun 2009; hundreds of posts, judging by the Wayback index). Tone examples: "Ten reasons making a budget is like losing your virginity," "How much does an a**hole cost," "My first whorehouse." Recurring formats included Booboo's Student of the Week (the founders' puppy "interviews" a New England college student about debt, Mar 2007); D. Expert, a satirical boorish advice columnist who solicited readers' worst money confessions (Dec 2007); interviews with personal-finance bloggers (Frugal Zeitgeist, Madame X, DebtKid); "Meet Geezeo User" profiles; and "Group Chatter" posts that highlighted live threads by username (Dec 2008).
  • A paid content lead. Katie McCaskey's profile listed "Content Strategy Director, Geezeo; Hell Raiser" and her own goals and confession, so staff took part as members (profile).
  • Syndication after TheStreet. From mid-2008 the blog carried TheStreet and Jim Cramer posts ("Cramer: Buying Unilever Off Mistaken Media") and a "Money 101" section, with TheStreet's network links in the header. This added volume but diluted the voice that had made the blog distinctive.
  • Press as content. Geezeo landed the WSJ, CNN's Open House, the Washington Post, Today.com, the NY Daily News and Marie Claire (which singled out the "20 something, money nothing" group as "particularly active"), and recycled the quotes on its homepage.
  • Member-made content was the groups, goals, confessions and product ratings. Members got banners to put on their own blogs and Facebook pages (May 2008), but no evidence was found of rewards beyond blog shout-outs.

People, programs and governance

  • Brand Executives / Geezeo Agents (2007). A recruited student ambassador program, "represented primarily around New England" with "plans to branch out nationally." Members came from Boston University, Babson, UW-Madison and elsewhere, did campus promotion, and were welcomed by name on the blog, including a "Brand Executive of the Month" (May 2007; Sep 2007). This was closer to a campus marketing internship than a superuser program.
  • Geezeo Rock Stars (Aug 2007). A call for "a strongly opinionated group of individuals that are passionate about their finances" to become a power-user group shaping the product ("Your emails are personally read by the team", blog). No evidence was found of how big it got or what it did.
  • Feedback loop. The official "Geezeo Feature Requests" group (70 members by Feb 2008), a Get Satisfaction group, and an open beta of the July 2008 redesign that founders answered in the comments (blog).
  • Brands in the community. Zecco ran an official group (21 members), and Geezeo invited brokers to "join in on discussions with customers" around their ratings (CNET). This brought commercial voices into a peer space.
  • Team size. Small: seven employees in December 2009, doubling to about 14 after the Alliant deal (Hartford Business). No moderation policy was found beyond the rule that you had to hold a product to rate it.

Growth loops

  • Press. The confession and Weight Watchers angles were reliably quotable, and consumer press in 2007 and 2008 kept presenting Geezeo as one of the "big three" social PFMs alongside Mint and Wesabe.
  • Campus ambassadors and college groups to reach the target demographic.
  • Twitter syndication of confessions (@moneyconfession, 779 followers per X), plus a Facebook group and member banners.
  • TheStreet network distribution from April 2008, which probably drove the 12x traffic growth (estimate; no attribution data published).
  • Sweepstakes that required registering or logging in.
  • SEO from public group and goal pages (Wayback captured hundreds of /goals/show/ URLs), but no traffic data were found.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence. TheStreet.com invested $1.85M plus about $0.2M in legal fees, declined the $12M buyout option, and wrote the stake down by about $1.5M in Q1 2009 (10-Q). The consumer community did not produce a fundable standalone business.
  • Company claims. Ward said 90% of users wanted these tools from their own bank, and Glyman said the consumer model "could have succeeded" but bank software was better (AB, Mar 2009). The first bank-era customers bought the social layer explicitly: Alliant CU said Geezeo's "PFM capabilities and collaborative community aspect enhance the overall member experience, increase member retention and help us attract a younger demographic" (CU Times, Dec 2009). Geezeo then sold a white-label "Community Answers Platform" from 2010 to 2012 ("Now you can communicate with your audience and not talk at them", product page, Apr 2010). No evidence was found that any bank's community got traction, and by 2016 the product list no longer included community.
  • Company outcome. 155 PFM clients by December 2012, more than 300 by May 2014, a Jack Henry partnership in 2017, and acquisition by Jack Henry in July 2019 (Geezeo history page, 2019; Jack Henry). The price was not disclosed. In the bank era, CMO Bryan Clagett reported "about a seven percent user adoption rate at its client banks" (AB, 2012), the same engagement problem the consumer community had faced.

What they did right

  1. Found an emotionally true, low-effort contribution format. Confessions needed one sentence and allowed anonymity, which lowered the bar for a taboo topic. They produced the press stories (WaPo) and a member who later dropped anonymity ("used to post as 'anonymous' on Geezeo but now goes by 'Liam'").
  2. Wired progress to real data. Goals that update from linked accounts are a better retention primitive than self-reported check-ins (blog).
  3. Gave the brand a voice. The puppy mascot, D. Expert and staff profiles with their own confessions made a finance site feel human to 20-somethings.
  4. Listened to the community about the business model. Hearing that users preferred bank-delivered tools led to the pivot that Wesabe, which pivoted later and sold self-serve, failed to survive (AB, Jul 2010).
  5. Removed a conflict of interest when the customer changed. It dropped peer-driven referral fees the moment banks became the customer (AB, Mar 2009).

What they did wrong

  1. Too many rooms for too few people. About 80 groups for about 20,000 users, most with 1 to 15 members, including city groups with 0 or 1 member (Feb 2008). A reviewer wrote: "they're trying to be a bit too broad at first, and they're spreading the activity a bit too thin... As far as community activity goes, 43things.com wins it hands down" (PFSR).
  2. The core utility was rough. The same reviewer described one-at-a-time transaction editing as "a user nightmare... I actually ended up giving up," and beta users hit registration errors and missing banks (blog comments, Jul 2008). Even a member confession read "im trying mint.com" (confession #110). Community could not compensate for Mint's simpler product, and Mint had five times the traffic by January 2009.
  3. Monetization through the community's trust. Peer ratings drove referral fees, ads arrived, and brokers were invited into discussions, which blurred peer advice and paid placement. A financial planner warned readers, "Make sure you know what qualifies them to give you the advice" (WaPo p.2).
  4. Bought growth with sweepstakes and a media partner's traffic rather than deepening engagement. The traffic peak (January 2009) coincided with the decision to leave consumers.
  5. Abandoned members without a public goodbye. No shutdown post was found: the blog stopped in June 2009, its comments filled with spam by July 2009, registrations closed, community URLs started redirecting, and the "old Geezeo" survived only as a stripped tool (Wayback). Unlike Wesabe, Geezeo did not hand the forum to members (see the Wesabe report).
  6. A long data-retention tail. A 2012 data file was exposed through a third-party provider in May 2019 (Jack Henry 8-K, Oct 2019). The 8-K does not say whose data the file held. It is a reminder to delete data when a product is retired.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Design a one-sentence, anonymity-optional contribution for a topic people are ashamed of (confessions). It produces content, belonging and press at almost no cost to the member.
  2. Tie social goals to real product data so progress shows up without members doing anything. Make sure the shared view (who else is on this goal) actually works.
  3. Give the brand a playful, human voice (a mascot, a fake columnist, staff who participate as members) when your category is dry or anxious.
  4. Treat the community as research about your business model. Ask members where they want the product delivered. Geezeo's "90% would rather get it from their bank" finding was worth more than its traffic.
  5. Put named experts in front of the community, each with a home inside it. Do it early and consistently, not as a late add-on.

Avoid these

  1. Don't launch dozens of empty rooms. Start with a few dense spaces, and split only when one gets crowded.
  2. Don't expect community to rescue a frustrating core workflow. Members who can't link their bank or tag transactions leave before they ever reach the groups.
  3. Don't monetize through peer trust (referral fees on peer ratings, brands in the threads) without hard separation. And when you pivot away from members, close the space publicly: export, notice, archive or handoff, rather than leaving it to spam.

Score justifications. Retention mechanics 3: confessions, auto-updating goals and SMS were genuinely good hooks, but there was no progression system and the groups were thin. Content engine 3: a prolific, distinctive blog and expert program, diluted after TheStreet syndication and stopped in mid-2009. Member ownership 2: members could create groups, but almost all stayed tiny, and no member-run institutions emerged. Staff investment 3: content director, campus ambassadors, founders in the comments and paid experts, then withdrawn at the pivot. Business impact evidence 2: SEC filings show a failed strategic investment and write-down, and the bank-era value of the community rests on company and client claims. Durability 1: the community lasted about two years and left no successor.
