TL;DR

  • What it was. Starbucks V2V ("Volunteer to Volunteer") was a social network for organizing community-service "actions," hosted at v2v.net/starbucks on a white-label platform from the Brazilian volunteering company V2V.net. It opened to partners (employees) by invitation in spring 2008 and to everyone in about November 2008. It reached about 12,357 members and 1,168 actions by January 2009. It sat almost unused through 2010 and had closed by January 2011 (Wayback homepage counters; closure notice).
  • Why people came back (the few who did). They came back for a real local event they were running or joining, such as a store manager's Earth Day clean-up or a painted oil drum. The site's live "As It Happens" feed, dashboard notifications, "Recommend" button and per-store virtual pages were meant to pull them back between events. Almost nobody came back once their event was over.
  • What Starbucks fed it. One V2V program manager on the Online and Content team (Nancy Richardson, who also moderated My Starbucks Idea) did the feeding, with a couple of admin accounts ("Virtual Barista", Anali Orr). Starbucks ran monthly cause features on the homepage, a feature on the 2008 New Orleans Leadership Conference, a case story in the FY2008 responsibility report, V2V T-shirts, and a small link from the January 2009 "I'm In" pledge site (MSI featured moderator, Dec 2008; FY2008 report).
  • Biggest mistake. Starbucks built the empty structure before there was activity to fill it, then sent its biggest volunteering moments elsewhere. It auto-generated a "virtual store" for every location and left nearly all of them to a placeholder "Virtual Barista." The January 2009 "I'm In" campaign, which drew over a million pledged hours, ran on a separate Salesforce site that pointed people to HandsOn Network first. After that, V2V's homepage went behind a login wall and its stores went quiet (StarbucksMelody, Aug 2010; pledge5 site, Jan 2009).
  • The one lesson. A cause community needs a steady supply of real, local, bookable things to do, and a clear owner for that supply. Starbucks later got both by plugging into HandsOn Network and then VolunteerMatch rather than running its own social network. Community-service hours roughly tripled after the switch, though the yearly Global Month of Service and district mandates also drove that rise (FY2009 report; FY2013 report).

What the community is

Starbucks V2V was a Facebook-style network ("Think Meetup meets LinkedIn," wrote Brand Autopsy's John Moore, a former Starbucks marketer) where partners and customers made profiles listing their skills and passions. They could create actions (events or causes), participate in them, recommend them, link related actions, favorite people, and post photos and conversation (Brand Autopsy, 2008; V2V FAQ, Mar 2008). Its FAQ called it "a social network with a purpose… It connects organizers with those who want to act." Its guidelines framed it as "a grassroots, Starbucks partner- and customer-driven online social network that will… extend the human connection beyond our stores" (guidelines, Mar 2008).

From about August 2008 the site added a Stores tab. A "virtual store" page was generated for every Starbucks location worldwide, with addresses for the US, Canada, the UK, Japan, Korea, Malaysia, Thailand, the Philippines and elsewhere. Each page invited people to "make this store the heart of your local neighborhood." Every page started under a default admin leader, "Virtual Barista," and partners could ask to take it over (store page, Toronto, May 2010; store gallery, Oct 2008).

Official vs unofficial. The only official space was the V2V site, plus a thin @StarbucksV2V Twitter account (joined July 2008, 3,079 followers, 3,745 following, no posts visible today; X profile). The unofficial coverage came from fan blogs: StarbucksMelody.com (Melody Overton, who joined V2V as "Melody O." and created actions such as "Re-Use your coffee tumbler!!") and Brand Autopsy. r/starbucks has nothing to say about the site, because its first archived post is from January 2010 and its first comment from November 2010, when V2V was already dormant (Arctic Shift stats).

Status today. Defunct. By 5 January 2011, v2v.net/starbucks redirected to a page that said: "The Starbucks V2V is closed down. However, you can still get involved at: www.starbucks.com/partners. Not a partner? Check out opportunities with Hands on Network" (Wayback). The URL has returned 404 since October 2012 (CDX).

Origin and strategy

V2V came out of the same early-2008 transformation push as My Starbucks Idea (launched March 2008). Howard Schultz had returned as CEO, and Starbucks was looking for "third place" experiences online. V2V belonged to the Starbucks Shared Planet CSR program. Its strategic job was the community goal: "contribute more than one million hours of community service per year in communities where we do business by 2015," set against a 2008 baseline of about 246,000 hours (US and Canada). The FY2008 responsibility report shows the goal under strain: participation in the older Make Your Mark volunteer program "has declined the past two years… due to competing business priorities." The report's fix was to "develop a global strategy… encourage store managers to promote volunteerism and leverage V2V." The same report described V2V as launched "in 2008 to extend and encourage human connections beyond our stores," with "more than 10,000 members in more than 60 countries" (FY2008 report).

Platform. Starbucks didn't build the software. V2V.net, a Rio de Janeiro company that grew out of the Programa Voluntários and its Portal do Voluntário (launched 2000 with Rede Globo, Globo.com and IBM Brasil), built the V2V tool around 2003–2004. It sold branded corporate volunteer networks to Itaú Unibanco, Bradesco, HSBC, C&A, Pfizer and others, and lists "Starbucks Coffee" (and Nike) among its clients (v2v.net, Aug 2011; V2V company profile). In the earliest snapshots, the most visible members were V2V.net's own people: founder Bruno Ayres (listed in Seattle) and Marianna Taborda (later V2V.net's CEO) were creating and joining actions in April–May 2008 (Wayback, Apr 2008; Wayback, May 2008; ZoomInfo). In other words, the vendor seeded the network.

Champion. The internal owner was Nancy Richardson, on the Online and Content team and Category Manager for Digital Strategy in 2008–09. She was listed as the V2V program manager in her My Starbucks Idea moderator profile, and the March 2008 pre-launch page asked partners to email her (MSI, Dec 2008; pre-launch page, Mar 2008; RocketReach). There's no evidence of an executive sponsor. Brand Autopsy noted that during the invitation phase V2V had "only been mentioned officially by the company as an update entry on MyStarbucksIdea" (Brand Autopsy).

Trajectory. Sign-ups grew steadily under invitation-only (about 1,300–2,000 a month from May to October 2008). The biggest jump, about 3,000 in a month, came between October and November 2008, when registration opened to everyone and 10,000 store managers did service at the New Orleans Leadership Conference. Growth then fell from about 2,250 in November–December to about 290 in December–January. Actions held at roughly one per ten members. For scale, Starbucks had about 150,000 partners (FY2009 report) and served more than 50 million customers a week (Business Wire, Mar 2011), so 12,000 accounts was under 10% of the workforce alone. The figures are cumulative registrations; no active-user data was ever published.

What keeps people coming back

The mechanics were the 2008 social-network kit. Retention mechanics score 2/5: the hooks existed, but no ritual or progress system drove return visits.

  • A live feed and notifications. The homepage "As It Happens" feed showed who had just joined which action. The Dashboard collected messages, "recommended actions and notifications from V2V," and updates about "your actions and your People" (FAQ).
  • Social amplification. The FAQ promised that "Recommendations will significantly increase participation in your action." Members could favorite people and link related actions.
  • Exclusivity at first. "Registration is available by invitation only… browse the People Gallery for someone you know and ask them for an invitation," with invitations "exclusive to partners during this development period" (Wayback, May 2008). Invitation-only produced steady, partner-driven growth. Once it ended, momentum didn't follow.
  • Local identity through virtual stores. The idea was that the store, not the brand, would be the tribe. A few partners made it work. Charles Bowring ran Warrington, UK, store #12909's page ("the home of people who care… pop in and enjoy a coffee tasting with us") and linked it to UK alumni and cycling-for-CO2 actions (Wayback, Sep 2008). Most store pages never got past the default.
  • Real-world payoff. The strongest reason to return was a concrete event. Newtown, Connecticut, store manager Peter Johnson used V2V for an Earth Day 2008 nature-center project (30+ volunteers) and then a public Oil Drum Art project. "I support reducing paper use, and if you go to my site at v2v, you will find suggestions," he told the Newtown Bee (Newtown Bee, 15 Aug 2008; FY2008 report).
  • Missing: no streaks, hour logging, badges, leaderboards, seasons or recurring events of its own. Hour logging arrived only with the 2010 HandsOn-based replacement, whose four verbs were Lead, Join, Record and Share (StarbucksMelody, Nov 2010). There were no switching costs either: a member's action history was worth nothing anywhere else.

The content engine

Content engine scores 2/5: there was some steady programming in 2008, and it stopped once the homepage went behind a login.

  • Monthly cause features on the homepage. These included the 2008 Leadership Conference ("nearly 10,000 Starbucks partners will converge on New Orleans"), AIDS Awareness Month walks in October, "Believe in the power of 10,000" photos in November, International Volunteer Day on 5 December and (RED) holiday cards in January (Wayback, Sep 2008–Jan 2009).
  • Staff-seeded actions were then attached to store pages by an admin: "How to Build a Community Service Project," "A Day On with Martin Luther King, Jr.," "2009 Partners on the Farm" and "I'm in! Are you?" They were added in bulk to stores where nobody was active (Wayback, Tanzania/Saudi store page, Sep 2009).
  • Third-party causes used it as a channel. GOOD magazine promoted its "CO2 World" GOOD Sheets through both MSI and V2V (HuffPost, 2008).
  • Member-made content was the actions themselves, which ran from AIDS walks and food banks to a "Quote of the Day" and a "Clover Coffee Experience." Many were coffee-fan or social posts, not volunteering. Nothing was curated or rewarded beyond appearing in the feed. No newsletter, webinar or video series turned up: no evidence found.

People, programs and governance

Staff investment scores 2/5.

  • Staff: one program manager (Nancy Richardson) plus admin accounts (Anali Orr, Seattle/Green Lake, and the "Virtual Barista") (Wayback store pages). The vendor's team seeded early content. There's no evidence of regional community managers.
  • Rules: the guidelines made V2V "an extension of the Starbucks work environment." They barred "religious or political causes" and commercial promotion, told partners the time was "not considered paid time," and warned that breaking the rules "may have a negative impact on your role in the V2V community and possibly with your employment with Starbucks" (guidelines). For a volunteering site, that's a chilling frame: unpaid, on your own time, and tied to your job.
  • Programs: no ambassador program existed on V2V itself. When it was replaced, "district community leads" (partners) carried feedback upward. One of them, "Wongster," passed partners' complaints about losing customer access up the chain (StarbucksMelody comments, Nov 2010).
  • Feedback into product: no evidence found. This was a CSR program, not a product-feedback loop, even though it shared a moderator with MSI.

Member ownership scores 2/5: members could create actions and claim store pages, but Starbucks controlled the frame, and few members took ownership.

Growth loops

  • Partner-to-partner invitations (March–October 2008) were the only loop that clearly worked. The counter rose from 148 to 6,850.
  • Store-to-customer was the planned loop: virtual store pages, store posters and a "V2V Community Bulletin Board Flyer" action (Wayback, Dec 2008). Evidence suggests it barely ran. A former partner asked, "Was the V2V network advertised?… never saw anything about the V2V" (comment, Nov 2010).
  • Campaign spillover was wasted. "I'm In" (21–25 January 2009) offered a free tall coffee for a five-hour pledge, with TV by BBDO. It ran on pledge5.starbucks.com (Salesforce Sites) and logged more than 500,000 pledged hours within days, later over a million (Ad Age; InfoWorld; Dominique Hind). Pledgers were sent to HandsOn Network to find opportunities, and V2V was a secondary tile: "Make a deeper commitment to volunteerism—join this online community" (pledge5, Jan 2009). Weeks later V2V's homepage went behind a login wall.
  • SEO: none to speak of. Login walls and auto-generated store pages produced no searchable value.

Hours fell while V2V was the flagship tool. Starbucks blamed the 2009 drop on "global economic challenges that required a realignment of our business and, consequently, limited our capacity to coordinate community service activities." V2V doesn't appear at all in the FY2009 or FY2010 reports (text search, 0 hits). Hours rose only after the switch to a HandsOn-based site and an annual Global Month of Service, which some districts made "mandatory for the management teams" (StarbucksMelody comments, Feb 2012). That's correlation, not proof. No figures on retention, churn, NPS or cost were found.

What they did right

  1. Tied the community to a real-world action, not talk. The core object was an event with a place and time, which gave members like Peter Johnson a reason to recruit and to come back (Newtown Bee).
  2. Seeded with insiders first. Opening to partners by invitation for about seven months gave it a trusted core and steady growth: 148 to 6,850 accounts (Wayback).
  3. Mixed employees and customers in one space. This was what members valued most and missed most when it ended: "It was cool that partners AND customers could get involved on V2V. In fact, I'm pretty sure that's how you and I connected" (camspi, Nov 2010).
  4. Rode a flagship IRL moment. Featuring the New Orleans conference matched the largest monthly sign-up jump.
  5. Bought rather than built, and later swapped out quickly. Using V2V.net's white-label platform made launch cheap. When it failed, Starbucks moved to partners that already had large opportunity databases (HandsOn in 2010, VolunteerMatch later), and the volunteer program kept going (3BL/VolunteerMatch).

What they did wrong

  1. Built empty structure ahead of demand. It auto-generated a virtual store for every location, some with wrong data: the Taif, Saudi Arabia store was filed under "Pemba North, Tanzania." Nearly all were left under the "Virtual Barista." Consequence: a site full of empty rooms. "Very few partners ever volunteered their time to make the store page a lively place different from the default graphics" (StarbucksMelody, Aug 2010).
  2. No supply of opportunities. Members had to invent every action. There was no nonprofit database, and the network was too thin to match people nearby. Consequence: Starbucks' own big campaign routed pledgers to HandsOn Network, which "gains an extensive database of projects and non-profit organizations," and that's what the replacement was built on (StarbucksMelody, Nov 2010).
  3. Split its own attention. "I'm In" got TV, in-store displays and a new Salesforce site, while V2V got a link. Consequence: about a million pledged hours flowed past the community without joining it.
  4. Hid the activity. From about February 2009 the homepage was a login form with no feed or counters. Consequence: visitors saw no social proof, and the network stopped looking alive.
  5. Under-promoted and under-staffed. It had one program manager, was announced quietly through an MSI update and got no store-level push that a partner could remember (Brand Autopsy; merryncole, Nov 2010).
  6. Employment-policy framing. Warnings about consequences "possibly with your employment," unpaid time and a ban on religious and political causes all worked against grassroots volunteering (guidelines).
  7. A quiet shutdown that shut out customers. It closed with a one-line notice on the vendor's domain. The replacement was partner-only, so the customer members lost their space. "I'm not sure I see the benefit of making it partner-only" (Alyson, Nov 2010). Starbucks reversed this by February 2012, when community.starbucks.com allowed customer log-ins again (StarbucksMelody, Feb 2012). A Starbucks social-impact lead later summed up the whole run: "Everything we tried before VolunteerMatch took a lot of time and was really expensive. And we were never able to get the sites to do everything we wanted them to do" (Anna Cunningham, 2018).

Durability scores 1/5: the site was active for about a year and dead within three.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make the core object a real-world commitment with a date and place. Events give members something to recruit for, return to and report on.
  2. Seed with insiders by invitation, then open up at a flagship moment. V2V's best growth came from partner invites and a 10,000-person IRL event.
  3. Mix your staff and your customers in one space. Members valued the partner–customer mix most. Don't give it up for administrative convenience.
  4. Plug into an existing supply instead of asking members to invent it all. Integrate the opportunity, template or content database that already exists, the way HandsOn and VolunteerMatch provided one, so a new member always has something to join nearby.
  5. Close the loop with progress tracking. Lead, Join, Record and Share (logging hours) gave the successor what V2V lacked: a reason to come back after the event and a number the company could report.

Avoid these

  1. Don't scaffold empty rooms. Thousands of auto-generated local pages under a placeholder host made the community look abandoned. Create local spaces only when a named person claims them.
  2. Don't run your biggest campaign past your community. If the TV-and-free-coffee moment points somewhere else, the community will never reach critical mass.
  3. Don't hide activity or frame participation as an HR obligation. Login walls, "not paid time" and threats to your employment kill voluntary energy.
