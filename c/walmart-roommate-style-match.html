TL;DR

  • What it was: a sponsored group on Facebook, launched Wednesday Aug 8, 2007, for college students heading to dorms. Members could take a "Roommate Style Match" decorating quiz, compare styles with a roommate, download a dorm shopping list, and click through to Walmart.com. Walmart said it would run until Oct 31, 2007 (Reuters, Aug 8, 2007; Reuters snippet).
  • Why people came back: almost nobody did for the stated purpose. The only recurring activity was a running fight on the group's Wall about Walmart's labor practices, unions, China sourcing and small-town retail. Facebook analyst Jeremiah Owyang counted only 10% of the 20 newest Wall posts as positive (web-strategist.com, Aug 24, 2007). Computerworld found that none of the 100+ comments were about dorms (Aug 24, 2007).
  • What Walmart fed it: a clever quiz and some e-commerce widgets, then nothing. Walmart turned discussion boards off, did not post as staff, did not cross-promote the group from Walmart.com, and never answered the critics in the group. Its only reply was a press statement (Owyang, Sep 10, 2007).
  • Biggest mistake: it treated a social space as a campaign microsite, for a brand that young people distrusted and that had just been embarrassed twice in social media (The Hub in 2006, and the Edelman-backed "Wal-Marting Across America" fake blog). With nothing on-topic to talk about and nobody from Walmart there, critics filled the space. Target's rival group from the same season, which had discussions turned on and a staff member answering questions, had about 6x the members (Owyang, Sep 10, 2007).
  • The one lesson: you can't rent a community for a season. If the brand has no reason for people to talk to each other, and no humans in the room, the loudest existing sentiment about the brand becomes the community.

What the community is

A Facebook sponsored group (facebook.com/group.php?gid=2784351093) titled "Wal-Mart Roommate Style Match," also called "Wal-Mart's Back to School sponsored group." Sponsored groups were a paid Facebook ad product from before Pages existed: brands paid to customize a group with branded modules. Facebook replaced this kind of brand presence with free Pages and self-serve Facebook Ads on Nov 6, 2007, a week after Walmart's group was due to end (Meta newsroom, Nov 6, 2007).

Modules, as catalogued on Sep 10, 2007 (Owyang; YouMoz):

  • "Your Personal Checklist": a dorm supply list with e-commerce links to Walmart.com
  • "The Roommate Style Match Quiz": a persona quiz ("What is your favorite way to study?", "If your life were a movie, what genre would it be?") that assigned a style such as "Brain-Stormer," showed a drawing of your room, and listed "recommended products." Results could be posted to your profile and sent to friends (Owyang, in Sandy Carter, The New Language of Marketing 2.0, IBM Press 2009, sample chapter)
  • "Mix it Up": combine two roommates' styles (same-gender pairings only)
  • "SoundCheck": clips from Walmart's music-performance site (Bon Jovi, Mandy Moore)
  • "College Store": Walmart's "earth-friendly" products, plus freshman tips, and links to its new Site to Store service
  • A Wall open to any member. The discussion board was turned off.

There were no official spaces besides this group. Unofficial spaces were simply the anti-Walmart groups and sites that commenters linked to from the Wall (Wal-Mart Watch, Wake Up Wal-Mart, The High Cost of Low Price). Wal-Mart Watch invited people to join its own Facebook group in the same post where it mocked this one (Wal-Mart Watch blog, Aug 21, 2007).

Status today: defunct. Walmart planned to end it on Oct 31, 2007 (Reuters). The exact takedown date wasn't found. The Wayback Machine never captured the group's contents (it needed a login); a 2016 capture shows only "You must log in to continue" (Wayback, Dec 27, 2016).

Origin and strategy

  • Business goal: back-to-school sales, not community. Reuters framed it as Walmart "hop[ing] to grab a larger chunk of back-to-school shopping dollars," in a season when high gas prices were squeezing shoppers and Walmart had cut prices on 16,000+ items. Spokeswoman Karen Burk: "We realize that this is an audience that we need to be talking to, and that this is a channel we need to be on" (Reuters, Aug 8, 2007). CIO said the hope was that "college students would talk up and buy back-to-school products via Facebook and Walmart.com" (CIO, Oct 4, 2007).
  • The lesson it applied from The Hub. After its own MySpace-style teen site, The Hub, closed after 10 weeks in 2006 (see walmart-the-hub), Walmart went to where students already were instead of building its own network. Forrester's Josh Bernoff approved: "It is much easier to get someone on Facebook to join your group than to get someone to come to your Web site and join your community" (Computerworld, Aug 24, 2007). Owyang: "Having learned their lesson to consider joining existing communities before building their own…" (Aug 24, 2007).
  • Who built it. Owyang listed Walmart's agency as Edelman (Sep 10, 2007), the PR firm behind the 2006 "Wal-Marting Across America" blog that turned out to be company-funded (Adweek, 2009). No internal executive champion was named in any source. Spokespeople were Karen Burk (launch) and Jami Arms (response to the backlash).
  • Cost. Contemporary bloggers put sponsored groups at $150K for 3 months up to $300K for 3 months (YouMoz, 2007); another observer captioned a screenshot "Walmart paid $100k+ to be on facebook and this is what happened" (Flickr, Sep 10, 2007). Both are third-party estimates. Walmart never disclosed the price.
  • Planned as a campaign. The ~12-week run (Aug 8 to Oct 31) was set from day one. Owyang warned at launch: "This is a relationship that Wal-Mart now needs to think about nurturing, not a campaign that can be turned 'off' at a specific date" (Carter 2009, quoting Owyang's launch post).

Benchmarks from the same season: Target's rival sponsored group "Dorm Survival Guide" had 7,106 members, 33 discussion threads, 396 photos and 462 Wall posts on Sep 10, 2007, with mostly positive sentiment (Owyang). Apple Students had 423,641 members (YouMoz). Owyang's own niche "Web Strategy" group passed 1,500 members in about the same time (Aug 24, 2007). Facebook then had about 34 million users (CIO). Growth flattened after the late-August press: about +260 members from Aug 24 to Sep 10, then about +20 after that. Owyang judged that only "a few students and a handful of folks" admitted to shopping at Walmart, so the group had not reached its target segment.

No Reddit discussion of the group was found in the Arctic Shift archive (Reddit was tiny in 2007). The main forum discussion was a Slashdot thread, "Users Trash Wal-Mart On Its Facebook Site" (Slashdot, Aug 26, 2007), submitted by the Computerworld reporter.

What keeps people coming back

Score: retention mechanics 1/5. Nothing was designed for a second visit.

  • One-shot utility. The quiz and checklist are things you do once before move-in day. Nothing refreshed: no weekly content, no challenges, no seasons, no "show us your finished dorm" follow-up. Target, by contrast, got students to link their YouTube "dorm room tours" (Owyang).
  • Social sharing, not social ties. The quiz's best feature was posting your result to your profile and sending it to friends (Owyang called it the "killer app"). That spreads the quiz but connects people to their existing friends, not to each other in the group. Pairing with a roommate had to be done by hand (Carter 2009).
  • No place for peer help. With discussions off, the only shared surface was a flat Wall. Owyang had hoped to see "if students begin posting questions about dorm life. Will other students respond or will Wal-Mart step forward…" They didn't, and it didn't.
  • The retention loop that did form was conflict. People returned to argue: critics posting Wal-Mart Watch links and documentary screenings, defenders telling the "smelly friggen hippies" to look at job creation, and members accusing each other of being paid shills (Wall posts Aug 20–23, 2007, in Owyang). That debate was the group's only real engagement. BusinessWeek called it "a pretty healthy bull session on Wal-Mart's role in the U.S." (Aug 20, 2007).
  • No staff ties, recognition, status, or switching costs. No evidence of any of these.

The content engine

Score: content engine 1/5. A fixed set of interactive widgets made by the agency at launch: quiz, checklist, Mix it Up, SoundCheck clips, green-products store, freshman tips. They were well made. Owyang called the quiz "very clever," and an investing blogger called the room-planning idea "Brilliant" (ValuePlays, Aug 9, 2007). But all of it was e-commerce: every module ended in a Walmart.com link. No evidence of new content during the run, no staff posts, no responses on the Wall, and no member-made content encouraged beyond 27 photos (vs Target's 396). Owyang also noted that Walmart never promoted the group from its own site (Aug 24, 2007).

People, programs and governance

Scores: staff investment 1/5; member ownership 1/5.

  • Team: no community manager, host or Walmart voice is visible in any captured Wall post. The only communication was through the press office.
  • Moderation: Walmart left the Wall open and didn't delete critics. Spokeswoman Jami Arms: "We recognize that we are facilitating a live conversation, and we know that in any conversation, especially one happening online, there will be both supporters and detractors… We're happy that so many of our customers are talking on Facebook about why they like Wal-Mart" (Computerworld, Aug 24, 2007). That last sentence misdescribed a Wall where Owyang counted 10% positive posts.
  • Structure: discussion boards were disabled, so members couldn't create topics, help each other, or keep on-topic talk apart from the protest. Owyang's main recommendation was to turn them on and "segment the conversations about going back to school… Continue to allow critics (you can't stop it anyways)" (Aug 24, 2007).
  • Ambassadors/superusers: none. Owyang and commenter Connie Benson argued that Walmart needed a human evangelist, in the mold of Robert Scoble at Microsoft or Lionel Menchaca at Dell, before any social campaign could work (Aug 25, 2007).
  • Feedback into product: no evidence.

Growth loops

  • Intended: viral quiz sharing (profile posts and friend invites) inside Facebook's then-closed college network, plus whatever traffic the sponsorship placement brought.
  • Actual: the strongest growth driver was negative press. BusinessWeek (Aug 20), Wal-Mart Watch (Aug 21), GenDigital, Computerworld (Aug 24), Slashdot (Aug 26) and La Presse (Aug 27) all pointed readers at the group to watch the fight. Membership roughly tripled during that week (312 → 934), and many of those joining were critics. One member complained, "I don't know how I became a member of it" (Wall, Aug 23, 2007).
  • Missing: no cross-promotion from Walmart.com or stores (Owyang), and no roommate-to-roommate invite flow.

Evidence of business impact

Score: business impact evidence 1/5. No sales, traffic or conversion figures were ever published by Walmart, and none appear in its filings or press releases (none found). What exists:

  • Analyst forecasts, not results. Forrester's Bernoff "expects the Facebook effort will lead to more sales than the company's previous two Web 2.0 projects" (Computerworld).
  • Reach was trivial. Roughly 1,200 members against about 34M Facebook users, and an estimated $100K–$300K spend: on the order of $100–$250 per member, before counting how many members were hostile (estimate from the third-party cost figures above).
  • Reputational effect. CIO used the group as evidence of Walmart's slipping digital edge, while saying it "has so far gone more smoothly" than The Hub (CIO, Oct 4, 2007). It became a lasting textbook case: cited in Fournier & Avery's "The Uninvited Brand" (Business Horizons, 2011), a 2009 IBM Press marketing book (Carter), a WVU dissertation on retail disclosure (WVU), and PR-industry advice pieces (PRCrossing, 2011).

What they did right

  1. Went to the audience instead of building a walled garden. It fixed The Hub's core mistake, and experts endorsed the shift at the time (Bernoff, Owyang).
  2. A well-made, shareable hook. The persona quiz plus room drawing was praised even by critics, and it used Facebook's social mechanics (posting to profiles and sending to friends) better than most 2007 brand units (Owyang via Carter).
  3. Didn't censor or pull it. BusinessWeek ("a good call… not to censor the page or take it down"), GenDigital ("we wholeheartedly applaud the world's largest retailer for keeping the page live and not folding") and Owyang ("bold enough to try it again, and hasn't pulled the site down") all credited this. Deleting the posts would have turned a minor story into a censorship story, as Walmart's earlier fake-blog episode had shown.
  4. A calm public line. Arms's "supporters and detractors" statement avoided getting defensive. CIO noted that "Typically, such comments would put Wal-Mart on the defensive."

What they did wrong

  1. Campaign, not community. A fixed 12-week run, one-shot widgets and a hard end date. Owyang warned about exactly this at launch. Consequence: nothing to return to, and nothing left after Oct 31.
  2. Discussions turned off, so there was no on-topic place to talk. The only surface was the Wall, which made the protest the main content. Consequence: "none [of the comments] focused on the company's goal for the site" (Computerworld), against Target's 33 threads.
  3. No humans in the room. No staff host, no replies, no evangelist. All communication went through PR statements to reporters. Consequence: critics were never answered in the space, defenders were accused of being paid, and the brand's side came only from a spokesperson's email.
  4. Ignored its brand-trust problem. 2007 was the peak of organized anti-Walmart activism (Wake Up Wal-Mart, Wal-Mart Watch, The High Cost of Low Price), and college students were a core audience for it. Bernoff: "Wal-Mart has more enemies than most people… If you give people an opportunity, they are going to come after you." Owyang: "very difficult to try a community marketing strategy with eCommerce hooks without first addressing the brand detractors." Consequence: the group became a ready-made venue for its opponents, whom Wal-Mart Watch actively directed there.
  5. The same agency, same playbook. Edelman, still associated with the 2006 fake blog, ran another agency-built, polished social unit with no real Walmart voice. Consequence: skeptical observers read it as more of the same.
  6. No promotion and the wrong segment. Not cross-promoted, and members who actually shopped at Walmart were scarce (Owyang). Owyang also noted that the people who decide dorm purchases are often parents, a point ValuePlays made from the opposite side. Consequence: about 1,200 members against Target's 7,106 in the same season and the same format.
  7. Overstated success in public. "We're happy that so many of our customers are talking on Facebook about why they like Wal-Mart" didn't match the Wall. Consequence: small, but it fed the perception that the brand wasn't listening.
  8. No learning carried forward from The Hub (partly). Walmart changed the venue but not the model: brand-controlled, sales-first, no staff presence. Its later and more credible social efforts went the other way: the Check Out blog (March 2008), written unfiltered by Walmart buyers (NYT, Mar 3, 2008); the Elevenmoms blogger council (2008); and an official Facebook Page only in October 2009 (Agorapulse, 2014).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Go where your users already are. Joining an existing platform beats building a new destination, as experts said at the time. But you then need a reason to stay, not just a reason to visit.
  2. Lead with a useful, shareable tool. The style quiz plus roommate matching was a good acquisition hook because it solved a real moment (move-in) and spread through profiles. Pair a hook like that with a recurring reason to come back (a "show your finished room" thread, weekly tips, Q&A).
  3. Open real discussion and seed it with experts. Target's best thread was "Tips from someone with experience," started by a Target employee, with 55+ replies (Owyang). Peer Q&A on topic is what pushes the off-topic fight to the margins.
  4. Don't censor critics, and answer them as a person. Leaving the posts up was the one thing everyone praised. The missing half was a named staff member replying in the space instead of only through the press.
  5. Measure engagement against a benchmark. Owyang's side-by-side (members, threads, photos, posts, sentiment) showed the gap within a month. Track those numbers and change course mid-run.

Avoid these

  1. Renting a community for a season. A hard end date and fixed widgets make an ad unit, not a community, and members can tell.
  2. Launching social with an unaddressed trust deficit. If your brand has organized critics, they will use any open space you create. Fix the issue, or put a credible human voice up front, before inviting conversation.
  3. Disabling the structures that let members help each other. Without threads, topics or peer Q&A, the Wall becomes a protest board, and the loudest sentiment about your brand becomes the community.
