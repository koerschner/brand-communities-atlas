TL;DR

  • What it is: Amazon.com's built-in message boards. Starting around 2006, a "Customer Discussions" box sat on product pages, author pages and tag pages, alongside topic forums (Kindle, Gold Box, Romance, Religion, Politics, Classical Music, Meet Our Authors and many more). Any customer in good standing could post (guidelines, Wayback Jul 2007). Amazon retired them on October 6–13, 2017 (eBook Evangelist).
  • Why people came back: not because of anything Amazon designed. Members built rituals themselves: serial social threads ("Kindle Kitchen #6", "RAOK #52", "Australian/Kiwi kindle users thread #4 – the BBQ"), daily deal and freebie threads, and monthly "what are you reading?" polls. Email subscriptions to threads brought them back. Some of these threads passed 8,000–9,900 replies (Wayback Jan 2013; ILMK, Aug 2015).
  • What Amazon fed it: very little. There were staff "Announcements" (firmware updates, launches), light moderation, a yes/no "adds to the discussion" vote that hid low-rated posts, and a volunteer "Kindle Forum Pro" badge. In its last years Amazon took features away: the forum link left the Kindle page, then search went, then the General Help forum, then the Pro badges (eBook Evangelist, Oct 3 2017; MobileRead, Oct 2017).
  • Biggest mistake: Amazon ran a social space with no owner. It wouldn't moderate it properly and then shut it down. Trolls, sock puppets, anti-author "swarms" (which drew an Anne Rice petition in 2014) and, at the end, floods of fake support phone numbers took over, and Amazon walked away. It announced one date, closed a week early, and sent members to Amazon Spark, a shopping feed that was itself shut down 20 months later (Guardian, 2014; KBoards, Oct 2017; The Verge, Jun 2019).
  • The one lesson: A forum attached to a store will draw a real community whether or not you plan for one. Decide on purpose what it is for and staff it to match. Amazon's purpose-built replacements (structured Customer Q&A that emails questions to verified owners, and a staffed device help forum) outlived the open forums because each had one clear job.

What the community is

Amazon Customer Discussions were Amazon's native message boards. Amazon's 2007 guidelines describe them as a way to "connect you with other customers to share your questions, insights, and views about products available on Amazon.com." Anyone could read. Posting required a purchase history and an account "in good standing in the Amazon community" (guidelines, Jul 2007). There were three layers:

  1. Product-page discussions. A "Customer Discussions" box under the reviews listed active threads about that product, with a "Start a discussion" field. Author pages had their own forum, though even authors had trouble finding it (Kathy Ide, Aug 2015).
  2. Topic and community forums at amazon.com/forum/<name>. Jansen and Whitman sort them into "community forums" (science, politics, music, religion) and "product forums" (e.g. Kindle). Most were set up by Amazon (Whitman & Jansen, IEEE 2016). In 2007–2009 the Kindle forum sat inside the tag-based "Customer Communities" pages (amazon.com/tag/kindle/forum), next to tagged products and Listmania lists (Wayback Nov 2007).
  3. Comments on customer reviews. These used the same framework and outlived the forums until Amazon retired them on December 16, 2020, saying they were "rarely used" (Business Insider, Dec 2020).

Who ran it: an "Amazon Customer Discussions team" took feedback (guidelines, 2007). Staff and moderators posted as "Amazon Official" / "Amazon Community Team" (Whitman & Jansen). No public executive owner or team size has been found.

Status: defunct. The Kindle forum's own banner read: "This Amazon forum is retiring October 13, 2017… we encourage you to explore Goodreads Groups for books and Spark for other ways to engage with your interests" (Wayback, Oct 13 2017). In practice the forums went dark on October 6 (eBook Evangelist, Oct 9 2017). The successor, the Amazon Digital and Device Forum (amazonforum.com), opened October 9, 2017. It covers devices and digital content only, is moderated by "[Amazon Staff]" employees, and keeps a "Kindle Legacy Forum" archive of 2009–2017 Kindle help threads (amazonforum.com Getting Started). The general discussions were not archived.

Origin and strategy

  • Launch, about 2006. Wikipedia's list of Amazon services says Amazon introduced "product wikis (later folded into Amapedia) and discussion forums for certain products" in 2006 (Wikipedia; that sentence has no citation). The earliest Wayback captures of discussion threads date from June 2007 and of the guidelines from July 2007 (CDX). The forums were part of Amazon's mid-2000s run of participation features: Amazon Connect author blogs (Feb 2006, press release), Askville (Dec 2006–Oct 2013), Amapedia (2006/07–2010, Wikipedia), customer Tags (phased out 2012–Jan 2013, Indies Unlimited) and Listmania. Every one of them was eventually retired.
  • Stated purpose: commerce first. The 2007 guidelines' example posts are all about buying ("Do the Calvin Klein t-shirts run small?", "If I want a pocket-sized digital camera, do I have to give up decent optical zoom?"), plus "debate plots, characters, lyrics… with other fans" (guidelines). The goal was word of mouth at the point of sale, not a members' club.
  • The Kindle made it matter. The Kindle forum went live with the first Kindle (launched November 19, 2007). Two days later it had 53 threads, including "Can I read in the Dark?" and "An Amazing Idea Completely Ruined" (Wayback, Nov 21 2007). For a $399 device with no store shelf, it was the main place buyers could question owners. Good e-Reader's editor called it "a big part of the early success of the Kindle," and said it was also where Amazon engineers announced critical firmware updates (Good e-Reader, Oct 3 2017).
  • The strategy drifted away from it. By 2014 Amazon was building Customer Questions & Answers, a structured Q&A that emails a shopper's question to people who bought the item (ManagingCommunities, Aug 2014). Amazon's own help page says Q&A "replaces Customer Discussions in product categories that have a high volume of product questions" (Amazon Q&A guidelines). In 2017 Amazon pointed social energy at Spark (an Instagram-style shoppable feed for Prime members) and Goodreads (acquired 2013). The old forums no longer served any business goal (Good e-Reader, Oct 6 2017).

Size and activity over time

Amazon never published membership or post totals. One quirk distorts every number: each forum's listing showed only about 1,000–1,027 threads ("Showing 1-25 of 1023 discussions"). That is a display window, not a total. Gizmodo noticed that the Gold Box forum's "available archives only go 1,023 discussions deep" (Gizmodo, Jan 2015). Reply counts on long-lived threads are the best activity proxy.

Trajectory: fast organic growth in 2007–2008 driven by the Kindle. A long plateau of very active social threads from about 2009 to 2015. Neglect and feature removal in 2016–2017. Then shutdown. No member counts were ever published.

What keeps people coming back

Nearly all the stickiness was member-made:

  • Serial social threads as a "third place." Regulars kept numbered threads going for years, starting a new part when one got too long: "KINDLE KITCHEN #5/#6: A place for sharing, caring, chat and FUN!", "RAOK #52" (Random Acts of Kindness), "Australian/Kiwi kindle users thread #4 – the BBQ", "Al's Place", "Thread of Everlasting Ennui" (ILMK, 2015; Wayback 2013; Wayback 2017). Good e-Reader noted "many threads that have over a thousand pages," and said the most popular were about favourite books and "what wine goes well with erotica and romance novels" (Good e-Reader).
  • Daily utility rituals. Deal-hunting threads ("Discounted / Price Dropped Kindle eBooks", "Freebie Books – Links Only – No Self Promotion", "Free Books & Chat – October 2017") and monthly "Informal Poll – What are you reading?" threads gave people a daily reason to visit. One reader said, "I visit the price drop forum daily"; another, "I spent a lot dough 'saving' money on books from posts there!" (KBoards, Oct 2017). These threads also fed straight back into purchases.
  • Notifications. Every thread had "Receive e-mail when new posts are made" and an RSS feed (Wayback 2013). Once Amazon hid the navigation, subscriptions and bookmarks were the only way back in (eBook Evangelist).
  • Light status. There was a per-post "adds to the discussion" yes/no vote that hid low-rated posts by default (guidelines), and a later Kindle Forum Pro badge. Good e-Reader credited the voting with keeping the space "safe"; others called downvoting "a constant source of contention" (eBook Evangelist).
  • Identity and tribes. Genre forums (Romance, Classical Music, Religion, Politics, the reviewers' forum) produced regulars, cliques and "forum police." Members of the politics forums later rebuilt an "Amazon Tavern" thread on another site (Just Plain Politics, Apr 2019).
  • Switching costs. Some were real but untapped: years of thread history, reading recommendations, and the convenience of one Amazon login. When the forums closed, the regulars mostly moved together to KBoards, MobileRead, Goodreads and topic forums (below). That shows the bonds were between members, not with Amazon.

Absent: streaks, levels, leaderboards, challenges, staff hosts, events, and any in-app presence. The forums never appeared in Amazon's mobile app. Spark, the 2017 replacement, was mobile-only (Good e-Reader).

The content engine

  • Company content: minimal. In the Kindle forum Amazon posted sticky "Announcements" (software updates, new models, "Welcome to the Kindle Discussion Forum – A Congregation of Kindle Enthusiasts", "Kindle Fire Tips: Getting Started"). The scraped window held only 4 announcements (Whitman & Jansen). Announcements could draw a lot of replies (1,039 on one 2013 firmware notice), so they doubled as unofficial bug-report threads (Wayback 2013). No newsletters, events, courses or video were tied to the forums.
  • Member content: almost everything. Over 95% of Kindle forum threads and posts were user-generated. Posts with Amazon links were rated about 12% more helpful, and every link in the sample pointed to an Amazon domain (Whitman & Jansen). The commerce loop ran itself. Members also wrote reference threads and outside guides (e.g. "The Kindle Cookbook – Third Edition Now Available," Dec 2008, Wayback).
  • Curation and reward: none apart from the Forum Pro badge. Amazon never featured member content anywhere else on the site.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: "Amazon Official" posters and moderators. Moderation was real but inconsistent. Gizmodo found deleted comments in the Gold Box forum next to an "inflammatory" post that stayed up, and said the place was "curated in some fashion. Just not enough for it to make any sense at all" (Gizmodo, 2015).
  • Kindle Forum Pros: unpaid volunteers chosen by Amazon "based on your forum contributions." Members got a badge and, as one Pro saw it, "more…attention from Amazon when I ask them questions." The downside was being "accus[ed] of being in Amazon's pocket" (Bufo Calvin, ILMK, Aug 2015). The program "was dumped with no announcements or explanation… They just removed the badges from our accounts" (MobileRead, Oct 2017).
  • Rules: the 2007 "guest at a friend's dinner party" guidelines banned abuse, spam, affiliate links and repeated self-promotion (guidelines). Authors were pushed out of reader forums into a separate Meet Our Authors forum, which many called "a sort of prison where we'd have no contact with readers." Reader "forum police" punished self-promotion, in one case by tagging an author's book page with "four nasty tags" (Writers in the Storm, Aug 2012).
  • Enforcement failures: default usernames weren't unique, search and sorting were poor, long-time posters were "banned with no explanation," and moderators changed "almost on a weekly basis" (MobileRead). Banned accounts could still file reports, and "socks became a norm" (Just Plain Politics).
  • Feedback into product: no documented loop. Whitman and Jansen found 26% of Kindle threads were complaints or malfunctions and recommended that Amazon use the forum to "identify problems with the product" (Whitman & Jansen). We found no evidence that Amazon did so in any structured way.

Growth loops

  • Product-page placement. This was the forum's real advantage: every shopper on a product page saw live threads. It turned browsers into posters without any marketing.
  • Search traffic. Threads were public and indexable, so long Kindle threads ranked for troubleshooting queries (inferred from the volume of help threads; no SEO data found).
  • Purchase influence. "Before I bought my first Kindle, this forum was a tremendous help… I don't think I would have bought my first Kindle without it" (eBook Evangelist, Oct 2017). But only 13% of Kindle threads were pre-purchase; 87% were post-purchase (Whitman & Jansen).
  • Leakage. The forum's regulars also created rivals. KindleBoards (now KBoards) grew from Amazon forum members ("How many of us got here originally because Leslie [left a breadcrumb] when Kindles were new and Harvey started Kindleboards?"), and it took in the refugees in 2017 (KBoards).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. Amazon never disclosed deflection, conversion or retention figures for the forums. No SEC filing mentions them. (EDGAR full-text search only covers 2001 onward, and Amazon's 10-Ks don't discuss community features.)
  • Academic (descriptive only): Whitman and Jansen (IEEE, 2016) analysed 12,467 Kindle forum posts. The forum was mainly a post-purchase support channel (87% of threads; 26% complaints and problems), and activity followed Kindle release dates. They conclude it gave Amazon "customer service and product development" value but did not measure any of it (paper; IEEE Xplore).
  • Anecdotal: buyers credit the forum with their Kindle purchases (above). TeleRead argued that the forums "reduce the load at least somewhat on Amazon's phone support people" (TeleRead, Oct 2017).
  • Revealed preference: Amazon replaced the forums with things it could measure and control: structured Q&A seeded by purchase history (ManagingCommunities), a staffed device-help forum (TeleRead, Oct 10 2017) and Spark. That suggests Amazon valued the support function and saw no value in the social one.

What they did right

  1. Put the conversation where the purchase happens. Discussions on product pages turned shoppers into participants with no acquisition cost, and the Kindle forum filled within days of launch (Wayback Nov 2007).
  2. Low barrier to post, purchase-gated identity. Requiring an Amazon purchase to post cut down drive-by spam in the early years, and readers didn't need to sign in (guidelines).
  3. Community-weighted visibility. The "adds to the discussion" vote and hiding low-rated posts by default were early versions of what Reddit and Stack Overflow later made standard. Observers credited them with preventing "rampant trolling" (Good e-Reader).
  4. Leaving room for off-topic social life. Amazon let regulars run kitchens, BBQs and rant threads, and those threads produced the most loyalty (ILMK).
  5. Recognising volunteer experts. The Kindle Forum Pro badge cost almost nothing and gave Amazon a group of loyal helpers (ILMK).

What they did wrong

  1. No owner, no purpose. The forums were neither a support desk nor a staffed brand community. The Kindle discussion forum ran alongside a separate official Kindle Help forum, and the Gold Box forum turned into an anything-goes "clearinghouse of crazy" (eBook Evangelist; Gizmodo). Consequence: nobody inside Amazon could show what it was worth.
  2. Trolls and inconsistent moderation in the book and politics forums. There were reviewer–author wars, sock puppets and organised one-star "swarms." Anne Rice backed a 2014 petition against the "gangster bullies in the forum" (Guardian, Mar 2014; Anne R. Allen, Oct 2017). Consequence: reputational risk on the product pages and the review system Amazon depends on.
  3. Slow decline by subtraction. Amazon removed the navigation link, search, the General Help forum and the Pro badges, all without announcement (eBook Evangelist; MobileRead). Consequence: activity fell, which then justified closing it.
  4. Letting spam take over at the end. In the final weeks "entire forums [were] taken over by scam/bot threads," including "hundreds of scam posts, fake support numbers" (KBoards; MobileRead). Krebs on Security noted that fake Amazon support numbers were "heavily promoted using Amazon's own online customer support discussion forums" (Krebs, Feb 2018). Consequence: real harm to customers, and a pretext for shutting down.
  5. A botched shutdown. The banner appeared intermittently. Posting was disabled before the stated date, which then moved from Oct 6 to Oct 13, and the forums actually went dark on Oct 6. There was no export, no archive of the general forums, and replies to the closure notice were blocked (eBook Evangelist; KBoards). Good e-Reader called it "an utter debacle" (Good e-Reader, Oct 9 2017). The Vine program's member boards were also closed "in late 2017 without explanation" (Wirecutter, Jun 2018).
  6. Pointing members at a product they didn't want. Amazon sent forum regulars to Spark, which was mobile-only, Prime-only and built around shopping. Spark shut down in June 2019 after its executive sponsor left (The Verge; Business Insider). Consequence: the community scattered to KBoards, MobileRead, Goodreads groups (a "Vine" group calling itself "the original Amazon Vine Voice Forum" has 1,837 members, Goodreads), Talk Classical (Oct 2017), an "Amazon Refugee Forums" site (37,462 posts in 382 topics by 116 members as of its last index, yuble.com, search snippet) and political boards. Amazon kept none of that goodwill.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Put community on the surfaces people already visit (product page, settings screen, in-app help). Placement did more for Amazon than any program did.
  2. Route questions to verified owners. Amazon's later Q&A emailed buyers when someone asked about a product they owned. It's the best seeding mechanic Amazon found, and it works for any app that knows who uses which feature (ManagingCommunities).
  3. Support member-made rituals. Recurring numbered threads, daily deal threads and monthly "what are you working on" polls held the most loyalty. Give them a pinned slot, a name and a host.
  4. Let the community vote on visibility. Helpfulness votes plus hiding low-rated posts by default kept a public forum readable at scale.
  5. Recognise volunteer experts publicly, and back them. A badge plus a direct line to staff (the Forum Pro model) costs very little. Keep the program running, and give it a way to escalate to staff.

Avoid these

  1. Running a space with no owner and no goal. If no team is accountable for a forum's purpose and health, it fills with trolls and spam and then gets cut. Decide up front whether it's support, advocacy or social, and staff it to that.
  2. Shrinking a community bit by bit, then closing it abruptly. Removing search, links and badges without saying so, then closing early with no archive or export, wastes years of goodwill and pushes members to competitors' forums.
  3. Moving members to a product built for your metrics, not their needs. Sending readers and Kindle regulars to a Prime-only shoppable feed (Spark) failed within two years. A migration has to keep what members came for.

Score notes. Retention mechanics 2: email subscriptions and voting existed, but the rituals were member-made. Content engine 1: four staff announcements in six years of the Kindle forum. Member ownership 3: members created over 95% of content and their own institutions, but had no tools or authority. Staff investment 1: thin moderation and a volunteer badge that was later withdrawn. Business impact evidence 1: one descriptive academic study and anecdotes. Durability 2: about 11 years, then shut down with no archive of the general forums.

Related communities worth adding

  • KBoards (formerly KindleBoards) — independent (founded by Amazon Kindle forum members, 2008) — fan-run forum that absorbed Amazon forum refugees; an example of a breakaway space outliving the official one.
  • MobileRead Forums — independent — long-running e-reader enthusiast forum and calibre support home; took in Kindle forum refugees.
  • Amazon Digital and Device Forum (amazonforum.com) — Amazon — staff-moderated successor help forum since Oct 2017; still active.
  • Amazon Customer Questions & Answers — Amazon — structured product Q&A seeded by emailing verified owners; replaced Customer Discussions on high-volume categories.
  • Amazon Spark — Amazon — 2017–2019 shoppable social feed for Prime members; failure.
  • Askville — Amazon — Q&A site, Dec 2006–Oct 2013; failure.
  • Amapedia — Amazon — product wiki, 2006/07–2010; failure.
  • Listmania! / "So You'd Like To…" guides — Amazon — customer-made curation lists, retired around 2013; failure that angered power users.
  • Amazon Vine Voice member forum — Amazon — invite-only reviewer boards closed late 2017; continued as a Goodreads "Vine" group (1,837 members) and r/AmazonVine.
  • Barnes & Noble Nook forums — Barnes & Noble — competitor's e-reader forums, shut down in September 2017, a week before Amazon's.
