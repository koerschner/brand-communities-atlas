TL;DR

  • What it is: Heineken's first public open-innovation portal, ideasbrewery.com. It launched on 3 April 2012 with a 48-hour live stream and ran three or four time-boxed global challenges: sustainable packaging (2012), "Reinvent the draught beer experience" (2012), and a two-stage 60+ Challenge (Dec 2012–Aug 2013). People posted a 500-word pitch with images, rallied votes, and the best ideas went to a co-creation phase and a paid workshop in Amsterdam. In 2014 it was relaunched as the Innovators Brewhouse.
  • Why people came back: mostly they didn't. Participation came in bursts tied to each brief, prize ($5,000–$10,000 in total) and workshop trip. Between challenges the only hook was a newsletter promising the next one. The peak was about 150 ideas in a challenge.
  • What Heineken fed it: strong launch content for every challenge: a designer locked in a room for 48 hours on a live stream, two comedians performing the draught brief, a Hollywood director judging the 60+ insights, a "60-second" design challenge for inspiration, outside expert juries, an Amsterdam workshop for six finalists, and PR from Edelman and Make Amsterdam.
  • Biggest mistake: no internal owner for the output. Heineken's own innovation team later said the portal "had a cumbersome review process and without a direct connection with the internal clients there was little commitment to adopt winning ideas" [R&D Today, 2017]. The 2014 fix ("all ideas welcome") swung the other way and produced a flood of poorly targeted ideas that were costly to evaluate.
  • The one lesson: a co-creation community lasts only if each challenge has an internal "client" who has committed in advance to take the winner forward, and members can see what happened to past winners. Without that it is a marketing contest, and its people leave when the prize money is paid.

What the community is

Ideas Brewery was an age-gated website (you entered date of birth, country and, in the US, state) where Heineken posted one challenge at a time [Univ. of Waterloo SMBP blog, Jun 2013]. The Wayback Machine index of ideasbrewery.com shows how it worked: an AgeGate, Account/Signup and SocialLogin (Facebook), a Dashboard with SubmitIdeaPopup, image Attachment/Upload and Crop, public Idea/Detail pages, vote/Idea and vote/Undo, Jury, News, CaseStudy, Challenge/Previous, and a Home/Tweets widget [Wayback CDX for ideasbrewery.com/*, 2012–2014]. All submissions were public and could be voted on. TriplePundit noted at launch that this was unlike Unilever's private submissions [TriplePundit, Apr 2012].

The spaces around it were thin: @IdeasBrewery on Twitter, a Flickr account, the "heinekeninnovation" YouTube channel, and a LinkedIn group called "HEINEKEN Ideas Brewery" [SMBP, Jun 2013]. No member sizes were found for any of them. A search of the Arctic Shift Reddit archive and Hacker News found no fan or participant discussion. There was no forum, no member profiles worth the name, and nothing members could own.

Origin and strategy

Heineken had been running co-creation pilots on outside platforms for several years: a "walking fridge" sequel video contest on Brandfighters, an "Invent a new Heineken experience" concept contest on eYeka, and the Facebook-driven Heineken Open Design Explorations, which produced "The Club" in Milan in April 2012 [Yannig Roth, Sep 2011; Cool Hunting; Red Dot]. Mark van Iterson, head of brand design, said Heineken had been "crowdsourcing for more than 10 years" and that Ideas Brewery's difference was "a highly formalized and structured approach", with input from design professionals, packaging-materials experts and industrial designers [Packaging World, Aug 2013, snippet]. The agency 8global, which built the platform, describes the brief as "a digital gathering place for a network of global creative thinkers, which would act as an activation point for solving several annual open innovation challenges" [8global case study].

It had three strategic jobs:

  1. Feed the innovation funnel. Ellen Bark(-Lindhout), Innovation Program Manager in Global Commerce Innovation (at Heineken since 2009), said Heineken's growth "relies on high-value innovations" and that great ideas "can come from anywhere, inside or outside the company" [PSFK interview, Mar 2013, via Yannig Roth's translation]. Heineken was targeting an innovation rate (share of revenue from new products) that reached 5.9% and €1.1 billion in 2013 [Heineken FY2013 results].
  2. Sustainability credentials. The first challenge served the 2010 "Brewing a Better Future" packaging targets. Heineken's 2012 sustainability report lists Ideas Brewery next to the internal "Ideas Contest" and the supplier program THINC (33 supplier pitches, seven next-generation ideas) [Heineken Sustainability Report 2012].
  3. Brand engagement. Bark said: "Consumers are more engaged with brands that open up to them, and a co-creation platform like IdeasBrewery.com lets them co-determine Heineken's future" [PSFK via Roth, translated]. Marketing Week tied the activity to Heineken's push, with Iris Worldwide, to be seen as a design-led brand "in a similar way to Apple or Audi", and said much of it "centres on Heineken's Ideas Brewery" [Marketing Week snippets, 2012–13].

The internal sponsors were Alexis Nasard, Chief Commercial Officer, who recorded the site's intro video, and the global innovation team (Bark, Paul Stanger, Bram Westenbrink, François-Xavier Mahot), with R&D director Willem van Waesberghe on the first jury [YouTube; Ottman, Jul 2012; Heineken PR, Aug 2013].

What keeps people coming back

Very little, by design. Every mechanic served a single contest:

  • Time-boxed challenges with a clear brief and deadline gave each wave urgency, but nothing joined one wave to the next. The bridge was a newsletter: "For more information about upcoming Ideas Brewery Challenges, sign up to the Ideas Brewery newsletter" [Heineken PR, Aug 2013].
  • Public voting counted for 25% of the score in the 60+ challenge (innovation, technical feasibility and commercial feasibility made up the other 75%) [SMBP, Jun 2013]. Core77's sponsored post told entrants "The more votes you get, the better your chances are to move to Phase 2!" [Core77, Apr 2012]. That pushed people to recruit friends from outside, not to engage with other entrants.
  • Staged funnel and status. Challenge 1 had a second-stage "closed innovation environment" and prizes for "best co-creators" who improved other people's ideas [Core77; Ottman]. This was the only peer-collaboration mechanic, and it was dropped in favor of a six-person workshop.
  • The Amsterdam workshop was the strongest hook, but only for six people per challenge: flights and hotel paid, two to two-and-a-half days with Heineken innovators and outside coaches, and a pitch to a jury [Heineken PR, Aug 2013; SMBP].
  • Cash and recognition: $5,000–$10,000 per challenge, press releases naming the winners, and the promise of working with Heineken ("The chance to work with HEINEKEN on my creative idea is very exciting" – Tony Dianoff).

Missing: no persistent profile or reputation, no leaderboard across challenges, no member-to-member space, no status for past winners, and above all no visible "what happened to the winning idea" loop.

The content engine

This was the program's real strength. Heineken treated each brief as a launch campaign:

  • "48 Hours of Live Innovation" (3 Apr 2012): designer Janne Kyttänen (Freedom of Creation) was locked in a secret location for 48 hours, live-streamed on ideasbrewery.com, working on the packaging brief [Brandingmag; Godmother Films; LSN, Apr 2012].
  • Comedy briefs for draught: comedians Adam Fields and Samba Schutte turned Heineken's consumer research into a stand-up set released in two waves ("Watch, learn, laugh and be inspired!") [Wayback Page/ComedyVideos, Aug 2012; Dutch Daily News].
  • Insight-first 60+ design: stage one crowdsourced observations of 60–70-year-olds, judged by director Donald Petrie and photographer Naomi Harris, with a documentary as the prize. Stage two asked for ideas built on the three winning insight themes [Packaging Strategies, Apr 2013]. Designers Eva and Cuto of Stone took a "60 seconds" challenge to seed ideas [same].
  • Expert juries as content: green-marketing author Jacquelyn Ottman, Core77 editor LinYee Yuan, Alex Goh (DesignTaxi), Dominic Wilcox and Daniel Quinn (Happen) [Ottman; Heineken PR].
  • Workshop and compilation videos, Edelman-run press releases, and trade-press placements (Core77 posts with "utm_campaign=Heineken2012" links, StudentCompetitions, Packaging World) [Wayback CDX; Core77].

Member content was the pitches themselves (500 words, one to three images, optional PDF or video), shown publicly. Bloggers sometimes curated them (Best in Packaging picked out the "Turn bottle", the "Bottle Mug" and the "Easy Star Bottle") [Best in Packaging, Jul 2013]. Heineken never built a system to build on member work after a challenge closed.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: a small global innovation team (Bark, Stanger, Westenbrink, later Mario Weber), the brand design head (van Iterson), and agencies: 8global (platform), Edelman and Make Amsterdam (PR), Godmother Films (video) [sources above]. Team size: no evidence found.
  • Governance: Heineken wrote every brief, set the criteria and picked the juries. Legal constraints were heavy: an age gate, a country whitelist that excluded California, Quebec and Yukon, and eligibility rules [Packaging World, Apr 2012]. The IP terms in the T&Cs were not found (the page sits behind the age gate in the archive).
  • No ambassador or superuser program. The only roles open to outsiders were finalist and juror.
  • Feedback into the product was the weak point. There was no public status for ideas after the award, and internally no business unit had asked for the ideas. Weber's 2017 post-mortem says so directly [R&D Today].

Growth loops

  • Vote-gathering: entrants promoted their ideas on Facebook and Twitter because votes counted [TriplePundit; SMBP]. This brought in visitors but few contributors.
  • Earned and partner media: design, packaging and student-competition outlets (Core77, Packaging World, Packaging Strategies, DesignTaxi, The Drum, Campaign Brief) covered every launch and award.
  • Geography: reach widened from 12 to 19 countries. India became the largest source of registrations (27%), which says the prize pulled in design students and freelancers more than Heineken drinkers [PSFK via Roth].
  • No referral program, no SEO compounding (the content was age-gated and later lost), and no alumni loop.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none that ties Ideas Brewery to revenue, retention or a launched product. No release was found announcing Fahrenheit +60, the Easy Star Bottle, the Heineken-O-Mat or the draught winner as products. Heineken's own assessment was that the portal "was successful in generating inbound ideas around specific challenges" but saw "little commitment to adopt winning ideas" [Mario Weber, R&D Today, Dec 2017].
  • Company claims: the team said "[We] are very happy with the results of this new crowdsourcing initiative" [SMBP, Jun 2013]. Bark described it as "a global crowdsourcing platform which we have successfully used to generate high quality and viable ideas" [Packaging Strategies, Apr 2013].
  • Context that is often misattributed: a 2018 Harvard student essay credits Heineken's open innovation with "over $1.1 billion in revenue" [HBS Digital Initiative, Nov 2018]. That figure is Heineken's total revenue from all innovations in 2013 (innovation rate 5.9%, driven by products such as Radler) [Heineken FY2013], and it is not attributable to the portal.

What they did right

  1. Tight, insight-led briefs. Each challenge had a narrow question, stated criteria and weights, and a required format. A Waterloo analyst praised it: "they've encouraged creativity while also clearly defining the scope… submissions which are more focussed and more feasible" [SMBP, Jun 2013]. Jury quality backed this up: Ottman called the first batch "a surprising array of quality ideas" [Ottman, Jul 2012].
  2. Crowdsourcing insights before ideas. The two-stage 60+ challenge first collected observations, then asked for ideas built on the winning themes. The winner said he "spent weeks researching the over 60's before I even put pen to paper" [Heineken PR, Aug 2013]. Few brand challenges do this.
  3. Making the brief itself entertaining. The live-streamed 48-hour marathon was the most-watched asset of the whole program (7,417 views) and earned coverage in LSN, Brandingmag and TriplePundit.
  4. Deep in-person co-creation for finalists. The paid Amsterdam workshops turned a pitch into a concept and produced the program's most heartfelt participant quotes.
  5. A candid post-mortem and iteration. Heineken said in public what went wrong, swung to "all ideas welcome" (2014), then settled on "targeted, client-owned challenges" in Innovators Brewhouse 2.0 (2017). That version drew over 500 registrations and 150+ ideas across five live challenges within weeks [R&D Today], and in 2018 produced a winner with a real product tie-in: BruVue's IoT sensor for Heineken's SUB home draught system [Brewbound/BusinessWire, Feb 2018].

What they did wrong

  1. No internal client, so nothing happened to the winners. This is Heineken's own diagnosis [R&D Today]. The consequence: no success stories to show the next wave of entrants, so each challenge started from zero. The only follow-up on Fahrenheit +60 was Paul Stanger's hope "to see if we can bring the idea into reality" [Heineken PR, Aug 2013].
  2. An episodic contest with no community layer. Gaps between challenges, no profiles, no peer space, and rewards for six people at most. It never built a regular group of returning creators; the YouTube numbers (hundreds to a few thousand views) show how small the audience was.
  3. Popularity voting in an expert contest. Votes counted 25%, which rewarded social reach. TriplePundit asked: "is this American Idol style of crowdsourcing a better way to generate new ideas or just a marketing gimmick?" [Apr 2012]. Best in Packaging noted that one public entry (the "Turn bottle") repeated the already-patented Flip Bottle [Jul 2013]. Quality control fell to the jury.
  4. Overcorrecting in the 2014 relaunch. "All ideas welcome" produced "poorly targeted" ideas that were "costly to evaluate and follow up" [R&D Today]. It took until 2017 to find the middle ground.
  5. Letting the asset rot. The ideasbrewery.com domain, printed in every press release, lapsed. By 2025 it served a Turkish casino-affiliate page [Wayback, Apr 2025]. Members' public idea pages, the program's only lasting member-made content, are gone.
  6. Topic choice drew ridicule. The beer blog The BeerCast called the 60+ push "doomed to fail" and "a tacit admission that years of lager marketing has failed" [Sep 2013]. Contests that read as segment-hunting invite this.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make the brief a show. A live-streamed expert attempt, or a comedian explaining the problem, gets people to the starting line better than a rules page. Do it for every challenge.
  2. Crowdsource the insight before the solution. Run a short "tell us what you see" round, publish the themes that win, then ask for ideas built on them. It raises idea quality and gives non-designers a way in.
  3. Publish the criteria and their weights (innovation, feasibility, commercial fit) so entrants self-select and judges can explain their decisions.
  4. Give finalists a deep in-person (or live) co-creation session with your real product team. It is the most memorable thing you can offer and produces your best advocates.
  5. Write your post-mortem in public and iterate on the format. Heineken's path from one-off contests, to an open inbox, to client-owned challenges is a good roadmap.

Avoid these

  1. Running challenges nobody inside has asked for. Before launch, name the product owner who will take the winner forward, and publish a status ("in pilot", "shelved, and why") for every past winner. That loop is what brings creators back.
  2. Letting popularity votes decide merit, or running "all ideas welcome" inboxes you can't afford to triage. Use votes for discovery and staff for selection.
  3. Treating the community as a campaign microsite. Keep profiles, archives and the domain alive after the program ends. Losing them wipes out members' work and can hand your URL to spammers.

Score notes. Retention 2: votes, stages and a newsletter, but nothing carried over between challenges. Content 3: inventive launch content every time, little curation afterwards. Member ownership 1: Heineken wrote the briefs, judged, and controlled everything. Staff investment 3: a dedicated innovation team, agencies, juries, paid workshops. Business-impact evidence 1: no product or revenue tied to it, and the company's own review says the winners weren't adopted. Durability 1: about two years, then a rebrand, and the domain is now spam.
