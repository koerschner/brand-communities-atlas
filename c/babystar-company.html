TL;DR

  • What it is. Oyatsu Company's free fan community for Baby Star Ramen (ベビースターラーメン, the crunchy noodle snack sold since 1959). It runs on the Commune white-label community platform as a web site (opened 1 Feb 2024) and an app (1 Aug 2024). Members "join the company" (入社) as "employees" (社員), "clock in" (出社) by logging in, earn points as "salary" (給料) and get "promoted" up a corporate ladder.
  • Why people come back. A promotion ladder (主任 → 係長 → 課長 → 次長 → 部長), where each promotion triggers a physical gift box, a clear file and a "promotion certificate" in the post. Members also get regular chances to vote on or pitch real products. Fans proudly post their promotions on X.
  • What the company does to feed it. Flavour-idea calls and votes, two in-person "ラー麺バー" (ramen-member) fan meetings with R&D staff present, prize campaigns, and cross-brand co-creation with another Commune community, the BAQET bakery restaurants' fan club "バケット広場". The output was real: two fan-made flavours on national shelves (June 2025) and three rounds of fan-voted or fan-invented restaurant menus.
  • The biggest mistake. Scale and dependence. The brand is known by 97% of Japanese consumers, yet the community peaked at about 3,000 members. Every touchpoint sits on a single vendor's platform, so when Commune was breached in October 2026 the community went offline for more than a month, and no Oyatsu notice explaining why could be found. It also replaced an earlier ambassador program (2021–2023) that nobody publicly wound down.
  • The one lesson. A playful identity frame (members as "employees") plus visible co-creation and tangible rewards for status can make a few thousand fans very loyal. To get more than PR anecdotes out of it, you need scale, data to measure impact, and control of your own platform.

What the community is

Baby Star Company (ベビースターカンパニー) is described as "a dreamlike, laid-back company that thinks only about snacks" (landing page). Fans register and post Baby Star products they spot in shops ("patrol" finds), new ways to eat it and product ideas. They comment and like, and earn points for logging in and posting, which they trade for digital novelties (landing page; Spuron interview, Jun 2025).

  • Where. A Commune-hosted web community (babystartest.commmune.com, with babystar.jp as its branded domain) plus a native iOS/Android app (Google Play listing). The community has tabs called "Challenge" (チャレンジ), "Bonus" (ボーナス) and "Snack Plaza" (おやつの広場) (Jan 2025 PR).
  • Who runs it. Oyatsu Company's marketing team, with a "Baby Star Company management office" (運営事務局); members say they ask it for permission before posting rewards on X (member post, Feb 2025). The contact address is babystar_ambassador@oyatsu.co.jp, a holdover from the predecessor program (Google Play). Commune, the vendor, provides operational support and co-markets the community (Commune news, Dec 2024).
  • Unofficial spaces. No fan-run forum, subreddit or Discord turned up. The only spillover is a few members posting under #ベビースターカンパニー on X (an X API full-archive search for the community name and "麺バー" returned 88 posts between Dec 2023 and Oct 2026, a few of them unrelated ramen-shop posts; the relevant ones are mostly sweepstakes replies, one member's daily snack photos, and a few superfans announcing promotions).
  • Status today (2026-11-07). Offline. Commune's whole platform was breached on 5–6 Oct 2026 and all communities were put into maintenance (Commune notice, 9 Oct 2026). From 16 Oct 2026 communities reopened one by one, web-only, with apps and email sign-up disabled, and only for client companies that asked to reopen (Commune, 16 Oct 2026). On 7 Nov 2026 babystar.jp and the community home both redirect to commmune.com/static/brand-maintenance.html, and Oyatsu's landing page shows the "join" button greyed out with "※臨時メンテナンス中" (temporary maintenance), checked directly. A member noticed: "Baby Star Company has been like this for ages, what happened?" (X, 19 Oct 2026). Oyatsu does not appear on public lists of companies confirming leaked member data (piyolog roundup; Security Measures Lab list), so whether its members' data leaked is unknown.

Origin and strategy

  • Stated rationale. Baby Star has 97% brand awareness and 92% of consumers have eaten it (launch PR, Dec 2023, via CNET Japan). Oyatsu had talked to fans through its home page and social accounts, but "as lifestyles diversify" it wanted "a new point of contact with each Baby Star fan and a deeper relationship" (Spuron). Commune's announcement is blunter: the community was set up "to accelerate customer loyalty and discover customer insights" (ロイヤル化の加速や顧客インサイトの発見) (Commune, 11 Dec 2023).
  • Business model link. A packaged snack has no subscription to retain, so the logic is (a) co-creating limited editions that come with a ready-made story ("ファンと作った味", "a flavour made with fans", printed on the pack), (b) fan-sourced ideas for the brand's long-running collaboration program "#こんなとこにもベビースター" (#Baby Star in a place like this?!), and (c) loyalty among heavy users. The Spuron writer, a marketing outlet reviewing it, put the hoped-for mechanism plainly: knowing a product's back-story makes you pick it up in store, and "you can't betray your oshi (favourite), so you reach for other snacks less" (Spuron).
  • Predecessor. The "employee" community replaced the Baby Star Ambassador program. That program ran from at least May 2021 to 2023 on the ambassadors.jp platform: fans linked their Twitter account, posted with #ベビースターアンバサダー to earn points, ranked up and received rank-specific digital member cards and novelties, and "participation in product development" was listed as "being planned" (May 2021 PR; Wayback capture, Apr 2023). The new community reused that program's recruitment copy word for word ("とにかくベビースターが大好き！ / みんなにもオススメしたい！"). Its URL now returns an error page. In short, Oyatsu moved fans from a social-amplification program (posting on Twitter) to an owned space where it could run co-creation. An even earlier 2018 cohort of recipe-blogger "ambassadors" was influencer marketing, not a community (blogger post, Jul 2018).
  • Champion. No named executive owner was found; the Spuron interview quotes only "the person in charge" (担当者). Character work around mascot Hoshio-kun is run by the marketing division (Tokyo Character Forum).

What keeps people coming back

  1. The role-play frame. Everything is office vocabulary. Registering is "joining the company", logging in is "clocking in", points are "salary", prize draws are "employee benefits" (福利厚生), the selection exam is "everyone passes", the workplace is "anywhere with internet" and the job title is "Baby Star lead for marketing / R&D / sales" (Jan 2025 PR). Members are also "麺バー/ラー麺バー" (a pun on メンバー, member). A marketer reviewing it singled this out: "registering is called 'joining the company'... I felt a sense of team and 'we're building it together'" (X, Jun 2025).
  2. Promotion ladder with physical rewards. Points from logins and posts promote you from 主任 (chief) to 係長 to 課長 (section manager) and up (Google Play). Members' posts show the ladder runs at least to おやつ次長 (deputy GM) and おやつ部長 (department head). Promotions arrive in the post: a Hoshio-kun clear file, a promotion certificate (昇格証書) and a box of snacks (X, Feb 2025; X, Feb 2025). One member got two boxes at once after skipping a rank (X, Jan 2026). This is the strongest retention hook on record: the same members announce each step months apart (課長 Feb 2025 → 次長 Apr 2025 → 部長 Sep 2025).
  3. Real co-creation with visible outcomes. Members suggested regional ramen flavours (32 ideas), voted them down to two, tasted prototypes with the R&D staff who made them, gave feedback that changed the recipes (more corn and butter for Sapporo miso; more chive aroma for Taiwan mazesoba), and voted on the package design. The products launched nationally on 2 June 2025 after about a year of development (PR TIMES). Even members whose ideas lose get, in the Spuron writer's words, "a sense of ownership, 'I gave my input on the new flavour'" (Spuron).
  4. IRL fan meetings. Two "ラー麺バー" meetings: a tasting plus ideas workshop (Nov 2024), then a new-product discussion plus a preview of a karaoke-chain collab menu (Jul 2025). Both ended with a mascot surprise (Hoshio-kun, then Butamen-kun) and photos (Commune; Gourmet Press). Attendees said they loved meeting other fans and hearing developers' struggles.
  5. Small, frequent rewards. Digital wallpapers (an "Oekakisan" illustrator collab given to every member), anniversary votes with snack-box prizes, and app-download draws (Jan 2025 PR; Aug 2024 PR).
  6. Peer content. Members post "today's snack" and arrangement recipes that other members copy (Aug 2024 PR; X, Sep 2026).

No evidence was found of streaks, leaderboards, peer moderators or regular weekly rituals. Cadence is set by campaigns, not programming.

The content engine

  • Company-made content. An announcements feed ("one box for all the Baby Star info that gets buried elsewhere"; Google Play), calls for ideas, polls, quizzes, prize campaigns and event recruiting. Outside the community Oyatsu runs a large collaboration program (#こんなとこにもベビースター reached its 22nd collab by July 2025; PR TIMES), plus the year-long "さぁ、おやつの時間だ！" program from Baby Star Day, 2 Aug 2026 (PR TIMES), recipe pages, a YouTube channel and the Baby Star Land shops. The community is fed by those programs rather than having its own editorial calendar. No newsletter, webinar or how-to series was found.
  • Member-made content. Recipes and arrangements are the main user-generated content. They are encouraged and harvested: fan recipe culture is the stated reason for the BAQET collab ("a fan culture of using Baby Star in cooking has taken root"; Commune). In September 2025, member "ツーゥさん"'s idea became the Baby Star Marron Danish at BAQET restaurants nationwide, and the reasons it was chosen were published (PR TIMES). Members also post fan art of the mascots (X).
  • PR flywheel. Nearly every community milestone becomes a PR TIMES release (launch, app, anniversary, co-created product, fan-meeting report, collabs), and Commune re-publishes each as its own case marketing (Commune news). The community is as much a PR asset as a retention one.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. The team size is not disclosed. A "management office" runs moderation and rewards, and R&D staff "rushed in from far away" for the first fan meeting (Commune). Commune provides launch-to-activation support (Commune).
  • Rules. Membership terms and a privacy policy are hosted on babystar.jp (landing page links). Members ask permission before posting rewards externally, which suggests a light-touch but controlled culture (X).
  • Superusers. The "employee" ranks are the superuser program, earned through activity rather than applications. No moderator or host roles for members were found.
  • Feedback loop. Product feedback is direct and visible: idea call, vote, tasting, recipe change, launch. The company says it wants to make co-creation "a continuing mechanism rather than a one-off", including "participation in the development process" (Spuron).

Growth loops

  • Owned and earned channels. Every release ends with an employee "recruitment ad". The brand's X account (10.6K posts) ran follow-and-repost draws for the 1st anniversary, which pulled in sweepstakes accounts ("I've joined the company! Hoping to win"; X, Jan 2025).
  • Referrals. 100 points for both the referrer and the new member, from Aug 2024 (PR TIMES).
  • Packaging. Co-created products carry "ファンと作った味" on the front and mention the community on the back, so the product itself advertises the community (Spuron).
  • Cross-community. The BAQET collab, which grew out of a February 2025 Commune client event, exposed each brand's fans to the other community, and the partnership repeated in July 2025, September 2025 and August 2026 (PR TIMES, Jul 2026).
  • Limits. Despite all this, membership stayed around 3,000, which suggests the loops brought prize-seekers and a small core of superfans rather than broad reach.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. Oyatsu is private. There are no disclosed sales for the co-created products, no retention or purchase-frequency comparison and no NPS. (Contrast Kewpie, which publishes 8.3 bottles a year for fan-club members against 6 for ordinary consumers; AppUnity.)
  • Company claims. "Many responses right after launch" and warm comments from voters (Spuron). The AppUnity roundup's "results" for Baby Star Company list only the kinds of ideas submitted (AppUnity).
  • Outputs delivered. 2 national limited flavours (Jun 2025), 3 fan-voted restaurant menu items (Jul–Aug 2025), 1 fan-invented bread (Sep 2025), a karaoke-chain collab menu tested at a fan meeting (Aug 2025), and a joint quiz program (2026). Also, a TV Tokyo "Cambria Palace" feature on Oyatsu (Feb 2026) mentions gathering fans for tastings as part of the brand's strategy (TV Tokyo Plus).

Score justifications: retention mechanics 4 (a well-built role-play status ladder with physical rewards, co-creation and IRL events, but no daily ritual); content engine 2 (campaign announcements, not programming); member ownership 3 (real influence on flavours, packs and menus, but no member-led spaces or roles); staff investment 3 (R&D at events, gifts mailed per promotion, frequent PR, but a small undisclosed team on a rented platform); business impact evidence 1 (anecdotes only); durability 2 (2.7 years old, a predecessor program quietly replaced, and the vendor outage in Oct 2026).

What they did right

  1. A sticky identity metaphor applied consistently. The "company" frame runs through sign-up, rewards, ranks, member names and even the event name. Members adopt it in public ("ベビースターカンパニーのおやつ次長、ミント☆です"; X, Aug 2025).
  2. Status you can hold in your hand. Mailing a certificate and a snack box at each promotion turns invisible points into something worth photographing, and each post advertises the community (X).
  3. Closing the co-creation loop in public. From idea call to shelf in about a year, with fan-tasting changes spelled out, "made with fans" printed on the pack, and the rejected ideas acknowledged (Kitakata, Toyama Black, Katsuura tantanmen and others; Spuron).
  4. Putting R&D in the room. Fans valued hearing "the developers' struggles" and being listened to (Commune event report).
  5. Creative cross-brand co-creation. Pairing two client communities on the same platform gave each a fresh project and new members, and has repeated for over a year (Commune).
  6. Upgrading from Twitter amplification to an owned space. The ambassador program could only collect hashtags; the community gives Oyatsu voting, tasting and first-party relationships (Wayback, 2023).

What they did wrong

  1. It stayed small. About 3,000 members for a brand known to 97% of the country, and no growth figure after mid-2025. Sweepstakes-driven recruitment (anniversary follow/repost draws) brings registrations, not regulars. Consequence: co-creation rests on a few dozen to a few hundred active voters (264 votes across two communities for the BAQET menu).
  2. Single-vendor dependence and silence during the outage. Web, app, domain and member data all sit on Commune. After the October 2026 breach the community went into maintenance, Commune reopened communities only on request and web-only, and a month later Baby Star Company was still offline with sign-ups disabled. No Oyatsu notice explaining the outage could be found on its landing page or in news searches, and members were left guessing (X, Oct 2026). This came after Oyatsu's own March 2025 breach exposed about 170,000 campaign winners' names, addresses and phone numbers (2012–Feb 2025) (ITmedia), which makes fan-data trust a sensitive topic for this brand.
  3. Churned programs without a visible handover. The 2021 ambassador program promised rank-ups and "product development participation", then disappeared (its page now errors) and was relaunched under a new name and platform. Ambassadors' accumulated rank did not visibly carry over, and the shared mailbox name is the only trace.
  4. A thin content calendar. Without weekly programming, activity spikes around campaigns, and the visible X spillover is dominated by one or two superfans and sweepstakes replies.
  5. No measurement shared, possibly none collected. Unlike peers (Kewpie's purchase uplift, Otafuku's cooking-frequency survey, Nippn's sales gain from a fan-voted tagline; AppUnity), Oyatsu has published nothing showing the community pays back. That makes it vulnerable to budget cuts.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give members a role, not just an account. A coherent metaphor (employees, a crew, a guild) applied to every touchpoint (sign-up, points, ranks, events) gives fans an identity they will state in public.
  2. Make status physical at milestones. A mailed certificate or small box at each rank-up is cheap and gets photographed and shared; points alone do not.
  3. Run co-creation end to end and show the trail. Idea call → vote → tasting with the people who build it → visible changes → "made with members" on the product → credit the winner by name and acknowledge the runners-up.
  4. Put builders in the room. Developers at IRL meetings turn feedback into a relationship and give members insider stories to retell.
  5. Partner communities with each other. A joint project with a complementary brand's community gives both a reason to return and cross-pollinates members.

Avoid these

  1. Don't rent your entire community without an exit and incident plan. Own your domain, member list, export path and a status channel. When the vendor goes down, tell members yourself, quickly.
  2. Don't recruit with sweepstakes and call it growth. Follow/repost prize draws bring registrations that don't become regulars. Measure active members and voters, not sign-ups.
  3. Don't launch without a measurement plan, and don't let programs lapse quietly. Track purchase or retention lift from day one, and when you replace a program, carry members' status across and say what happened.
