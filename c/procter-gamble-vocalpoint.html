TL;DR

  • What it is. Vocalpoint is a panel of mothers that Procter & Gamble screened for big social networks. It went national in March 2006 and had 600,000 members within weeks. P&G's in-house word-of-mouth unit, Tremor, ran it and sold it to P&G brands and outside clients. It still exists, but as a general product-testing site run by Tremor, which is now an independent company.
  • Why people came back. A steady stream of full-size samples and high-value coupons, extra coupons to hand to friends, and the feeling that "they care what you have to say." Coming back was transactional. Members returned for the next box, not for each other.
  • What the company fed it. Tremor researched a "reason to care" for each product, separate from the TV message ("Mom, can I help?" for Dawn Direct Foam), and mailed talking-point kits. It also sent a weekly newsletter (The Inside Track) and ran surveys, polls, message boards, lifestyle articles, house parties and, later, prototype trials and shops.
  • Biggest mistake. P&G refused to make members disclose the P&G connection. That drew an FTC complaint and a "stealth army" BusinessWeek headline in 2006. Later the network let deal blogs and affiliate links fill it with sample-seekers, which wore down the "connector" idea and the community content until the home page became a feed of outside links.
  • The lesson. Giving advocates something exclusive to share, plus a story built for conversation, measurably moves sales. But a panel held together only by free goods hollows out once the free goods slow down or the parent company loses interest.

What the community is

Vocalpoint (vocalpoint.com) is a membership network that Procter & Gamble created through its word-of-mouth business unit, Tremor. Tremor itself launched in 2001 as a teen panel [1][3][16]. Vocalpoint targeted mothers, which P&G defined as women with children aged 19 or under. It recruited them with banner ads on sites such as iVillage, then screened them for "connector" behaviour. BusinessWeek reported that Vocalpoint moms speak with 25 to 30 other women a day, against about five for an average mom [1]. Members got samples, coupons, a weekly newsletter and surveys. In return they were asked to try products, tell friends and report back [1][2].

Over 20 years it has gone through four phases:

  • 2005-2010, the "connector army". Campaigns ran through mailed kits and offline conversation. Steve Knox ran Tremor and Vocalpoint until he retired in October 2010 [1][2][20].
  • 2008-2014, the mom community site. Message boards, activities, polls, articles, daily tips, member-submitted stories, house parties and a Twitter account. The footer read "© Procter & Gamble ... Vocalpoint is brought to you by Tremor" [11][12][13].
  • About 2014-2019, leaving P&G. In January 2014 tremor.com redirected to LinkedIn. By 2015 its pages said "© 2015 Tremor" with no P&G mark [21]. Tremor's LinkedIn page now says "After 10+ years of proven results inside of P&G, TREMOR is now ... a highly entrepreneurial, independent company" [22]. Tremor's owner, Geoff Schroeder, ran it as a P&G division (CMO 2011-12, CEO 2012-13), was president of the agency MKTG from 2014 to 2015, then ran "Tremor (part of the Dentsu Aegis Network)" before owning it outright [23]. The exact transaction terms are not public (no evidence found). P&G still owns the VOCALPOINT trademark, renewed in 2019 [24]. That suggests a licence, though this is an inference.
  • 2020-today, the "product-lover's playground". Anyone 13 or older can join, so it is no longer moms only. It offers "Real-Life Research" (surveys, concept tests, prototype trials, live research, "Spark Tank" co-creation sessions), "Insider Shopping" (The Find and ShopLab storefronts) and "Influencing Others" (reviews, content creation, sharing) [4][5]. Tremor LLC owns and operates it under the trade name Vocalpoint [6]. Tremor now sells four such panels: Vocalpoint for women and moms, OneFrontPorch for boomers, NewHeartlandSpeaks for millennials and UnRatoJuntas for US Hispanics. It also runs a paid-influencer network [7].

Unofficial spaces. No dedicated subreddit or fan forum was found. The real unofficial layer was the mom deal-blog circuit (Deal Seeking Mom, Amy Loves It, Money Saving Mom and others). Those blogs pushed Vocalpoint sign-ups, sometimes through affiliate links [14][15][25]. Reddit mentions are sparse and mostly about P&G's separate brandSAMPLER program [26].

Origin and strategy

P&G set out to "bring real science and scale" to word of mouth [16]. Tremor's early lessons shaped Vocalpoint:

  1. Recruit "trend spreaders", not trendsetters, because trendsetters want to be different and don't spread things.
  2. Never reuse the mass-media message.
  3. Build every campaign on three platforms: "Get it first", "Inside scoop" and "Influence power" [16].

Teens were the wrong audience for a company whose buyers are mostly mothers: 80% of Tremor's work was for outside clients such as music and movie studios [1][2]. So in 2005 P&G built a moms version. It ran tests in Columbus, Tulsa and Buffalo, recruited at scale from December 2005 and went national in March 2006 [1][17]. Knox reported to P&G Global Marketing Officer Jim Stengel. He planned for about half of Vocalpoint's business to come from outside clients, with P&G brands getting right of first refusal in each category [2].

The business model was a service, not a subscription. Brands paid Tremor to reach the panel [2][9]. Keeping members engaged was a cost of running that service. Knox said outside clients mattered partly because "maintaining the interest of panelists" requires variety [2]. P&G also used the panel to learn "what Mom would say" (Cincinnati Business Courier, July 2009) [27]. By 2011, Vocalpoint Voice, a 5,000-member online research community, was sold to clients for insight work [9].

Trajectory. The panel hit its size target within weeks of launch and held at a claimed 500,000-600,000 through 2011. Vocalpoint has published no member counts since. On-site engagement visibly thinned between 2013 and 2019. The site still runs programs today: a Gillette men's shaving trial in 2022 and another Gillette project in 2025 [32][19].

What keeps people coming back

  • A drip of free goods, on a variable schedule. Members describe a "steady stream" of full-size products and high-value coupons, and their delight that "this free stuff keeps coming" [1][14][15]. Gaps between "try and tell" campaigns could run "a couple of weeks", and the site's articles were there to fill them [14]. This was the main hook, and it is not a community bond.
  • Being heard. "They care what you have to say. That's what I like most," a Vocalpoint mom told Time in 2007 [8]. The Inside Track asked members their opinion on everything down to the newsletter's own design [1]. Walter Carl of Northeastern called the motive "social currency": inside access and the sense that companies care [8].
  • Something to give away. Kits carried a dozen $1.50 coupons and a novelty item, such as a child's-foot sponge for Dawn [1]. A connector became the "coupon lady" at work [1], so sharing raised her standing instead of making her look like a shill.
  • Participation raises your odds of selection. The FAQ says completing surveys is "the best way to qualify for new programs ... the more you put into VP, the more you get out of it" [5]. The home page adds: "The more you share your product experiences with others, the more opportunities will be available to you" [4]. Members picked this up: "participate in their polls and discussions ... I try to pay attention to the emails" [25]. This is the closest Vocalpoint came to a progression system. No evidence was found of points, badges or tiers. A 2013 page titled "Give More. Get More." hints at a reciprocity program, but its contents weren't captured [12].
  • Recognition and hosting. From 2012 to 2014, members could host "Have You Tried This Yet?" RSVParties with party kits and icebreaker games. The site had a "Featured Hostess" slot, bylined member stories ("Submitted by: Mary Lynn C.") and a live feed of other members' actions [12][33][34].
  • Co-creation. Early "Activities" had members vote on things like the design of a new PUR water bottle, then mailed them the winner [11]. The 2020 site has members pick "top styles, fragrances, features or flavors" and join multi-week Spark Tank sessions [4].

What was missing: peer-to-peer ties. The message boards were mostly product reactions and lifestyle prompts. No evidence was found of rituals, member-run groups, superusers or relationships with staff that would make leaving cost anything beyond losing the samples.

The content engine

  • Talkable messages built from research. For each campaign, Tremor did "tremendous research ... to find the reason to care." The word-of-mouth message was deliberately different from the advertising. Dawn's TV ads stressed grease-cutting, but the Vocalpoint kit said "Mom, can I help?" and framed the soap as fun for kids, tapping an existing conversation among moms about chores [1]. Tremor's 2003 playbook was the same: give an exclusive, a sneak peek, or real influence [16].
  • Newsletters. The Inside Track went out weekly in 2006 [1]. Bloggers in 2009-2011 described a weekly newsletter "full of tips from other moms" [35]. By 2020 it was monthly [5].
  • Editorial site, 2008-2014. It had sections such as Health & Beauty, Food & Recipes, Around the House and My Family & Me, plus articles, surveys, message boards, a "We're listening" page and a daily tip [11][12]. Sponsored content programs ran too, such as a 10-week program around the book What I Know in 2009 [36].
  • Member-made content. Bylined member stories (recipes, decorating tips), photo uploads and product reviews, with Sharing Guidelines on what members could post [12]. Bloggers who were members posted reviews and giveaways tagged #sponsored [12][34]. The 2020 site pushes reviews with photos and video because "the ones with pictures or videos attached are always at the top of the list" [4].
  • Decay. By 2019 the home page mostly linked out to other publishers' articles (Good Housekeeping, Babble, Allure) [13]. The newest blog posts date from 2020 [30].

People, programs and governance

  • Leadership. Steve Knox was general manager and later CEO of Tremor and Vocalpoint from about 2000 to 2010. He retired in October 2010 after 33 years at P&G [2][20]. P&G marketing director Chris Laird was named Tremor CEO in November 2010 [21]. Geoff Schroeder was CMO and then CEO from 2011 to 2013, and now owns the company [23]. Team size at the peak: no evidence found.
  • Selection. Members were screened at sign-up. Many applicants were turned away ("it told me I didn't meet their requirements") [25]. Campaigns were matched by profile, and today you only see the opportunities you qualify for [5].
  • Payment policy. Members were deliberately not paid, which Knox called "crucial to the program maintaining credibility" [2]. By 2020, some programs paid gift cards [5], and Tremor separately runs a paid-influencer network [7].
  • Disclosure. In 2006, P&G left it to each connector to decide whether to mention the affiliation. It noted that the coupons were marked "Vocalpoint." That put it at odds with the Word of Mouth Marketing Association's ethics code [1][8]. By 2013, member posts carried #sponsored and the site had "Vocalpoint Consumer Guidelines" [12]. That followed the FTC's 2006 staff letter, which said material connections "should generally be disclosed" [18], and the FTC's 2009 revision of its Endorsement Guides [37].
  • Feedback into products. Members fed P&G and client product work directly through surveys, design votes and prototype tests [4][11]. This was the most real form of "voice" the program offered.
  • Data. The 2018 privacy policy allows the collection of biometrics in research studies and of data from commercial aggregators [6]. It's legal and disclosed, but it reflects a panel-as-asset mindset.

Growth loops

  1. Paid acquisition plus screening. Banner ads on parenting sites fed the screener [1]. That is how the panel reached 600,000 in about three months [2][1].
  2. Coupons as referral tokens. Each kit carried extra coupons for friends. Connectors talked to about 25-30 women a day, so reach multiplied [1].
  3. Deal-blog syndication. On 13 November 2009 several mom deal blogs promoted Vocalpoint on the same day. A reader asked, "Are you all being paid to promote this by Procter & Gamble?" [25][35]. At least one blogger later disclosed an affiliate link for sign-ups [15]. This brought in volume, but it brought sample-seekers, not screened connectors.
  4. Members invite friends. "Invite a Friend" (2008) and refer-a-friend messages (2018 policy) [11][6].

Evidence of business impact

All of these figures come from the company and are unaudited:

  • Dawn Direct Foam, October 2005: unit sales in the three test markets were double those in markets without Vocalpoint [1]. A later summary called these "double-digit sales increases in test markets" [10].
  • Febreze Air Effects, July 2005: sales were 17% higher [1].
  • Tremor teens, 2003: a TV show's ratings were up 171% after a script-teaser mailing, and a new store's sales were up 30% against control stores, with each teen telling about nine friends [16].
  • Client demand: WD-40 used Vocalpoint rather than advertising for its No-Mess Pen launch [2]. BillMyParents, a public company, bought a campaign in 2011 [9][38]. More than 10 P&G brands were working on national campaigns in 2006 [1].
  • Independent research: Keller Fay and P&G found that about 90% of word of mouth happens offline [8]. Walter Carl's BzzAgent studies found that agents who disclosed their affiliation gained credibility [8][39]. A Columbia field study of a comparable panel (SheSpeaks, not Vocalpoint) found that seeding campaigns "compare very favorably" with print and newspaper inserts on coupon redemption [40]. No peer-reviewed study of Vocalpoint itself was found.
  • Strategic signal: P&G ran Tremor as a business unit for more than 10 years, then let it go independent [22]. That suggests the capability was worth more as an outside agency than as a core P&G asset. This is an inference.

What they did right

  1. Designed the message for conversation, not repetition. "Mom, can I help?" beat a repeat of the TV claim, and Tremor's playbook said so explicitly [1][16].
  2. Recruited for network position. Screening for "connectors" and "trend spreaders" is why 600,000 members could plausibly reach millions [1][16].
  3. Gave advocates a gift to share. Extra coupons turned members into generous friends rather than salespeople [1].
  4. Measured with control markets. Test-vs-control sales comparisons made word of mouth fundable inside P&G [1][16].
  5. Gave members real influence over products. Design votes, prototype tests and Spark Tanks give members a reason to engage beyond freebies [4][11].
  6. Survived 20 years. Few brand panels from 2006 still recruit and run programs [4][19].

What they did wrong

  1. The no-disclosure stance. Commercial Alert filed an FTC complaint about Tremor in October 2005. BusinessWeek called the moms a "stealth army." Knox's argument that not requiring disclosure was the "high road" broke with WOMMA's code [1][17]. The FTC's 2006 guidance pointed the other way [18], and P&G later adopted #sponsored [12]. Consequence: lasting reputational baggage. SourceWatch and PR Watch still frame the program as stealth marketing [41].
  2. Letting deal-seekers in. Blog and affiliate promotion brought in volume. It also produced suspicion ("are you all being paid"), anger at rejections ("that stinks") and claims of being "scammed" [25]. All of that undercut the premium "connector" positioning.
  3. No member-to-member layer. Retention depended on shipments. When content shrank to a curated link feed in 2019 [13], nothing social held people.
  4. Ownership churn and drift. The program moved from P&G to an agency network to a small independent company [22][23]. Over the same years it went from moms to everyone 13 and up [5], and from free trials to shops and paid influencers [4][7]. Each shift diluted what the community was for.
  5. Friction and broken promises lately. In June 2020 a reviewer complained that the FAQ required an account. In November 2025 a reviewer and a neighbour each said the promised Gillette-trial incentive never arrived, along with "very poor communication" [19]. With only two reviews this is anecdotal, but it shows how trust erodes in a points-free, reward-based panel.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Write a separate "reason to talk" for advocates. Find the conversation your users already have, such as "kids don't do chores," and hand them a hook that fits it. Don't hand them your ad copy [1][16].
  2. Give advocates something to give. Invite codes, extra trials or perks for friends make sharing generous rather than salesy [1].
  3. Recruit and rank by reach and responsiveness. Screen for connectors, and make it visible that participation raises a member's chances of getting the next opportunity [1][5].
  4. Offer real influence. Votes on design, prototype access and small co-creation groups give people a reason to stay beyond free goods [4][11].
  5. Measure against a control. Without test-vs-control markets, Vocalpoint could not have proved its worth [1].

Avoid these

  1. Don't hide the sponsorship. Make disclosure the default from day one. It's the law's direction, and research suggests it can add credibility [8][18][39].
  2. Don't let freebie-hunters define the community. Affiliate and deal-site acquisition fills the funnel with people who leave when the free goods stop [25].
  3. Don't build a panel and call it a community. Without peer ties, rituals or member status, engagement decays the moment the company's investment drops, as Vocalpoint's 2019 home page shows [13].
