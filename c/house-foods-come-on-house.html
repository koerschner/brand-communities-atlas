TL;DR

  • What it is: House Foods Group's free member site (comeon-house.jp), launched January 2020 on top of an earlier health-content club, House Club Wellness (2018). It had 326,964 members by November 2025. It combines prize campaigns, editorial content, member surveys and topic comment boards. In November 2025 House added a "Co-creation Community Lab" and, in January 2026, its first brand fan club, "Tongari-tai" (とんがり隊) for Tongari Corn (press release).
  • Why people come back: mostly prizes. About 35 lottery campaigns a year, daily-play anniversary games, and comment boards where posting often enters you into a draw or doubles your odds. Topics that offered a prize drew 1,294 comments on average, against 700 for topics that didn't (my count of all 93 Talk Room topics).
  • What House feeds it: a steady flow of company-made content: about 250 "from House" articles, 12 member-survey reports, monthly comment prompts, and product votes whose winners actually reach shelves. Two examples are the Tongari Corn Butter Soy Sauce re-release (January 2024, after 27,509 votes) and Fruiche Sweets Caramel Banana (July 2023, after a 3,890-person survey) (Yorozoo News; from House #248).
  • Biggest mistake: it calls itself a "community site" but behaves like a broadcast-and-sweepstakes list. Of 94,800 Talk Room comments, I found only 8 identifiable staff posts. Members can't reply to each other or like posts, and in 2021 an operator promised to "consider" adding likes and comments, which never shipped. Survey turnout fell from 8,418 (2021) to 3,353 (2023) even as membership grew, and the Open Data survey series stopped.
  • The lesson: a big incentivized list is cheap to build and useful for research, but it doesn't turn into a community on its own. House's 2025–26 move is the right one: small, named, brand-specific groups with face-to-face developer access, built on top of the list. Whether it works is still unproven.

What the community is

Come on House (カモンハウス, comeon-house.jp) is the group-wide member site of House Foods Group Inc., the Tokyo-listed maker of Vermont Curry, Java Curry, Tongari Corn, Fruiche, GABAN spices and Ukon no Chikara. Registration is free and needs only a nickname and an email address; since March 2021 members can also sign in with X, Facebook or LINE (SNS login campaign; FAQ). The site has no forum threads, no direct messages, no member profiles that others can see, no badges or levels, and no likes or replies. Unofficial spaces: I found no fan-run subreddit, Discord or forum (searches and the Arctic Shift Reddit archive turned up nothing). The visible outside activity is sweepstakes bloggers and sweepstakes aggregators (Koubo, Ken-kaku, Kensho Shufu) re-posting Come on House campaigns (Koubo; Kensho Shufu).

Origin and strategy

  • Predecessor (2018–2020). "House Club Wellness" (ハウスクラブウェルネス) opened in August 2018 as a free health-information member site with "member-only campaigns every month". It ran on Salesforce's community platform (blogger's 2020 description; Wayback copy, Apr 2019; 5,000-winner sign-up campaign, Feb 2019). When Come on House launched, members were moved across with a password reset (FAQ; old login page). The first Open Data survey (6,683 respondents) was fielded in October–November 2019, before launch, so a panel already existed on day one (Open Data #001).
  • Launch (15 January 2020). House opened it with big prizes, including Tokyo Disneyland invitations (sweepstakes blog, 20 Jan 2020; X, 17 Feb 2020; 6th-anniversary page gives the date as 15 Jan). Its stated purposes were "continuing to understand the customer's mind" and "creating new value together with customers," because the ways companies and consumers connect were changing (2025 release).
  • The business logic is CRM for a low-involvement category. House's digital team says it plainly in a 2026 LINE case study. Food choices are made at the shelf, purchase data sits with retailers, and makers struggle with "invisible consumers." Its member site and fan community are the "visible" loyal layer, where House "can grasp attributes through purchase data and surveys and achieves deeper relationships and retention through continuous contact" (Kentaro Seido, team manager, Digital Creative Section, Corporate Communications) (LINE Yahoo for Business, 24 Feb 2026). Even House's e-commerce agency describes the site as something that "has lots of fans" and as a source of customer insight (Pencil client voice).
  • Who champions it. No named community lead appears in public sources. Campaigns are run by "Come on House Operations Office" (カモンハウス運営事務局), within corporate communications. Its research ties into House's in-house marketing research function, which has talked publicly about consumer understanding (Nikkei, Sep 2025). The Lab's slogan, "co-creation of new value" (共創による新価値創出), uses the same phrase as House's eighth medium-term plan, although the 2025 integrated report doesn't mention Come on House itself (Integrated Report 2025).
  • The 2025 pivot. On 17 November 2025 House opened the Co-creation Community Lab, with three parts: idea posting, a "co-creation room" (surveys, workshops, developer round-tables) and "New Value NEWS" (showing members what their input produced). The first project was a Tongari Corn fan club, named by members through a three-week naming contest with 50 gashapon prize sets (release; Lab).

Reading the numbers. Registrations grew steadily to about 327k, but active participation stayed flat at about 5,000–8,000 commenters a year. It dipped in 2023, when House ran only seven topics, and recovered once House posted more often and tied comments to prizes. Participation is spread thin: the top 1% of commenters (about 290 people) wrote 15% of comments, and only 103 people ever commented 50 or more times. The median comment is 32 Japanese characters. Of the 6,254 members who commented in 2020, 839 (13%) still commented in 2026. That is a small but real core who have stayed for seven years.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Lotteries, in steady supply. The Activity Park lists about 33–36 campaigns a year from 2022 to 2026 (Activity Park). Prizes include product bundles, CoCo Ichibanya vouchers, Tokyo DisneySea Fantasy Springs previews (2024), concert tickets for 360 people (2025) and online factory tours. A standing Winners' Voices thread (3,193 comments) acts as proof that people really win. This is the main retention engine, and members say so in their own words (see Member voice).
  2. Comments tied to entries. Many Talk Room prompts are entry mechanics: "comment and your odds double" for the seasonal new-product surveys (4,426, 4,450 and 4,754 comments, the three largest topics ever), and a "three-month series" that entered people who posted in all three months into a 500-winner draw (2,484 / 1,831 / 1,239 comments) (topic 103 body; capture). It works: prize topics averaged 1,294 comments against 700 for the rest. But comments get shorter and blander ("hope I win, like").
  3. Daily-play games around anniversaries. For the 3rd anniversary there was a sugoroku board game. The 5th (January 2025) had a "hit the piñata, collect points" game for 500 winners, made with the Japan Piñata Association (X; association). The 6th (January–February 2026) brought "Sugoroku Part 2": one dice roll a day, quiz squares, bonus dice on 6 and 16 February, 660 winners (page). A "scratch every day to collect points" campaign followed in July 2026 (scratch2607). These are House's streak mechanics, but they reset with each campaign. Nothing builds up over time.
  4. "Your vote ships." Votes and surveys visibly change products: Tongari Corn Butter Soy Sauce won 12,614 of 27,509 votes and came back in January 2024; second place, Rich Cheese, followed in April 2024 (Diamond Chain Store; thank-you campaign). The seven candidate flavors even became a Bandai gashapon line (Bandai, Sep 2023). This is the strongest non-prize hook, and it is what the Lab is built on.
  5. Belonging, newly added. Tongari-tai gives a named tribe, chosen by members ("we want it to be a team that livens things up together"), a digital membership card on My Page, and members-only events (club page; naming result). The 2026 events are small, held at the Tokyo head office, for adults only and under NDA, and put members in a room with the Tongari Corn developers. That is a strong sense of insider status, but each event reaches only 15 people.
  6. Contact with staff: rare. In 2020 the Stew Mix developer posted six times in their own thread, thanking members by nickname, and a Ukon no Chikara developer, "Curly," hosted a thread (205 comments). After that, staff voices almost disappear: 8 identifiable staff comments out of 94,800 (my count). Members have no host to grow attached to.

Switching costs: none apart from accumulated entries in a running campaign. Members have no profile, history, reputation or content worth keeping.

The content engine

  • from House: about 250 articles since 2020 (index). They range from developer stories (the "Smile Ball" tear-free onion team) and health columns from All About writers to event reports (the 2022 online factory tour, #90) and co-creation case stories.
  • Everyone's Open Data: 12 survey reports, each from 3,000–8,400 members, on curry habits, environmental attitudes, New Year food and more (index). House reuses them in its own PR: the 8,418-person environment survey fed a 2021 national news release for its microwave curry (House news). After August 2023 the series was replaced by lighter "which side are you on?" polls (どっち派) and Sustainable Table pages.
  • Member content turned into company content: House mines Talk Room comments for articles. Nearly 2,000 Fruiche hack posts became a 2023 milk-usage article (#97), and a healthy-longevity thread became a "ranking of ideas." This is House's only real loop for member-made content, and it is curated entirely by the editors.
  • Cadence: about 1–2 Talk Room prompts a month (7–21 a year), about 3 campaigns a month, plus a newsletter. It is steady across seven years, with a visible lull in 2023.
  • Other channels feed in: the X account @housefoods_now promotes Come on House campaigns, and adjacent experiments (the cluster metaverse island in 2024, the AI Roux Mix Maker in 2025, the LINE mini-app HOUSE QUEST WORLD in 2025–26) run beside it (AdverTimes; Roux Mix release).

People, programs and governance

  • Team: not disclosed. It is run by the "Operations Office" under corporate communications. Agencies are likely but unnamed; the forms run on the SPIRAL platform (house-form.jp), judging by the site's code.
  • No superuser or ambassador program. Heavy commenters (one has posted 185 times) get no recognition, title or role. Tongari-tai is the first tier, and anyone can join it.
  • Moderation: comments appear to be pre-moderated. I found almost no complaints in 94,800 comments ("hard to use" appears 0 times; one person mentions trouble logging in).
  • Feedback into product: done through structured votes and surveys rather than open discussion. The Lab's idea-submission terms are one-sided. House takes no confidentiality duty, pays nothing, has no duty to reply or evaluate, and gets perpetual, royalty-free rights to modify and use anything submitted (idea terms). The fan-club naming contest also warned that House might file the winning name as a trademark (release). These are standard legal protections for a food company, but they leave members with no share in what they help create.

Growth loops

  • Prize-driven sign-ups. Every campaign requires registration ("just a nickname and email!"), and campaigns are syndicated by sweepstakes sites and bloggers, which explains most of the 327k (Koubo; Ken-kaku).
  • Earned media from co-creation. "Fans voted, it's back" stories reached national trade and consumer outlets (Yorozoo News, Diamond Chain Store, Shokuhin Shimbun), and the November 2025 Lab release was picked up by Tokyo Shimbun's PR TIMES channel and food trade sites (Shokuhin; Food Weekly).
  • Brand-to-site funnel. House's corporate site lists Come on House under "Learn & Enjoy," and the X account links campaigns back to it (housefoods.jp/data).
  • No referral program, no member-to-member invitations, and no SEO from member content (Talk Room comments load by script).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence (outputs, not outcomes): at least three products shaped by members: Fruiche Sweets Caramel Banana (July 2023), Tongari Corn Butter Soy Sauce (January 2024) and Rich Cheese (April 2024). Member survey data has been used in national PR. 327k first-party contacts.
  • Missing: sales lift from the voted flavors, repeat-purchase or LTV figures, email performance, costs. The integrated report doesn't mention the site. Butter Soy Sauce had already been re-released six times before the vote, and its developer said the result "reaffirmed" its known popularity (Yorozoo). So the vote confirmed demand more than it discovered it.
  • Sister program for comparison: House's LINE mini-app HOUSE QUEST WORLD hit its registration target in 4 of 9 months, with heavy users staying 2.6× longer than in past campaigns and a revisit rate 1.3× target. This is the only quantified engagement result House has published from its CRM work, and it deliberately uses low incentives (LINE Yahoo).
  • Academic research: none found.

What they did right

  1. Built on an existing list instead of starting over. House moved House Club Wellness members across in 2020 and then put the co-creation layer inside the existing site in 2025 rather than launching a separate community. The Japanese case catalogue singles this out as the transferable move (Community Zukan F15).
  2. Closed the loop visibly. Vote results, dates, vote totals and the products on shelves are all published ("Butter Soy Sauce, January 2024; Rich Cheese, April 2024"), and House followed up with a "thank you for voting" campaign (tongari202405).
  3. Showed members their own data. Open Data shares survey results back with members, which makes the panel feel like a conversation and gives House PR material.
  4. Kept up a steady cadence for seven years. About 35 campaigns and 7–21 prompts a year with no gaps, plus anniversary events every January. The site grew every year and is still being invested in.
  5. Moved from mass to small, named groups. Tongari-tai combines a name chosen by members, a membership card, developer access and NDA sessions. These are the right ingredients for real belonging and high-quality product input.

What they did wrong

  1. A "community site" with no community features. Members can't reply to, like or follow each other. A member asked for likes and comments in April 2021; the operator answered that it would "refer to it in a future renewal" (topic 35). Five years later it still doesn't exist. Result: 29k people have commented, but almost nobody talks to anyone else.
  2. Prize dependence made engagement shallow. The biggest threads are entry mechanics. Median comment length is 32 characters, and some comments are just "hope I win." Engagement only recovered after 2023 once House posted more prize prompts.
  3. The panel shrank while the list grew. Survey turnout fell from 8,418 (2021) to 3,353 (2023) while membership roughly kept climbing, and House then ended the Open Data series. That suggests most members are dormant prize-seekers.
  4. Staff disappeared. After 2020's developer threads, staff wrote 8 of 94,800 comments. No host, no recognition of the people who post most.
  5. Co-creation that is small and one-sided. Three 15-seat events in 2026 for a 327k base. A June 2026 Tongari Corn heritage prompt drew only 134 comments. The idea terms give House perpetual, unpaid rights, and the fan-club name could be trademarked. As of November 2026 I found no public report of fan-meeting results, the goods project vote, or a product credited to Tongari-tai, and the "New Value NEWS" slot on the Lab's home page still features the 2023 Fruiche story (Lab). The Tongari Corn flavor House launched in October 2026 was a brand collaboration (Hayashi rice sauce), not a member co-creation (Nikkei).
  6. Some friction. A winner of a factory-tour spot lost it after missing the email reply deadline. Another member said they "couldn't log in, finally managed" (Member voice).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Let votes ship, and publish the receipts. Name the winner, the vote count and the launch date, then thank voters with a follow-up. It's the cheapest form of co-creation that customers can actually see.
  2. Show the panel its own data. Publishing survey results back to respondents turns a research panel into content and gives people a reason to take the next survey.
  3. Put the community where your list already is. Add opt-in, named sub-groups (one per product) inside the existing account system rather than launching a separate platform.
  4. Small, high-status, in-person sessions with the builders (prototype tastings, package reviews, NDA) give your best fans a reason to identify with the product. Then report back to the whole base what the 15 people changed.
  5. Keep a calendar. An anniversary event every year plus a fixed monthly rhythm of prompts kept the site alive for seven years with a modest team.

Avoid these

  1. Don't call a sweepstakes list a community. Without replies, profiles, recognition or staff voices, you get a large list of members who never talk to one another. Build peer-to-peer features before you scale sign-ups.
  2. Don't make prizes the reason to post. "Comment to double your odds" inflates counts and trains people to write throwaway comments. Track distinct, repeat and long-form contributors, not totals.
  3. Don't take all the rights and give nothing back. One-sided idea terms and trademarking members' names are legally safe but cold. Give credit (names in launch material, a first taste, a share of the goods) so contributors feel ownership.

Score notes. Retention mechanics 2/5: there are prize and daily-play hooks, but nothing builds up (no status or streaks that persist), and members have no peer ties. Content engine 3/5: steady, varied company content and survey reports, but member-made content is only mined by editors. Member ownership 2/5: votes decide some products, but members create nothing they own and the terms are one-sided. Staff investment 3/5: a constant campaign cadence and HQ events, but staff are almost absent from conversations. Business impact evidence 2/5: three credited products and PR reuse, with no sales or retention data. Durability 4/5: nearly seven years old, growing, re-invested in during 2025–26.
