TL;DR

  • What it is: Asahi Breweries' members-only "entertainment site for enjoying alcohol" (お酒を楽しむためのエンタメサイト), launched in March 2015 on the group-wide Asahi Web Service login. Members earn a points currency, うまい！GOLD ("Umai! GOLD", "G"), by reading columns, watching TV commercials, playing a daily slot machine and scratch card, taking quizzes and voting in polls. They spend it only on entries into Park-only prize draws. It had about 500,000 members by January 2016 and is still running in November 2026 (Nikkei Digital Marketing, 2016-01-19; Asahi on X, 2026-10-27).
  • Why people come back: a points bank that only grows, a 500G login bonus once a day, daily games, and draws where more points buy more entries. For Japan's large kenshō (sweepstakes-hobby) crowd, it becomes a five-minute daily chore: "I never win. Not even once. But collecting Umai! GOLD has become my daily routine" (member blog, 2021-03-25).
  • What Asahi feeds it: a steady stream of light content (new commercials at 100G each, snack recipes, trivia, product news, seasonal columns, a VR brewery tour in 2020) and one or more Park-only prize campaigns at almost all times. Every item is a short path to a product page.
  • Biggest mistake: it was called a "community site" but never gave members a way to talk to each other. Asahi never published a member count after 2016 and missed its stated goal of 1 million that year. It then moved its customer-ID strategy to LINE (20.95 million IDs by 2025), so the Park became a legacy points layer. Housekeeping also hurt loyal users: inactive accounts and their points were wiped in 2024, the app closed in 2021, and campaigns stopped after the 2025 ransomware attack.
  • The one lesson: points and daily games reliably produce a habit and measurable page views, and that habit can quietly shape what people buy. But they select for prize hunters, not fans. Without peer interaction or member-made content, you get a traffic machine with a low ceiling, and it can be replaced whenever a bigger ID platform shows up.

What the community is

Asahi Park (www.asahibeer.co.jp/park/) is an age-gated (20+) section of Asahi Breweries' corporate site. There is no separate sign-up: logging in with an Asahi Web Service account, the free ID shared across Asahi Breweries, Asahi Soft Drinks and Asahi Group Foods for campaigns and newsletters, makes you a Park member. Deleting that account is the only way to leave (Asahi customer FAQ: leaving Asahi Park; Asahi Group Foods privacy page). Use and entry are free (FAQ).

The site layout has barely changed in eleven years. Comparing Wayback captures from December 2015, January 2020 and January 2026 (2015, 2020, 2026; text captures in sources/asahi-park/):

G cannot be cashed out or swapped for goods or other points; its only use is entering Park draws (FAQ). There is no forum, comment thread, member profile, follower graph or member-to-member messaging. The only "social" inputs are poll votes, ratings in occasional snack popularity contests, and, in the 2017–2021 app, a step-count ranking. That matters for this catalogue: Nikkei's 2016 headline called it a "community site" (コミュニティサイト), but it is closer to Japan's 2000s-era points-and-owned-media portals (Coca-Cola Park was the model) than to a peer community.

Unofficial spaces: no dedicated fan group, subreddit or forum was found. Member talk happens on sweepstakes blogs (Ameba, goo, Hatena) and aggregators that list Park draws (Kenshō Seikatsu, CamNavi, Koubo, Yumecam). The Arctic Shift Reddit archive and Hacker News have nothing on it.

Origin and strategy

  • Why it was built: Asahi's marketing was organized by brand, each with strong TV campaigns, and nothing carried people between campaigns or across brands. Takashi Edagawa, deputy section chief in the Digital Marketing Department, described the Park as "a place that connects one campaign to the next" (Nikkei DM, 2016-01-19).
  • The content-first bet: Hiroshi Ozeki, deputy general manager of Digital Marketing, explained that food-maker sites only drew people who already knew the product, and campaign sign-ups didn't stick. So the Park led with entertainment laid out as a curation-style tile grid, and products appeared "naturally" beside the content. Recipes were reframed from homemaker cooking to sake no ate (snacks to go with drinks) so men would read them too (Nikkei MJ via Nikkei, 2016-10-26/29).
  • Business model link: the stated goal was LTV. Asahi was already working with outside firms to match member data with purchase data, and planned to use registration fields (gender, birth date, region) for e-commerce recommendations (Nikkei DM 2016; Nikkei 2016).
  • Predecessor: the Park replaced an older "MY PAGE" whose "Umai! points" were carried over into Umai! GOLD (Asahi Park About, Jan 2016 capture).
  • Industry context: in the same year, Japan Coca-Cola announced it would close Coca-Cola Park (2007–2016, 13 million members, flat since 2013) and move to the Coke ON vending-machine app. Nikkei cast this as a turning point for owned media (Nikkei, 2016; Nikkei xTrend, 2016-11-22). Asahi kept its Park anyway.
  • Strategy drift: Asahi opened its LINE official account in February 2017 and gradually moved campaign touchpoints into LINE. Nikkei xTrend reported that Asahi "had also developed its own ID tied to an app" but chose LINE IDs as the base of its customer data platform: 20.95 million IDs, with the preferred brand known for ~6 million people (Nikkei xTrend, 2025-02-10; LINE Yahoo case study). In April 2024 Asahi merged its advertising and digital-marketing departments into a single Communication Design Department. The stated reason was that CRM data was "not being used" by the advertising side (LINE Yahoo case study, 2025-01-16). Asahi Park is not mentioned in any of this recent strategy material.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A daily login bonus plus capped daily games. 500G for logging in once a day, one slot spin a day ("already played today"), quiz and content awards with caps, and a "click for 50G, up to 5 times a day" box on every page (2016 capture; slot page). Caps that reset daily build an appointment habit: a member reports harvesting ~2,000G in five minutes a day, every day (blog, 2021).
  2. A stored-value bank. G accumulates with no stated expiry while the account stays active. Members hoard (319,605G in one case) and then pour hundreds of entries into one prize ("this time I entered about 100 entries a day," blog, 2021-04-27). The balance shown in the header is a switching cost. Asahi made that cost painfully clear in 2024 by deleting the G of accounts left unused for a year.
  3. Variable rewards. Draws weighted by entries, plus instant-win scratch cards. Rare wins become stories that get blogged: "Asahi Park Scratch #146 ... first win in my 9th year ♡" (blog, 2024-05-03).
  4. Exclusivity. "Park-only" campaigns and "original goods you can only enter for at Asahi Park" (2016 About).
  5. Paid ad exposure that turns into purchase. Paying 100G per commercial view turns ads into "content." One member: "Watching it every day I got curious what it tasted like, tried one, loved it, and ended up buying it every week. Asahi Park totally got me" (blog, 2021).
  6. What's missing: identity, belonging, recognition, peer support, staff hosts. There are no member names, no featured members, no ranks or badges (the only leaderboard was G-WALK in the app, gone since 2021). Members gather in kenshō blogs, not in the Park.

The content engine

  • What Asahi produces: TV commercials for every brand (Super Dry, Clear Asahi, Marufu, Nikka Frontier, Asahi Gold in 2026), a running "easy home snacks" recipe series, cocktail guides, seasonal columns ("toast cultures of the world," hot-pot parties, infused-liquor how-tos), a monthly "Vineyard Newsletter" (ぶどう畑通信) for wine, product-launch notices, trivia quizzes, fortune-type quizzes, and occasional features such as the 2020 Super Dry VR brewery tour made while real tours were closed (Jan 2026 capture; AdverTimes, 2020-05-21).
  • Formats that earned press: the 2016 "best thing to stuff in a chikuwa" popularity vote (50 fillings rated by members) with an interview with comedian Chikuwaman (Nikkei, 2016).
  • Cadence: content tiles turn over weekly, the slot and quiz are daily, scratch cards run about monthly, and an email newsletter "mainly about Asahi Park campaigns" (Asahi mail news).
  • Partners: cross-company draws, such as "Asahi Park x SHARP" (summer 2023), where SHARP shares winners' data with Asahi (SHARP COCORO MEMBERS), and Asahi-group collaboration draws using 500G keyword entries (X, 2025).
  • Member-made content: essentially none. The 2015 page advertised "register/post recipes to earn G" (2016 About), but the recipe feature is a personal bookmark book of Asahi's own recipes (2017 recipe page). In 2016 Asahi said it would use needs "drawn from the community where members interact" in content planning (Nikkei, 2016). No evidence was found that such a space ever shipped.

People, programs and governance

  • Owner: Marketing HQ, Digital Marketing Department (2015–2024), then the Communication Design Department (from April 2024) (LINE Yahoo case). Named people: Takashi Edagawa (deputy section chief, launch spokesperson) and Hiroshi Ozeki (deputy GM). Team size: no evidence found.
  • Programs: no ambassador, superuser, moderator or champion program. Governance means terms of use, age verification and a support FAQ. The FAQ is dominated by "my G isn't adding up" and "my G went to zero" troubleshooting, with long browser-by-browser cookie instructions (private mode and opening content in a new tab break crediting) (FAQ 111; FAQ 112). That suggests the points ledger is the main thing members care about and the main support burden.
  • Feedback loop: polls and popularity votes give Asahi light preference data. No ideas portal and no product co-creation (unlike Sapporo's YEBISU BEER TOWN).

Growth loops

  • Paid retargeting: Asahi showed Park ads to anyone who had visited an Asahi site, on the view that past visitors are the best prospects, and credited this for the 500,000 sign-ups (Nikkei DM 2016). It also planned retargeting of lapsed members.
  • Sweepstakes aggregators (Kenshō Seikatsu lists ~70 Park draws to July 2026, plus CamNavi, Koubo, Yumecam) act as free referral channels into the Park (Kenshō Seikatsu). Winners' blog posts add social proof.
  • Shared group ID: every Asahi group campaign uses Asahi Web Service, so any campaign sign-up already has a Park account.
  • Owned social: Asahi's X account regularly promotes Park-only draws. In 2015 the Park paid 100 points for "liking" Asahi's Facebook page (2016 About).
  • No peer word-of-mouth or referral program was found.

Evidence of business impact

  • Company claims (2016): per-member page views ~5x non-members; group-site page views 2.5x pre-Park; members' purchasing of Asahi products up 10%+ in one year "by our survey"; 12th place in the Nikkei BP Web Brand Survey (Nikkei DM; Nikkei). These are self-reported, with no control group. The 5x page-view gap is mostly selection: people who sign up for a points site are the ones who click.
  • Anecdotal conversion: the member who started buying Asahi The Rich weekly after daily commercial views (blog, 2021).
  • Revealed preference: Asahi has kept funding Park prizes and content for 11+ years and restarted campaigns within about three months of a ransomware crisis. At the same time it rebuilt its customer-data strategy on LINE, where it reports ad effectiveness ~2.7x with CDP data (Nikkei xTrend 2025). The Park looks like a valued but secondary channel.
  • No retention, churn, LTV or revenue figures; no filings mention it; no academic study found.

What they did right

  1. Clear job for the site: "connect campaign to campaign" across brands. The site still does exactly that eleven years later (Nikkei DM 2016).
  2. Content before product, framed for the drinking occasion. Snack-pairing recipes and playful polls instead of brand brochures. Asahi credited this for keeping visitors from bouncing (Nikkei 2016).
  3. Cheap, reliable habit mechanics: a daily bonus, capped games and a growing bank produced daily logins at small cost (prizes are six-packs for 60–100 winners).
  4. Paying for ad views inside the Park. Commercials became earnable content, and members report it changing what they buy.
  5. Durability and continuity: points migrated from MY PAGE in 2015, the site survived the 2021 app shutdown, and it came back after the 2025 cyberattack. The same mechanics still work in 2026.
  6. Smart acquisition: retargeting past site visitors, and a single group-wide ID that removes sign-up friction.

What they did wrong

  1. Called it a community, built a lottery. No peer interaction, member identity or member-made content, so no belonging and no switching cost beyond the points balance. Superfans document their wins on outside blogs.
  2. Stopped reporting, and probably plateaued. The goal was 1M members in 2016; by October 2016 Nikkei still said "several hundred thousand," and Asahi has published nothing since. The pattern matches Coca-Cola Park, whose 13M base stalled for three years before it closed (Nikkei xTrend 2016).
  3. Attracts prize hunters. The core users are hobbyist sweepstakes players who rarely win ("I've entered many times and never won ... I don't need the glasses, maybe I'll sell them lol," blog, 2026-02-21). When points are easy to grind, heavy grinders swamp casual fans' odds.
  4. Taking away stored value. Deleting a year's inactive accounts with their G (Mar 2024) punished lapsed members. The app's G-WALK ranking stopped without migration (Jan 2021), leaving a user worried she would "forget to visit" the web version (blog, 2021-02-18). Removing Facebook login (2025) forced password resets.
  5. Fragile ledger UX. Years of FAQ pages on G not crediting because of cookies, private mode or opening a new tab (FAQ 111).
  6. Like-for-points. Paying 100 points for a Facebook like (2015) appears to conflict with Facebook's November 2014 ban on incentivizing likes (Loeb & Loeb, 2014).
  7. Overtaken internally. Asahi built its CDP on LINE IDs rather than its own Park/app ID (Nikkei xTrend 2025), leaving the Park without a growth role. The 2025 attack (up to 1.914M records possibly leaked, including 1.525M customer-inquiry records; no evidence Park points data was included) paused campaigns for about three months (ja.wikipedia).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give the space one job tied to your marketing calendar (Asahi's "bridge between campaigns") and measure it against that job.
  2. Reset small rewards daily (login bonus, one spin, capped tasks) to make a five-minute daily habit. It is cheap and it lasts.
  3. Make useful product exposure earnable: reward watching a demo or reading a how-to, the way Asahi pays G for commercials. Members say it changes what they buy.
  4. Frame content around the moment of use (snacks to go with drinks), not the product spec.
  5. Use one shared login across all your properties, and retarget people who already visited, so joining takes no effort.

Avoid these

  1. Don't mistake a points ledger for a community. Without member identity, peer conversation and member-made content, you get prize hunters and a plateau, and a bigger platform (here LINE) can replace you.
  2. Never wipe or strand stored value (inactive-account point deletion, app shutdowns that end features with no migration). It punishes the members most likely to come back.
  3. Don't let the reward economy be farmed. If grinding wins, casual fans lose and the audience narrows to sweepstakes hobbyists. Cap entries or weight rewards toward real product engagement.

Score justifications

  • Retention mechanics 3: strong daily-habit loop, but no social or status layer.
  • Content engine 3: steady, varied, low-cost content, but almost all of it is advertising.
  • Member ownership 1: no UGC, no peer space, no member roles.
  • Staff investment 2: content operations and prizes only; no visible hosts or community staff.
  • Business impact evidence 2: only self-reported 2016 figures.
  • Durability 4: 11+ years, survived the app shutdown and the 2025 cyberattack, but it is now a secondary channel.
