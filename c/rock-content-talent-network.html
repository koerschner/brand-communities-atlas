TL;DR

  • What it was. Rock Content, a content-marketing company in Belo Horizonte, built a community for the freelance writers, reviewers, content planners and designers who produced its clients' content. It started in January 2014 as the blog Escreva Para Web, added a closed Facebook group in 2016, was renamed Comunidade Rock Content in April 2017, and around 2020 moved into a gated forum, the Community Space. The freelancer base on the platform grew from about 3,500 (January 2016) to more than 60,000 (May 2020) (Drubscky, 2016; talent.rockcontent.com, 2020).
  • Why people came back. First, paid work: tasks appeared on the platform, and the community was where freelancers heard about them, learned the house style and got answers. Second, company in a lonely job: off-topic "botecos dos freelas" threads, staff who answered informally, monthly meetups at headquarters, and recognition through the monthly "Rocking Freela" award, member columns and badges.
  • What the company fed it. A content machine with daily blog posts, a free certification that was the entry gate, a monthly ebook, the Rocking News newsletter, Freela Talks webinars, Facebook lives, a podcast, a nationwide freelancer survey (9,500 respondents) and a magazine written by freelancers. In 2017–2018 a team of five to six people did this work.
  • Biggest mistake. The economics under the community never matched its rhetoric of belonging. Pay was a flat ~R$0.05 a word (about R$25 for a 500-word text) for beginners and veterans alike. Work volume was unpredictable, approval took weeks, and rejections were harsh. Only about 2,000 of the 80,000 "freelancers" were active writers. When the company cut staff in 2023, the community's content stopped, and nothing was left for members to carry on.
  • The one lesson. A community can make a marketplace's supply side loyal, skilled and cheap to recruit. It can't stand in for fair pay and steady demand, and it dies with the marketplace unless members own something portable.

What the community is

"Comunidade Rock Content" was the umbrella name for several spaces built for Rock Content's Rede de Talentos (Talent Network). The network was the pool of approved freelancers who took tasks on the company's production platform (first "RC1", then Rock Studio) (talent blog, 2019). The company's own 2021 explainer separated the two ideas. The Talent Network was "every freelancer approved at Rock Content". The Community was the closed, opt-in group of Brazilian freelancers who were active, meaning they delivered tasks every month. "From time to time, members who are no longer active with Rock are removed from the group" (Rede de talentos e comunidade, 2021 snapshot).

Spaces over time:

  • Blog: escrevaparaweb.com.br (from 1 January 2014), then comunidade.rockcontent.com (from 18 April 2017), then rockcontent.com/br/talent-blog (by 2021), then pingback.com/br/talent-blog (Aug–Sep 2025), and now 404 (1 ano de Comunidade; Wayback CDX).
  • Closed Facebook group "Comunidade de Freelancers Rock Content" (facebook.com/groups/freelasrockcontent), created in 2016 for active freelancers only (Drubscky, 2016).
  • Facebook page "Comunidade Rock Content" (16,832 likes; 98% recommend from 42 reviews; the last post visible in search is from Dec 2022) (FB).
  • Community Space (space.rockcontent.com), a gated, category-based forum with badges. Its exact launch date is not documented. It had a "life in quarantine" category created at the start of the pandemic, and Rock Content's community-manager post named it by early 2021, so it was probably launched in 2020 (2021 explainer; Gerente de Comunidade, 2021).
  • YouTube "Comunidade Rock Content", described as "the channel of Talent Network". YouTube now says "This channel does not exist" (checked 2026-11-07).
  • Talent hub talent.rockcontent.com in Portuguese, English and Spanish (2021 snapshot).

Unofficial spaces. No meaningful fan-run space was found. The public critical voice lives on Reclame Aqui, LinkedIn articles and Threads. Reddit has almost no Portuguese-language discussion of it. The Reddit threads about Rock Content come from WriterAccess's US writers (covered in the Rock Champions report).

Relation to Rock Champions. Rock Content ran separate communities for customers (Rock Champions, a points-for-shares portal), freelancers (this one) and employees (Gerente de Comunidade, 2021). This report covers only the freelancer side.

Origin and strategy

Rock Content was founded on 4 March 2013 by merging an SEO software startup, EverWrite (Diego Gomes and Edmar Ferreira), with TextCorner, Vitor Peçanha's freelance-writer marketplace (pt.wikipedia). So freelance supply was in the company's DNA. Rock sold managed content: an in-house analyst planned the piece, and freelancers wrote, reviewed and planned it, with every role split out. Clients paid Rock, and Rock paid writers per task (Amaral Nunes, c. 2019).

The community existed to solve supply problems:

  1. Acquisition. Escreva Para Web was "our portal for attracting and training freelancers". It was run as an inbound funnel with SEO, ebooks for lead capture, and email (Drubscky, 2016). By 2021 the team in charge was literally named "Community Marketing", "responsible for the inbound marketing funnel for freelancers and for developing the Rock Content community" (2021 explainer).
  2. Quality. In June 2016 the free Produção de Conteúdo para Web certification became a prerequisite for signing up, which "significantly raised the qualification" of new freelancers (Drubscky).
  3. Belonging and retention. In early 2016 Rock had about 3,500 registered freelancers "but no way to interact openly with them, let alone encourage communication between freelancers". The fix was the closed Facebook group (same).

Champions. Luiza Drubscky, community manager from January 2016 and at first a team of one, ran the strategy (same). Gustavo Grossi and Raíssa Galvão joined in June 2016. In 2017 Arthur Guedes and Thiago Murça handled freelancer relations and the Facebook group, Bruna Moreira ran email, and Dimitri Vieira was editor-in-chief of the blog from Jan 2018 to Feb 2020 (1 ano de Comunidade; Vieira). In 2017 the community team moved from Content (operations) into Marketing "to get closer to the company's other blogs and acquisition teams". That move shows the community was treated as a growth channel first.

Trajectory. Growth was explosive from 2016 to 2020, driven by SEO and the certification funnel. Then came a plateau, as the company shifted to US revenue (60% by Oct 2021, per NeoFeed) and to a self-service model. The community froze in 2023 and was gone in 2025. Caveat: the headline numbers count registrations. The gap between "80,000 freelancers" and "more than 2,000 writers" (both late 2019) suggests that only a few percent were active at any time (an estimate from press figures).

What keeps people coming back

  1. Income, delivered through the platform. This was the primary hook. Tasks appeared in client "teams", and freelancers had to click "pegar tarefa" before someone else did (Amaral Nunes). The community was where platform updates, demand tips and job ads ("Classificados") appeared. Membership required monthly delivery, which tied belonging directly to work.
  2. Peer support for a lonely, uncertain job. "Working only as a freelancer can be a bit lonely… having access to people in the same boat helps a lot" (Bernardo Lopes, 2018). The Space had categories for "Vida de Freelancer", "Random" (memes, series, jokes), "Sucesso Profissional" and "Dicas e dúvidas", the last with tutorials "from freelancer to freelancer".
  3. Rituals. These included monthly in-person meetups at the Belo Horizonte office from late 2016 (an earlier 2015 version had lapsed) (Drubscky), "botecos dos freelas" off-topic threads (the Toddy-vs-Nescau thread "almost caused a civil war"), Facebook lives with recurring hosts (a Carnival 2018 live, a Christmas live with 10 guests, breakfasts with the community), the "Escola dos Freelas" event at HQ (2018) (1 ano de Comunidade), and later virtual coffees and a "1st global meeting of Rock Content freelancers" (2021 explainer).
  4. Recognition and status. Rocking Freela was a monthly award for the top three freelancers on the platform ranking (one each for writing, review and planning). Winners got a published case study emailed to the whole base, plus a Kindle (2016) or a year of Rock University courses (2020) (Drubscky; Revisão para quê?). One writer still cited "in May 2017 I was chosen Rocking Freela, in 1st place!!" two years later (talent blog, 2019). In the product, every task got a 1–5 star grade that set a writer's running average, and chasing the "redação nota 5" was a whole genre of member posts (Lucas Amaral, 2017). In the Space, activity earned Rock Starter → Rock Prodigy → Rock Master badges.
  5. Parasocial ties to staff. Hosts such as Arthur Guedes and Thiago Murça became "not just known names but familiar faces". Members praised admins who "communicate in a relaxed way" (Lopes).
  6. Switching costs. These were weak. A freelancer's star average and approved categories lived on Rock's platform, but rival platforms (Contentools, Workana, 99Freelas) paid the same or more. Experienced writers moved to direct clients (Amaral Nunes).

The content engine

  • Blog cadence. In 2016 it went from irregular posting to 3 posts a week, then 5 a week plus 2 ebooks a month from July 2016. By 2018 it posted daily, often several times a day. Topics: Portuguese grammar, productivity, design, marketing, freelance finances, career changes (Drubscky; 1 ano). The blog positioned itself as "the largest blog about web content production in Brazil" (2017 snapshot), and its voice was rebuilt around a storytelling "brand persona" in 2019 (talent blog).
  • Education as the entry gate. Applicants took the free certification and then a paid-nothing test text. Rejected applicants got feedback and reading lists, with up to three attempts for reviewers (Revisão para quê?). Training thus doubled as screening and as acquisition for Rock University, which reported 160,000+ new students in 2020 alone (Vieira).
  • Other formats. The Rocking News newsletter (email traffic rose 2,609% in 2016), Freela Talks webinars (~40 minutes) (comunidade), the "Imersão Freelancer" free course, guest columnists, the Community Cast podcast, and the Mercado Freelancer survey (2017, with 99jobs and We Do Logos), which was covered by O Globo.
  • Member-made content. The Coluna Freela opened on 15 June 2018 as a weekly, free-topic column. The only prerequisite was an approved application (talent blog). It reached 60+ columns by 50+ authors by Dec 2020 (Vieira). A "Coluna dos Embaixadores" followed (snippet), and freelancers wrote the third Rock Content Magazine (2020), with 14 selected (comunidade). Member columns were also cheap SEO content and testimonials for recruiting.

People, programs and governance

  • Team. One person in early 2016, three by mid-2016, and five to six across Marketing and Content in 2017–2018 (Drubscky; 1 ano). By about 2020–21 the work sat in a Talent Network (also "Talent Management") department split into Talent Success, which handled application review, onboarding and technical development, and Community Marketing, made up of a blog editor-in-chief, a lead-gen analyst and a community analyst (2021 explainer). In-house "moderators" (production and planning analysts) graded and gave feedback on every task.
  • Ambassadors. Fifteen members were selected in July 2019 to "foster Rock's values" and help keep members engaged. The criteria were embodying the culture code, acting as a partner and caring about the community. The program was "being reformulated" in 2021 and still said so in 2025, so it was effectively never relaunched (an inference from the unchanged page, 2025 snapshot).
  • Rules. The community had rules and moderation, and members were told they were "owners of the space", responsible for welcoming others, organizing topics and helping each other. The motto was "Comunidades cuidam, redes conectam" ("communities care, networks connect"). Recurring member surveys measured belonging.
  • Feedback loop. A "Plataformas" category in the Space took feedback and bug reports on Rock's tools, and admins used the Facebook group to announce platform changes (Lopes). No evidence was found that members influenced pay rates or approval rules.

Growth loops

  1. SEO → lead magnet → certification → application. Search traffic to the blog (0 to 103 Google top-3 terms in 2016) fed ebooks and the free course, which were the mandatory entry to the platform. Registered freelancers rose from about 3,500 to more than 14,000 in 2016 (Drubscky). This was the main loop, and it is well documented.
  2. Member stories as recruiting. Coluna Freela posts and Rocking Freela cases ("how I visited 37 cities freelancing", "how I became a writer in 90 days") worked as testimonials.
  3. Partnerships and content swaps with niche blogs (for example a guest-post exchange with Revisão para quê?, 2020).
  4. Research PR. The 9,500-respondent freelancer survey won national press. The community brought in suppliers, not paying customers. No evidence was found that it drove client sales.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard-ish (company-reported). Blog organic traffic +603% (2016). Email traffic +2,609% (2016). Freelancer base ×4 in 2016 and about ×17 by 2020. Blog at 1M+ sessions a month by Dec 2020. Freelancer NPS of 67 (Apr 2021).
  • Missing. No cohort retention for freelancers, no data on active share or earnings, and no cost per acquired freelancer. No numbers link the community to client retention.
  • Context. Rock reported R$35M in revenue in 2017 (PEGN). The CEO said top talents could earn about US$100k a year (Bloomberg Línea, 2021), but the press snippet doesn't say whether that applied to Brazilian freelancers. Score: business_impact_evidence 2. Traffic and sign-up growth are documented; retention and economics are not.

What they did right

  1. Built belonging before scale. The closed group, offline meetups and off-topic threads came in the same year as the SEO push, and members noticed: "a Facebook group that's actually good for something" (Lopes, 2018). Score: staff_investment 4. A dedicated, named team with recurring live hosts.
  2. Made education the front door. The certification raised quality and created a shared vocabulary. Even critics credit the training: "What I learned from these analysts was worth many courses" (Yuri Kin, 2021). Score: content_engine 4. Big, consistent and multi-format, though its community-specific formats (the podcast, the ambassador column) faded fast.
  3. Gave members a byline. Coluna Freela (60+ columns, 50+ authors), the magazine and ambassador columns turned members into authors. The editor-in-chief described his job as helping freelancers "earn the success, visibility and authority they deserve" (Vieira).
  4. Kept the space for active members. Inviting only active freelancers and pruning lapsed ones kept conversation work-relevant and stopped the forum filling with applicants. Score: member_ownership 3. Members wrote, moderated (as ambassadors) and were told they "own the space". But Rock controlled entry and removal, and members owned nothing portable.
  5. Layered recognition. Monthly awards, star grades, badges and spotlights rewarded quality, not just posting volume. Score: retention_mechanics 3. The rituals and recognition were good, but retention depended mainly on how much paid work was available.

What they did wrong

  1. Belonging rhetoric on top of commodity pay. About R$0.05 a word and roughly R$25 a text, flat for beginners and veterans, with "no knowledge of any adjustment… since the beginning" (Amaral Nunes; Leandro Abreu, 2017 rate). A journalism-industry critic set this against the union's minimum of more than R$600 for comparable work (Radar do Futuro). Consequence: experienced writers left, as "the main reason seasoned professionals abandon [these platforms]", and the brand's reputation among writers is still "R$25 a text" years later (Threads, 2026-04-24).
  2. Unreliable demand and slow approvals. Tasks came and went ("ficar na seca" means no earnings that day). Approval could take more than a month across reviewer, analyst and client (who had a 14-day window) (Lopes). A minimum withdrawal of R$100, twice a month, was introduced on the new platform (self-service post, 2021).
  3. Gatekeeping that read as humiliation. Rejected applicants and removed writers repeatedly say they were treated "como um analfabeto" ("like an illiterate") (Reclame Aqui 2021; 2023). The company's reply was that feedback is "very complete, with reading suggestions". The welcome on the blog and the experience at the gate didn't match.
  4. Unpopular changes during the pandemic. The Talent Management year-end letter for 2020 admitted that "necessary adjustments aren't always the most popular, and we felt that firsthand with our talents" (2020 retrospective). The changes themselves aren't described publicly. Rock also required freelancers to have a company registration (CNPJ/MEI) from about 2019 (Amaral Nunes; CNPJ guide, 2022).
  5. Self-service cut out the human middle. From about 2021, self-service clients briefed and managed freelancers directly in place of Rock's analysts, and the CEO made self-service the focus after the Jan 2023 layoff (Startups). The analysts who had been the community's daily contact became optional, and the company's own blog warned "sometimes it ties knots".
  6. Programs launched and abandoned. The podcast had three episodes (Aug–Oct 2019). The ambassador program was "being reformulated" for four years. The blog went silent after 12 Apr 2023 but stayed online looking alive, and its archive was moved and then 404'd. The YouTube channel was deleted. No farewell to the community was found. Score: durability 1.
  7. Tied to a declining company. Rock Content shifted to the US (60% of revenue by 2021) and cut about 140 staff in Jan 2023, 52 in Jul 2023 and more in Jul 2024. It ended its Brazilian operation in 2025, with Peçanha citing "a culture clash" among the reasons (Peça-chave #79; pt.wikipedia). By 2023 the company said its own SEO team used only WriterAccess, with 15,000 freelancers (Pingback resources).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make onboarding education the gate and the first ritual. A free certification that's required to join raises quality, sets shared norms and generates SEO and leads at the same time.
  2. Keep a separate, gated space for active participants next to the public content layer, and prune it, so conversation stays useful to the people creating value.
  3. Give members bylines. Weekly member columns, magazine issues and award case studies spread recognition and double as testimonials and content.
  4. Staff it with recurring, informal faces (lives, breakfasts, meetups) and make off-topic play legitimate ("Random", botecos) so people stay for each other, not only for tasks.
  5. Reward quality, not activity. Monthly top-three awards based on work ratings beat points for posting.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let community warmth cover a weak core deal. If pay, demand or product value is poor, belonging content reads as spin, and the best members leave first.
  2. Don't launch rituals you won't sustain. A podcast with three episodes, an ambassador program "being reformulated" for years and a blog left silently frozen all tell members the company has stopped caring. Shut things down openly.
  3. Don't make the community depend on staff roles you plan to automate away. Moving to self-service removed the analysts who were members' daily human contact. Plan the community's role before cutting the people it relies on.
