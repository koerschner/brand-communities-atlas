How it pays off for the company

No ads and no direct revenue. TL;DR

  • What it is: a text-only link aggregator and comment forum that Paul Graham built in February 2007 as "Startup News". Y Combinator (YC) still owns and funds it. It has no ads, an almost unchanged 2007 design, about 5 million monthly readers, and only two public moderators (PG 2007; dang 2022; tomhow announcement 2025).
  • Why people come back: a daily front page of 30 links that tech people treat as their morning paper, plus comment threads where experts often weigh in. Status comes from karma and from being taken seriously. Recurring rituals keep pulling people in: the monthly "Who is hiring?" thread, Show HN feedback and Launch HN (New Yorker 2019).
  • What YC does to feed it: almost no original content. YC pays for full-time, highly visible human moderation (curation, title edits, a "second-chance pool" for overlooked stories, public explanations) and maintains software against flamewars, voting rings and spam. It does this inside a deliberate design philosophy: protect the culture and keep growth linear (YC blog 2015; dang 2021).
  • Biggest mistake: opaque moderation (hellbans, silent downranking, unnamed moderators before 2014). Combined with YC's ownership, it produces lasting distrust and accusations of bias. That distrust spawned Lobsters in 2012 and still drives critiques of political bias in 2026 (jcs 2012; Drew DeVault 2026).
  • The one lesson: HN treats behavior and culture as the product and features as the risk. A sponsor gets most of the value by funding excellent community stewardship and keeping its commercial asks small, explicit and capped (three listed perks), not by producing content or pushing growth.

What the community is

Hacker News (HN, news.ycombinator.com) is a social news site. Members submit links or text posts, upvote them, and discuss them in nested comment threads. The front page is ranked by points divided by a power of age, then adjusted by flags, anti-abuse software, a flamewar detector and moderator action (HN FAQ). It has a few sub-formats:

  • Ask HN: text questions.
  • Show HN: "something you've made that other people can play with", with its own etiquette rules (Show HN guidelines).
  • Launch HN: one front-page launch per YC startup.
  • Monthly threads: "Who is hiring?", "Who wants to be hired?" and freelancer threads.

Who runs it: Daniel Gackle (dang) has run the community since 2014. Scott Bell (sctb) co-moderated publicly from 2016 to 2019. Tom Howard (tomhow) went public as a second moderator in April 2025 after "doing HN moderation work for years" (YC blog 2014; Tell HN 2016; Tell HN 2025).

Official vs unofficial spaces:

  • Official: the site itself, the Firebase API (since October 2014) and Algolia search (since 2014; Algolia was a YC W14 company) (Vale.Rocks 2026).
  • Unofficial: hundreds of third-party clients, Kale Davis's weekly Hacker Newsletter (since 2010, 60,000+ subscribers per Muck Rack), and the rival community Lobsters. Lobsters launched on July 3, 2012, three weeks after its founder was hellbanned from HN (Lobsters about; jcs 2012).
  • Reddit: no meaningful official or fan subreddit was found. The Arctic Shift lookups for r/hackernews and r/ycombinator returned no data.

Status: active and growing, nearly 20 years in.

Origin and strategy

Graham's launch post (Feb 20, 2007) gave four reasons for building it (announcement):

  1. YC had "funded about a hundred people," and emailing links around no longer worked.
  2. Reddit, itself a YC company, had lost its startup focus as it grew.
  3. He wanted something to build with his new Lisp dialect, Arc.
  4. Most important, he wanted to know applicants before they applied. "In our new online application form, you literally apply through your Y Combinator account, so we'll recognize usernames that have been thoughtful contributors."

So from day one the business logic was acquisition and screening of founders (deal flow), not subscription retention or support deflection. The site was renamed Hacker News on August 14, 2007 and broadened to "anything that good hackers would find interesting" (Wikipedia; guidelines).

Who championed it: Graham personally. By 2013 he was spending "three to four hours per day simply moderating the site... 'It was becoming my life'" (TechCrunch 2013). When he stepped back in March 2014, he split the job among a designer (Kevin Hale), a YC voice (Kat Manalac, Garry Tan), an engineer (Nick Sivo) and a full-time community lead, Gackle, "who has already been doing most of the moderation for the last 18 months" (YC blog 2014).

Editorial independence: in September 2015 Sam Altman made HN "its own autonomous unit of YC," no longer part of the investment branch, with Gackle given the option to report to YC's Board of Overseers. Altman: "The HN community feels like it owns HN, and we like it that way... the best way for HN to benefit YC is simply for it to maximally benefit the community" (YC blog 2015).

What YC gets: the FAQ now lists exactly what HN gives YC: job ads and startup launches placed on the front page, and orange founder names shown to other alumni (FAQ). dang calls these "the three things that HN gives back to YC in exchange for funding it" (dang 2021).

Trajectory: fast growth from 2007 to 2013, then a deliberate, "unmistakeably linear" rise. dang: "Growth is important, but rapid growth would be unstable. This is a community not a startup!" (2021). Monthly readership has hovered around 5M since about 2018, while comments, votes and page views keep rising.

Two current shocks:

  • AI-generated "vibe-coded" Show HNs. Show HN's share of all stories reached ~15% by January 2026, with 37% of Show HNs stuck at 1 point (Cnops 2026).
  • A tech-hiring slump that has hollowed out "Who is hiring?".

Demographics: no published survey. dang's 2018 ranges: US 32–56%, Europe 28–35%, India 2–7% (via Vale.Rocks).

What keeps people coming back

  1. A daily, finite, high-signal front page. The ranking formula decays stories fast, so the page turns over through the day. The New Yorker: "For some, it's the first Web site they pull up in the morning... Nearly all the software engineers I know check it religiously" (2019). Graham called it "a kind of virtual town square" and admitted "how addictive Hacker News can be" (2009).
  2. The comments are the product. Long, substantive threads with practitioners, sometimes article authors, are what members value. The guidelines push hard here: "Comments should get more thoughtful and substantive, not less, as a topic gets more divisive" (guidelines).
  3. Karma as quiet status plus a ladder of privileges. Flagging and vouching unlock at 30 karma, downvoting comments above 500, a custom top-bar color above 250, polls above 500 (Vale.Rocks; YC 2015). There is a public leaderboard, led by tptacek (one of the site's first users, per TechCrunch 2013). There are deliberately no badges or streaks. Graham's 2009 test of orange names for top commenters "divided [the culture] into haves and have-nots" and was rolled back (PG 2009).
  4. Rituals and cadences:
    • "Who is hiring?" began in 2011 when a user volunteered to consolidate scattered posts. YC took over the account in 2015 and fixed it to the first weekday of each month at 11 AM ET (Tell HN 2015; 2015). dang explains the principle: unofficial recurring threads get flagged, so "the solution... was to make it official, give it basically a system account" (2024).
    • Smaller rituals: a black memorial bar when a notable person dies (since 2009), Christmas colors, and collective tributes. On the day after Steve Jobs died, every front-page story was about him, which Graham likened to birds "forming themselves as an S" (TC 2013).
  5. Show HN as a reason to show up as a maker. A place to get blunt feedback from expert peers. Show HN rules ask commenters to be respectful because "anyone sharing work is making a contribution" (Show HN). In 2015 YC partner Kevin Hale ran scheduled Friday feedback sessions on Show HNs (Tell HN 2015).
  6. Parasocial trust in visible moderators. dang's mod comments explain each action in public and link to precedent searches. Members thank the moderators by name: "Strict moderation is the reason HN is the only reasonable discussion forum remaining on the internet" (Mizza 2019).
  7. Identity without vanity. Persistent pseudonyms ("users should have an identity that others can relate to"), green names for accounts under two weeks old, an in-group vocabulary ("hug of death", "middlebrow dismissal", "the Dropbox comment"), and inside jokes (guidelines; Vale.Rocks).
  8. Anti-engagement features that build trust. "noprocrast" lets users lock themselves out (default: 20 minutes on, 3 hours off). There are no notifications, no feed personalization and no ads (FAQ). The lack of manipulation is part of the appeal.

Switching costs are mostly social: years of karma and reputation, and the fact that the audience (engineers, founders, investors) is concentrated here.

The content engine

YC produces almost none of the content. All links and discussion are member-made. Graham's early essays seeded the conversation, and the first users "commented on Graham's essays" (New Yorker). The company's "content work" is curation:

  • Title editing: reverting editorialized titles to the original. "On HN, titles are not the prerogative of the submitter" (dang 2026).
  • Second-chance pool: moderators and users rescue overlooked stories and place them briefly on the front page. Moderators also email users inviting them to repost (Vale.Rocks; dang 2026).
  • Dupe policy: reposts are allowed if a story "has not had significant attention in the last year," which gives good work several chances (FAQ).
  • Moderator "related" comments listing past threads, and new curated lists (e.g. /thelang, Jan 2026) (dang 2026).
  • Launch HN editing: dang personally edits YC founders' launch posts "not to sound like marketing, sales, or PR" and publishes the instructions (2021; yli.html).
  • Lightweight YC programming: AMAs (e.g. YC's immigration lawyer, 2015), "Hacker News Highlights" posts on the YC blog (2015–2018) (Tell HN; Vale.Rocks).

Supporting member-made tooling: in 2014 YC opened an official Firebase API, giving scrapers a three-week grace period to migrate, and endorsed Algolia search. That spawned an ecosystem of clients, digests and data projects. Earlier, an HNSearch API contest gave the winner a monitor (Vale.Rocks).

Score: content_engine 2. Curation is excellent, but the company makes almost nothing itself. This model fits link aggregation; it does not transfer to how-to content for a product.

People, programs and governance

Staff:

  • 1 moderator under Graham (2013); dang full-time from 2014; dang plus sctb (2016–19); dang plus tomhow (2025–).
  • Engineers: Nick Sivo earlier. In 2024 the site moved from Arc-on-Racket to SBCL through an Arc-in-Common-Lisp implementation, "Clarc", for performance (YC 2014; Lisp journey 2025).
  • dang in 2026: "there are only two of us supporting it" and "We don't even see 10% of what gets posted" (1, 2).

Three-pronged model: "software automation where possible, human intervention where necessary, and as much community moderation as users can provide" (YC 2015).

Tools:

  • For members: flags, vouches (added 2015 as "Modnesty", letting the community overturn kills), and showdead for transparency.
  • For staff: a flamewar detector, voting-ring detection, rate limits, a delayed reply link on deep threads, shadowbans, and topic or domain penalties (TC 2013; Shirriff 2013, which found ~20% of front-page stories penalized).

Rules: short guidelines focused on behavior ("Be kind. Don't be snarky", "no political or ideological battle"). In 2026 they added: "Don't post generated text or AI-edited text. HN is for conversation between humans."

No ambassador or MVP program, unless you count the karma ladder. Feedback flows through hn@ycombinator.com (meta posts are discouraged) and through dang's public replies.

Experiments, publicly framed as experiments: Political Detox Week (Dec 2016, ended after a few days), Apply HN (2016; the community picked YC Fellowship startups), clickable domains (2015, removed after dislike), Meet the Batch threads (2021), and /showlim limits on Show HNs from new accounts (March 2026) (Detox; Apply HN; domains; showlim).

Score: staff_investment 4. A tiny team, but full-time, senior, long-tenured and intensely hands-on. Score: member_ownership 5. Members produce all content, police it through flags and vouches, and even created the hiring ritual.

Growth loops

  • Traffic pull for submitters. A front-page slot sends a "hug of death". Bloggers and makers chase HN, and their links in turn bring readers. Measured effect: AI repos gained 121 GitHub stars in 24 hours and 289 in a week after HN exposure (arXiv 2025). A study of 1,814 repos found significant rises in stars, forks and contributors (arXiv 2025).
  • Launch venue. Show HN and Launch HN make HN the default launch spot for developer tools. Every Show HN starts on /shownew, so anyone gets a shot.
  • Job-market loop. Free monthly hiring threads bring both employers and job seekers back each month.
  • SEO and permanence. Threads stay public for good and rank in search. dang avoids deleting account histories "because that would gut the threads" (FAQ).
  • Third-party distribution. The open API powers apps and newsletters such as Hacker Newsletter.
  • YC funnel. Footer "Apply to YC" notices and occasional pitches from dang ("If you've ever thought about applying to YC... just do it") turn readers into applicants (dang 2024). Graham found and recruited Watsi, YC's first nonprofit, after seeing it on HN twice (TC 2013).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: thin. YC is private and publishes no numbers for applicants sourced from HN, launch conversions or hiring outcomes.
  • Company claims: HN was built to vet founders before they apply (2007). Founders' HN usernames serve as their YC identity, and the jobs page is "very useful for Y Combinator" (TC 2013). Graham admitted he "indirectly makes money" through job ads at portfolio companies (same source). TechCrunch called HN "a key part of the venture firm's success" (2013).
  • Indirect evidence: about 5M monthly readers who cannot be bought through ads; measurable launch effects on GitHub adoption (arXiv above); and YC capping its own perks at "three things" because "the good will of the community is worth more than any story" (FAQ).
  • Cost: no figures. Estimate: two to three full-time staff plus modest hosting. Graham noted in 2013 that the site ran on one core (TC 2013).

Score: business_impact_evidence 2. The strategic logic is clear and credible, but the numbers are anecdotes and executive claims.

What they did right

  1. Culture before growth. Graham "wouldn't want it to grow as large as Digg or Reddit," and found that "it's bad behavior you want to keep out more than bad people" (2009). Linear growth was the stated goal (dang 2021). Outcome: one of the very few 2007-era communities still thriving. Digg collapsed after its v4 redesign.
  2. Design that repels the wrong audience. "The graphic design is as plain as possible... the only thing to interest someone arriving at HN for the first time should be the ideas" (PG 2009). No images, no ads, no redesign in 19 years.
  3. Mechanics aimed at known failure modes. The "Fluff Principle" (easy-to-judge links take over) led to off-topic rules and title edits. Flamewar detection and delayed replies slow down kudzu-like bad threads. No downvotes on stories. Karma gates on punitive powers (PG 2009; TC 2013).
  4. A paid, visible, explaining moderator. Moving from an anonymous founder-moderator to a named full-time steward who explains decisions in public built legitimacy. The 2025 announcement drew 1,825 points of mostly thanks (Tell HN).
  5. Commercial asks that are explicit and capped. Three listed perks, a "moderate less, not more" policy when YC is the topic, formal autonomy in 2015, and Launch HNs edited so they don't read like PR (FAQ; YC 2015).
  6. Turning member hacks into official rituals. Who is hiring, Show HN and vouching all started as user behavior that was then formalized and protected.
  7. Transparency valves. showdead (so users can check what editors killed), vouch, and an open API. Graham: being able to see killed items "keeps editors honest" (2009).
  8. Willingness to retract experiments (orange names, clickable domains, Detox Week), framed as "when we say 'experiment', we really mean it" (YC 2015).

What they did wrong

  1. Secret moderation hurt trust. In 2012 a top-50 karma user (jcs) posted a 482-point complaint about title edits. It was deleted, and he was hellbanned and "slowbanned" with no notice. Moderators' identities were then secret. Consequence: he founded Lobsters three weeks later, a durable rival with public moderation logs (jcs 2012; Lobsters). Shadowbanning is still policy (Wikipedia).
  2. Ownership-driven suspicion of bias. Graham faced complaints of pro-YC bias early on, which he denied (TC 2013). Critics still describe HN as how YC "projects soft power, promotes startups it funds, and feeds people with ideas into its incubation program" (DeVault 2026).
  3. Politics and flagging. Political Detox Week (2016) drew "censorship" criticism and ended within days. dang conceded it "won't solve HN's civility problem" (TechCrunch 2016; Pincus via search snippet). User flagging is now accused of bias from both sides: "please fix the flag abuse problem" (a right-leaning user, 2025); progressives "abandoned the forum" (DeVault 2026).
  4. Culture of cynicism. "Middlebrow dismissal" and "the Dropbox comment" became shorthand for HN's reflexive negativity. The New Yorker described threads where "humane arguments are dismissed as emotional or irrational" (2019). Consequence: it puts off creators and underrepresented groups, and it prompted the "don't be curmudgeonly" guideline.
  5. Changing rules mid-game (Apply HN, 2016). YC changed the rules after Maciej Cegłowski won the community vote. dang later apologized publicly: "We changed the rules of a game you had won" (Tell HN). The format was not repeated.
  6. A bus factor of about two. dang personally edits every Launch HN ("I am still the bottleneck") (2021). In 2021 the site nearly went down because SSL renewal notices were going to a departed moderator's email; a user caught it (Tell HN). Under the 2026 AI flood, moderators see under 10% of posts.
  7. Weak newcomer on-ramp. "HN is rather cryptic at first" (dang, 2026). Rules are deliberately vague so they can't be gamed, and /showlim now blocks genuine first-time makers too: "I wish we had a better idea what to do for those cases!" (dang). dang has also floated "karma inflation" to help new users (2020).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Fund a steward, not a content studio. One or two senior, long-tenured, public moderators who explain every intervention did more for HN than any feature. Give them real independence from marketing (YC's 2015 autonomy move).
  2. Write behavior norms against your specific failure modes, then build software that adds friction there. Examples: delayed replies on heated threads, karma-gated punitive powers, reverting editorialized titles. Target behavior, not people.
  3. Turn organic member rituals into official, scheduled ones. Who is hiring became an institution once it got a fixed date and a system account. Find the recurring thread your users already start and make it the official one.
  4. Keep your commercial asks few, explicit and published. A capped list of sponsor perks, plus "moderate less, not more" when your own product is the topic, keeps trust intact. Make launches earn attention by not sounding like PR.
  5. Give work more than one shot and keep the floor visible. A second-chance pool, reposts allowed after a year, showdead and vouch let members check the editors, and keep good contributions from dying because of timing.

Avoid these

  1. Silent punishment of respected members. Hellbans and secret moderators create martyrs and rival communities (Lobsters). Tell people when and why they are restricted.
  2. Changing the rules after members have played. Apply HN's reversal cost YC an apology and $20k. Pre-commit to rules for any community-voted program.
  3. A bus factor of one or two plus a cryptic on-ramp. When volume spikes (AI-generated posts in 2026), a tiny team falls back on blunt gates that also block good newcomers. Invest in onboarding and in reviewer capacity before the flood.

Related communities worth adding

  • Lobsters — community-run (founded by joshua stein) — invite-only HN spin-off with public moderation logs, founded 2012 after an HN ban; durable niche success.
  • YC Bookface / YC Startup School forum — Y Combinator — the private founder network and the public founder-education community that sit downstream of HN.
  • Indie Hackers — Stripe (acquired 2017) — founder community bought by a company to feed its ecosystem.
  • Product Hunt — AngelList (acquired 2016) — launch community that competes with Show HN.
  • Slashdot — VA Software/BizX — karma and meta-moderation predecessor; long decline.
  • Digg — various owners — cautionary tale of a redesign (v4, 2010) that drove users to Reddit and HN.
  • Tildes — nonprofit — Reddit/HN-style community with invite-only growth.
