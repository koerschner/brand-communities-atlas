TL;DR

  • What it is. Notes.net was the website Iris Associates, the Lotus subsidiary that actually wrote Notes and Domino, opened in January 1997. It had discussion forums where customers could "collaborate directly with the core Domino and Notes developers", a monthly webzine (Iris Today), and a download vault for betas and fixes (Wayback, Dec 1997; IBM AD112 slides, 2010). It became the Lotus Developer Domain (LDD) in April 2002, moved under IBM developerWorks Lotus in December 2003, and closed to new posts in late November 2018, "after 20 years" (farewell thread, Nov 2018).
  • Why people came back. For daily answers, from engineers and from a few hundred high-volume experts. In mid-2003 a member found that four IBM/Lotus engineers had written 11,263 posts on forums "where they are not paid to provide support" (t/25560). Members also came for early betas and fixes, and because the forums were Notes databases they could replicate into their own client.
  • What the company fed it. The builders themselves (the Iris team, then product managers such as Ed Brill posting in threads). It also published a monthly webzine with developer interviews (Iris Today, then LDD Today), a code Sandbox, the fix-list database, Redbooks, wikis, "Open Mic" support webcasts, and Lotusphere, the annual conference that drew more than 10,000 people at its peak (Provoke).
  • The biggest mistake. Repeated corporate reorganizations of the knowledge base: the 2002 to 2003 rebrands broke every notes.net link (t/32861). The 2018 closure pointed members to IBM Developer Answers, which IBM itself withdrew in March 2020 (IBM). After the HCL sale, about 20 years of answers were offline for most of 2020 and came back with every member's name scrambled (t/161288; t/163707).
  • The lesson. Put the people who build the product in the forum, and let members see each other's contributions. Then treat the archive as a permanent asset: keep its URLs, its search ranking and its authors' names. The archive was the community's main source of value long after the posting slowed.

What the community is

A family of vendor-owned web forums for Lotus Notes and Domino administrators and developers, hosted on Domino itself and reachable from a browser or a Notes client:

Unofficial spaces grew up around it and often outlasted it. OpenNTF (2002), an open-source catalogue launched "by people from the Lotus Notes/Domino community" (OpenNTF, 2012). The blogger "Yellowverse" and the Planet Lotus aggregator (Planet Lotus). IdeaJam (Nov 2007), a member-built ideas site (Roling). CodeStore (CodeStore). The Engage and other user groups, and today about 9,000 Stack Overflow questions across the lotus-notes, lotus-domino, xpages and lotusscript tags (Stack Exchange API, counts 3,505 / 2,579 / 5,454 / 1,240, with some overlap).

Status today: the original forums are defunct. The archive sits read-only at ds-infolib.hcltechsw.com/ldd/, which returned HTTP 502/504 on every request I made on 2026-11-07. A copy from February 2003 onward is imported into HCL's Discourse Domino Forum, which still takes new questions at a trickle.

Origin and strategy

Iris wrote the strategy on the site itself. "We are the developers of Notes and Domino," the team (Barb, Murray, Barbara and Shawn) explained in January 1998, and the site existed to (1) give "early looks at the product through both trial downloads and demonstrations on the site itself (we are 100% pure Domino)", (2) "establish a community through the discussion forums where you can talk with each other as well as to us", and (3) "deliver compelling content through our monthly webzine… [and] meet the product developers in the Iris Interviews" (Notes.net About page, Jan 1998). The December 1997 home page invited visitors to "Collaborate directly with the core Domino and Notes developers. Jump in!!!" (Wayback).

Business logic, inferred from those goals and from later practice:

  • Acquisition and trials. Public betas went out on notes.net; R5's first public betas were posted there in September 1998 (Computerwoche). By 1999 "as usual for Lotus, all packages can be downloaded from Notes.Net", with 90-day trial licences (heise).
  • Dogfooding as marketing. The site was a live, high-traffic Domino deployment. By 2010 the Domino infrastructure behind developerWorks Lotus averaged 374,000 unique visitors and 3.1 million page views a month. It acted as a "first customer" running pre-release builds: "real-world use and testing at the same time" (AD112).
  • Support deflection and feedback. Engineers answered in public, and beta forums collected feedback. No source puts a number on the money saved (see Evidence).

The early champions were the Iris web team (Barbara Burch, Murray Hurvitz, Barb Mathers, Craig Lordan, Kevin Giles) and Iris engineers who posted in their own time (AD112; Iris Today article credits, 2002). After IBM folded Iris into Lotus in 2001 (heise), the strategy shifted to IBM's: "an overall IBM software group initiative to create a common feel across developer sites" (ZDNet, 2002).

Trajectory. Activity rose steeply from 1997 to 2002. It held a plateau of several hundred new questions a week through about 2009. It fell by roughly 80–90% between 2009–10 and 2012–14, which matches Notes losing ground to Exchange and IBM's pivot to "social business". After that it was a long tail of 15–35 topics a week until the 2018 closure. The 2019 zero is real: IBM had closed its forums and HCL's had not opened. Caveats: the Discourse import starts in February 2003, so the busiest Notes.net-era R4/5 forum isn't counted. The search tool also caps results at 500, and the sample covers one week a year.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Access to the people who wrote the code. This was the core hook. Engineers ranked among the top posters: Julie Kadashevich (Agent Manager) was 4th all-time and Matt Chant 5th by 2003. In the R6 era "two IBMers [were] in the top 10, and three in the top fifteen", with Andre Guirard second overall at 4,928 posts (t/25560; t/103906). Product managers answered criticism in public. When an admin of 13 years posted "Venting" in 2008, Ed Brill replied with data on ND8 adoption and pricing (t/1920).
  2. A high-volume expert core. Stan Rogers alone wrote 6,387 R6-forum posts in four years. The top 10 wrote about 10% of all posts among nearly 27,000 posters (t/103906). For a question asker, that meant a likely answer: 1.84 to 2.2 replies per question.
  3. Member-built status. The company never shipped a leaderboard. Members built one. Hynek Kobelka exported his replica and published posts-by-month graphs and top-100 rankings per year (2003, updated twice). Another member repeated the exercise in 2006 (t/28676; t/31557; t/103906). The reactions show how much status mattered: "Don't tell my wife… how much time I must have spent posting here", and "even though it makes me feel like a real slacker".
  4. Product-native access (replication). The forums were ordinary Notes databases, so members could open them in the Notes client and replicate them offline. That was a habit loop built from the product's own signature feature (Notes.net About, 1998; LDD native-access article, 2002). When IBM later moved the forums to web-only designs, members kept asking for the old access back. A 2019 idea called it "IBM's horrible decision to migrate the forums off the discussion NSF database" (t/159778; IDEA-I-32).
  5. Release-cadence rituals. Each major version got its own forum (R4/5, R6/7, 8, 8.5, 9). Public betas ("Beta 4 of Notes/Domino Rnext… Try it and let us know what you think") gave regulars a recurring reason to return (Wayback 2002).
  6. Career identity and tribe. Forum reputation turned into consulting work, books, Lotusphere talks and later IBM Champion status. Many of the 2006 top posters became well-known community figures. Members spoke of themselves as "Yellow" and "part of the family" (t/1920; Lotus Evangelist). Lotusphere's "Ask the Developers" session repeated the forum's promise in person once a year (Wikipedia).
  7. Switching costs. The archive itself held hundreds of thousands of solved problems, findable through Google, and it was what members feared losing in 2019 ("shame to loose so much knowledge and information", t/159778).

I found no evidence of official badges, points, streaks or in-product notifications in the forums. They had RSS from 2005 and a "resolved" flag in the 9.0 era (resolved feed).

The content engine

  • Iris Today (about 1997–2002), a monthly webzine of deep technical articles and "Iris Interviews" with the engineers. Papers on Domino architecture cite it (VLDB 2000; Mohan/Jim Gray archive). In 2002 it became LDD Today, described as "a community for developers of Lotus software… Publishes a monthly journal" (El.pub).
  • The download vault. Gold releases, QMR hotfixes and betas (Wayback, 1997).
  • The Sandbox. A library of shared code samples, by staff and members, recommended in Domino textbooks (Domino Designer 7 book; News4Notes, 2005).
  • The fix list database, Redbooks and toolkits (Wayback, 2002; panagenda).
  • Forum FAQs compiled by members. Members posted FAQs as topics and submitted them through a feedback form (nd6forum FAQs).
  • Product wikis (from about 2008) and "Open Mic" webcasts. Support engineers ran recorded calls that were announced in the forums; one 2010 invitation drew 76 replies (Strongback, 2008; IBM Open Mic, 2013; t/11262).
  • Lotusphere (1993 to 2012, then IBM Connect until IBM folded it into Think in 2018), with Iris developer presentations posted on Notes.net afterwards (Wayback, 2002; Wikipedia).

The company curated member content lightly at best (the Sandbox, FAQs). The richest member output (OpenNTF, CodeStore, the blogs, IdeaJam) grew outside the official site. Many people "found their way over following a trip to the Notes.net Café forum" (CodeStore, 2001).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. A small web team in Iris's, later Lotus's, information-development group ran the site (AD112). Engineers posted voluntarily. By 2018 the forum managers were anonymous IBM staff, one of whom signed off: "I've seldom seen a user/developer community as passionate as this one" (t/154865).
  • Rules. A developer focus was enforced by peers. In one thread a regular told a user: "This is the Lotus Developer Domain, not the Notes End User Help Domain" (search snippet of nd6forum thread). In later years each post got a "Feedback number WEBB…" ID, a sign the forum was wired into IBM's feedback intake (nd85 example).
  • Recognition programs arrived late and sat outside the forum. IBM Champions for Collaboration Solutions started in 2011 (155 of IBM's 650 Champions in 2018), and they moved to HCL Masters, later HCL Ambassadors, after the sale (see the ibm-champions report; HCL Ambassadors).
  • Feedback channels grew more formal over time: beta forums (1998–2002), the member-built IdeaJam (2007), which IBM used for "LotusKnows" campaigns (portal2portal, 2009), IBM's "#domino2025" jams in 2017–18 (IBM, Dec 2017), and HCL's Aha! "#dominoforever" ideas portal (2018/19) (HCL Ideas).

Growth loops

  • Downloads and betas sent evaluators to the site, which put them next to the forums (heise, 1999).
  • Search. Forum threads ranked for error messages. A member recalled in 2020 that "in the past it was possible to just Google (an error message…) and get pages from all over, including from these old Notes forums". Each broken migration cut this loop (t/163707).
  • Spillover. Forum regulars started blogs, books, OpenNTF and IdeaJam, which in turn linked back to the forums (CodeStore; Heidloff, 2009).
  • No member-get-member loop. Buying decisions were made by IT departments, not by the users who hated the client (Guardian, 2006).

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence: none on revenue, retention or support savings. IBM never published forum impact figures that I could find. What exists:

  • Traffic: 374,000 UV and 3.1M page views a month in 2010 for the Domino-hosted dW Lotus site (AD112).
  • Volume of free expert labour: 11,263 posts from four IBM engineers by 2003, and about 282,000 messages from 26,960 people in four years of the R6 forum (t/25560; t/103906). This is an estimate of deflected support, not a measured saving.
  • IBM used forum volume as an adoption signal: "There are 20,000+ postings in the Notes/Domino 8 forum, and based on survey and call data we know that 50% of Notes customers have begun ND8 rollout" (Ed Brill, Dec 2008, t/1920).
  • Context: the product went from IBM's $3.5B reason to buy Lotus in 1995 to a $1.8B divestiture (bundled with other products) in December 2018 (TechCrunch). The community did not stop that decline. It may have slowed it by keeping a loyal admin and developer base, but that is unproven.

What they did right

  1. Put the builders in the room. "Talk with each other as well as to us" was the founding promise, and engineers kept it for years, ranking among the top posters (Notes.net About; t/25560).
  2. Bundled the community with things people needed anyway: downloads, betas, fixes and the fix list, so every upgrade brought admins back to the site (Wayback 2002).
  3. Ran the community on the product. The site was "100% pure Domino", reachable from the Notes client, and served as a pre-release test bed. The community demonstrated the product as well as discussing it (AD112).
  4. Paired editorial content with Q&A. Iris Today interviews put names and faces on the engineers, which made them easier to approach in the forums (search: Iris Today interviews).
  5. Made forum data open enough for members to build on. Because members could replicate the databases, they produced their own statistics, rankings and offline archives. In 2019 that turned out to be the only surviving copy for some (t/159778).
  6. Engaged critics in public. IBM PMs answered rants such as "Venting" and "User Interface Disasters in Lotus Notes" in the threads themselves (t/1920; t/68413).

What they did wrong

  1. Rebrand churn broke the knowledge graph. The site went from Notes.net to LDD to developerWorks Lotus within 20 months (2002–03). Members complained: "we've gone from a nice clean layout to a clunky corporate one", and "All links that use www.notes.net are now broken… you cannot search the internet using Google… the Notes Sandbox is a mess" (t/28708; t/32861).
  2. Took away the product-native access. At some point IBM moved the forums off replicable NSF designs (an XPages redesign was planned by 2010, AD112; the exact cut-over date is unverified). That removed a hook that members still wanted back years later (IDEA-I-32).
  3. Closed the forum and pointed members to a doomed platform. In November 2018 IBM sent members to IBM Developer Answers, which it withdrew 16 months later (t/154865; IBM). In 2019 there was a gap of months with no official forum (zero new topics in my March 2019 sample).
  4. A messy handover to HCL. In August 2019 members were told to use links that "doesn't work at all… Frustrated !!", and suspected that the HCL moderator was "a bot" (t/159778). The archive then disappeared. "Working hard??? Its been 6 months now. At this rate when the notes forums are available again, there won't be any notes users left!" (t/161288, Jul 2020).
  5. Scrambled authorship. The September 2020 restoration replaced every name for data-privacy reasons. One veteran found himself listed as "Umberto Nongeroson" in posts and "Yentl Quetkrotexnivu" in views. HCL added a banner and sitemaps only after members asked (t/163707). The reputation that had kept the experts posting was erased from the record.
  6. Never measured or rewarded contributors officially. Leaderboards were member-made. Formal recognition (Champions, 2011) arrived only as activity was already falling steeply.
  7. Late, tone-deaf corporate sites. By 2003 users were already blaming IBM's "improvements" for lost features and poor search (t/28708).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Staff the forum with the engineers who build the product, and make it visible. Named builders answering in public was Notes.net's main draw. Iris Today interviews made those engineers familiar names.
  2. Attach the community to unavoidable workflows (betas, downloads, release notes, the fix list), so every release brings users back.
  3. Build the community with your own product where you can (Notes.net ran on Domino and was replicable into the client). It shows the product working and ties community habits to product habits.
  4. Expose contribution data and recognize the top 1%. Members built their own leaderboards because the platform lacked one. Ship it, and add a formal program before activity peaks, not after.
  5. Hold an annual in-person ritual that mirrors the forum (Lotusphere's "Ask the Developers").

Avoid these

  1. Don't let rebrands and replatforms break URLs, search ranking and authorship. Ten years of answers are the asset. Keep redirects and names, and publish a read-only export before you sunset anything.
  2. Never close a community without a stable successor. IBM sent members to a platform it withdrew 16 months later, and the HCL handover left a gap of months.
  3. Don't strip out the product-native features (offline replication, client access) that made the community feel like part of the product.

Member ownership 4: most answers and all the leaderboards, plus the major spin-offs (OpenNTF, IdeaJam, Planet Lotus), came from members. Staff investment 3: an exceptional engineer presence early on that faded into corporate site management and closure. Business impact evidence 1: traffic and post counts only, no retention or savings data. Durability 2: a 21-year run, but closed in 2018 with the archive broken twice since.
