TL;DR

  • What it was: Kirin's members-only "co-creation community" (共創コミュニティ), opened on 8 Aug 2013 on Tribal Media House's white-label cocosquare platform, with Facebook login. It started as a project to brew a beer with 20–34-year-olds from Kanagawa, which became "YOKOHAMA ～港の風薫る生～" (March 2014). It then grew into Kirin's standing panel for sampling, market research (MROC), co-creation and "ambassador" activities. It won an Ambassador Program Award in 2016. It closed after "4 years and 6 months": posting stopped on 31 Jan 2018 and the site was gone by 1 Mar 2018 [1][7][19][29].
  • Why people came back: weekly "topics" (お題) about drinking life and new Kirin products; "コラボ" contribution points that unlocked product-logo stamps; a quarterly points ranking; frequent monitor draws (often two cases of beer); and a small core of regulars who bonded over nightly "今夜の晩酌" photos. The bond between members was the real glue. When Kirin pulled the plug, about 40 of them rebuilt the community on a free BBS within days and were still swapping regional gift boxes in 2025 [5][23][29][35].
  • What Kirin fed it: product-feedback prompts tied to every launch, monitor and prize campaigns, co-creation projects ("はまっ子ビール", 本搾りファンクラブ, こだわりの飲み方発見), offline Thanksgiving parties (感謝祭 2015, 2016), factory tours, a "一番搾り検定" quiz, a recipe collaboration, and members-only Grand Kirin craft-beer seminars ("びあのわ") [7][11][12][17][24].
  • Biggest mistake: it was run as an outsourced research panel, not as a community. By 2017, 37,500 sign-ups produced 6–55 participants per discussion. Kirin missed its own target of 100,000 members. Prizes were cut in its last year, and it closed with about one month's notice, no explanation and no archive. That alienated the most loyal Kirin drinkers it had, and several said their "Kirin share" of drinking fell afterwards [5][23][29][32].
  • The one lesson: if your community's real value is the members' bonds with each other, closing the venue doesn't end those bonds. It ends your place in them. Plan the exit (an archive, an alumni space, a successor) as carefully as the launch.

What the community is

Kanpai Kaigi ("Cheers Meeting") was a closed web community operated by Kirin Company's CSV division, digital-marketing team (social media team). It ran on cocosquare, a "community-panel" co-creation and market-research SaaS that Tribal Media House launched the same day. Tribal Media House is a social-media agency in the Netyear group. Pricing was reported from ¥1.2M setup plus ¥250k a month [3][4][7]. Members signed up with a Facebook account (later also email). The 2013 terms of service name Tribal Media House as the system provider [41]. From November 2014 non-members could browse, but only members could post, like or comment [14].

The site had tabs for ディスカッション (threaded discussions on company-set topics), アンケート (surveys), 参加者募集 (monitor and event recruitment), ブログ (operator posts), and 運営者のつぶやき (operator notes) [22]. Sub-communities lived inside it: the 本搾りファンクラブ (Honshibori chu-hi fan club), the こだわりの飲み方発見プロジェクト by GRAND KIRIN, and from March 2016 "びあのわ" (Bianowa), Grand Kirin's "beer love" community and members-only event series [14][17][24].

Unofficial space: "カンパーイ会議" (note the extra ー), a free mottoki.com BBS. A member known as きや created it on 25 Jan 2018, after the shutdown notice. It has 80+ threads (晩酌 photos, 限定ビール, 工場見学, 物資交換 gift swaps, baseball) and was still active in October 2025 [28][30].

Status today: defunct. The beach.jp "一番搾りコミュニティ" that Kirin later ran with QON and the Sankei newspaper (from January 2021) now says the circle "has already been dissolved" (このサークルはすでに解散されています) [36][37].

Origin and strategy

  • Business problem. Japan's "young people are leaving beer" (若者のビール離れ) worry. Tribal Media's case study lists three goals: get young adults drinking beer, raise awareness that Kirin Beer was born in Yokohama, and make better use of a Facebook fan base that had grown to "several hundred thousand" [9]. Kirin opened official Facebook and Twitter accounts in spring 2012 and built Kanpai Kaigi a year later [7].
  • Launch design (Aug 2013). Members had to be 20–34 and live in or come from Kanagawa. The schedule ran from online discussion (Aug–Sep), to a tasting (Sep), a brewing class (Oct), more online discussion (Oct–Nov), a final tasting (Dec), brewing, and sales at the Yokohama factory's Spring Valley restaurant in March 2014 [1][2].
  • Strategic framing. Champion 小川直樹 (Naoki Ogawa) called it "not a place to make today's or tomorrow's sales, but seed-sowing for the next generation" (今日、明日の売り上げをつくる場ではなく、次世代の種まき). He described a four-tier fan pyramid: loose participants, then Kirin fans, then loyal customers, then evangelists. Facebook, Twitter and email handle the bottom two tiers; Kanpai Kaigi was for the top two, which were meant to "evolve" into evangelists who pull lower tiers up [7].
  • Four jobs [7]: (1) product sampling and monitor reviews, usable in promotional material; (2) an MROC with "several topics a week"; (3) co-created content and products; (4) "ambassador" activities such as the 一番搾り検定 and factory tours.
  • Deliberately small and closed. Ogawa contrasted roughly 5 million loose SNS connections with a members-only core "to communicate deeply with each person". He admitted that colleagues asked how much a small core of fans could really contribute to company sales and brand value [6].
  • Ambition vs reality. In December 2013 the site had about 1,000 members, and Kirin set a target of 100,000 within two years [5].

Trajectory: sign-ups grew steadily, by about 7,000 between May 2016 and Sep 2017, because prize campaigns used registration as their gate. Participation stayed tiny. Between May 2016 and Sep 2017 the counter grew about 22%, while each discussion still drew a few dozen voices. The refuge BBS data shows that the core that mattered numbered about 40 people (39 posting handles), and six of them wrote more than 300 posts each [44]. There is no evidence of a subreddit or any other large unofficial space. This was a Japanese web-forum community.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A steady drumbeat of company prompts. "Tell us what you want to drink at a Christmas party", "your favourite Kirin campaign", "what to do before summer ends", and a "tell us what you think" thread for every launch (Ichiban Shibori renewal, Aki Aji, Tanrei Green Label, Hyoketsu flavours). The cadence was "several topics a week" [5][7][23].
  2. Points, badges and a league table. Every action (posting, commenting, receiving likes) earned コラボ points. Thresholds unlocked stamps with product logos such as "Lager" [1][5]. Each discussion paid 10 points and each survey 5 [22][23]. A quarterly "コラボ数ランキング" blog post published the leaders; at least 9 such posts appeared from 2015 to July 2017 [24]. Members remembered it: one regular joked that her ranking days were "over two years ago" [30], and another complained that the closure didn't even wait for the next quarterly 敢闘賞 (fighting-spirit award) [29].
  3. Material rewards. Monitor slots often meant two cases of beer delivered, then a private one-month research room with 50–80 winners [29]. There were open giveaways too: the 2016 "love letter to Ichiban Shibori" (100 winners × 2 cases) and the 47-prefecture Ichiban Shibori draw (1,500 six-packs) [13][16]. The refuge BBS later ran three "懸賞当選報告" (prize-win report) threads, which shows how many core members were also prize hunters [28].
  4. Insider access and status. Co-creating a beer that was then sold at the factory restaurant [1][7]; tasting before release; factory tours; craft seminars with master brewer Tomohiro Tayama, where photography was banned, which made them feel exclusive [26].
  5. Offline rituals. The カンパイ会議感謝祭 (Thanksgiving festival): the first in December 2015 offered free beer tasting and an all-you-can-eat menu of recipes developed from members' requests. The second was held in Yokohama on 24 December 2016 with a group kanpai and games [11][12][24].
  6. Peer belonging (the real glue). Nightly "今日の晩酌の写真" threads (up to "Ver3" by 2017), with regulars commenting on each other's snacks late at night [22]. Kirin didn't design this, but it was what members mourned: "I felt like I'd lost my reason for living" (生き甲斐をなくした感) [29].

The content engine

  • Company-made: discussion prompts tied to launches; an operator blog with categories for monitor recruitment (19 posts), event reports (18), ranking announcements (9), discussion summaries (6), "about Kanpai Kaigi" (6) and project logs [24]; how-to-enjoy posts (the most-read included "why old-style bottled beer gets a small glass" and "10 hours in the fridge to level up home beer") [14]; a collaboration with Kirin Recipe Note, where members requested "winter hosting dishes that go with beer" and a professional cook turned them into recipes served at the Thanksgiving festival [12]; and the Grand Kirin "びあのわ" events: a mug-making workshop, a Belgian-saison bus tour, a rakugo-and-beer night, and recurring "CRAFT BEER ROOM" tastings in Tokyo, Nagoya and Kyoto [17][18][25]. Bianowa seminars kept running on Kirin's DRINX e-commerce site into 2018 [27].
  • Member-made: product reviews and photos, names and logos voted on (the "YOKOHAMA ～港の風薫る生～" name came from a member naming vote) [9], senryu and love-letter entries, and "地元の誇り" (local pride) essays [15][16]. Kirin re-published the best as blog content ("the fans' love letters are too hot") [15].
  • Content as PR: Kirin turned the co-creation process itself into a story ("a beer young Yokohama people made themselves") and pitched it to TV, newspapers and the web. Ogawa used that media coverage to justify the community internally [6][9].

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: a small social-media team inside Kirin's CSV division, with Ogawa as public face from 2013 to 2016 [5][6][7][20]. Day-to-day community management, research design, fieldwork, analysis and tasting events were supported by Tribal Media House [9]. Members suspected it was "outsourced wholesale" (丸投げ): fans who mentioned it at Kirin breweries were told staff had never heard of it. Others disagreed, saying the Yokohama factory and Kirin City staff did know it [29]. Treat this as member perception, not fact.
  • Programs: the "ambassador" layer was activities (quizzes, tours, monitors), not a credentialed ambassador tier with criteria and perks. One blog category was a single "キリン一番搾りアンバサダー" post [24]. The 2016 Ambassador Program Award (alongside Kao, Calbee, Nestlé's Nescafé Ambassador and others) recognized Kanpai Kaigi as a program [19][20][21].
  • Moderation and tone: members valued that Kirin published harsh reviews of its own products: "even evaluations harsh enough to seem cutting were posted openly" [29]. Candour continued on the refuge BBS ("Kirin, have you lost your mind?" about Kirin The Strong Hard Cola) [33].
  • Feedback loop: formal, through research. Monitor results were summarized in blog posts (e.g. "一番搾り 小麦のうまみ 感想まとめ"); profile questions were expanded at members' request in July 2015 [14]. No evidence was found of a public "you said, we did" log beyond the 2013–14 Yokohama beer.

Growth loops

  • Prize gating. National campaigns required Kanpai Kaigi registration (the 47-prefecture Ichiban Shibori draw in 2016, the love-letter campaign) [13][16]. This grew the counter but brought in sweepstakes entrants, not participants.
  • Social funnel. Kirin recruited from its Facebook and Twitter fans, and from 2016 added share buttons to blog posts [9][14]. Bianowa seminars were advertised on Twitter; one attendee "had no idea Kirin did this" until an ad appeared [25].
  • Earned media. The co-created beer and the 2016 award gave Kirin trade-press coverage (Nikkei MJ, Nikkei xTrend, AdverTimes, DHBR, ITmedia) [5][6][7][8][20].
  • No referral program or SEO effect was found. The site was login-walled for posting.

Evidence of business impact

  • Vendor claim (soft): Tribal Media reports that after the はまっ子 project, over 50% of participants drank Kirin products more than before and over 70% told family, friends or colleagues about it [9]. That's a sample of about 200–300 people, self-reported, from the agency that ran it.
  • Product output (small): the co-created beer sold only at the Yokohama factory restaurant, from late March to just after Golden Week 2014. Nikkei xTrend noted the scale felt small "for all the effort of co-creating with young Kirin fans" [7]. Waseda's Tomoko Kawakami cites it, alongside Sapporo's 百人のキセキ, as typical of co-creation communities producing niche or improved products rather than breakthroughs [8].
  • Negative signal after closure: in a February 2018 thread titled "Has your Kirin share changed?", the refuge's founder wrote that his Kirin share "has definitely fallen" since the closure, "Suntory especially is surging". Several others agreed, and one said she'd thrown away her campaign seals [32]. That's anecdotal, but it is the only post-closure loyalty evidence found.
  • Platform economics: a Reuters/Fisco research memo (Dec 2015) said cocosquare had only "about 10-plus" client companies and growth was sluggish [10] (from the search snippet; the full page was blocked). That's context for a vendor-dependent community.
  • No sales, retention, NPS or LTV figures from Kirin were found. Kirin Holdings filings were not searched (it isn't SEC-registered). Business-impact evidence score 2/5: one vendor claim and PR anecdotes.

What they did right

  1. A clear, bounded first project. A beer for and by young Yokohama locals, with a dated schedule, mixed online and offline steps, and a real product at the end gave Kanpai Kaigi a story and a reason to join [1][9].
  2. Online discussion paired with offline tasting. Tribal Media noted that concept and naming can be done online but taste needs real tastings, so the top 30 met in person [5][9]. Offline moments (factory tours, the 2015 and 2016 Thanksgiving festivals, Bianowa seminars) created the friendships that outlived the site [11][24][31].
  3. Honest about its purpose. Ogawa never claimed it would move volume. He framed it as research plus seed-sowing and used the PR to defend it internally [6][7].
  4. Tolerated criticism. Publishing harsh reviews of Kirin's own products earned trust that members said rival brands' fan sites lacked [29].
  5. Gamification that members actually noticed. The quarterly コラボ ranking and logo stamps stuck in members' memories years later [29][30]. Retention-mechanics score 3/5: a real cadence and progress loop, but weak social features and a reward system that drew prize hunters.

What they did wrong

  1. A panel mistaken for a community. Joining was cheap (prize gates), participating was optional, and the site counted sign-ups. Result: 37,592 "members" but 6–55 voices per discussion in 2017 [22][23], and the 100,000 target missed by more than 60% [5]. Member-ownership score 2/5: members could post and vote but owned no spaces, roles or programs; the only member-owned space was the one they built after being shut out.
  2. Outsourced and under-championed. A white-label vendor platform and agency operations (per Tribal Media's own case study) left the community with little visibility inside Kirin. Members said brewery staff didn't know it existed [9][29]. Staff-investment score 2/5.
  3. Rewards became the relationship, and then disappeared. A member noted that "for over a year now the presents stopped" [29]. Another regular argued the program ended partly because many monitor winners took the two cases and never fulfilled their reviewer role [29]. Once the incentives that drove most sign-ups went away, the engagement went with them.
  4. An abrupt, unexplained shutdown with no archive. Members were thanked for "4 years 6 months" with about a month's notice. Posting stopped on 31 January even though the stated end date was 28 February. Discussion history was hidden, then deleted. "There should have been a more graceful ending" (もう少し粋な終わり方があったのでは) [29]. No public Kirin announcement was found. Durability score 2/5: 4.5 years for the official site; the bonds lasted longer than Kirin's involvement.
  5. No continuity for its most loyal customers. Kirin's later efforts were the "キリンビール大学" content site, a third-party-hosted 一番搾りコミュニティ on QON's Beach (2021, since dissolved), and the subscription-gated CLUB SVB for Spring Valley. None of them carried over Kanpai Kaigi members or their identities. One fan had asked キリンビール大学 "many times" for a Kanpai Kaigi reunion, and then found Beach wouldn't let her change her handle from an old Asahi fan site's "アサヒキャット" [34][36][37][38].
  6. Content engine score 3/5: frequent, launch-tied prompts and real events, but mostly extractive (tell us what you think) rather than useful to members, apart from the recipe collaboration and seminars.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Start with one concrete co-creation project with a date and a tangible output (a beer on tap by March). It gives people a reason to join and a story for the press [1][9].
  2. Mix online discussion with offline moments for the core, such as tastings, factory tours and an annual thank-you party. Those meetings create the peer bonds that make people return [11][24][31].
  3. Keep a visible cadence and progress loop. Weekly prompts, points for contributing and for being liked, and a public quarterly leaderboard were remembered for years [5][24][30].
  4. Publish criticism of your own product. Members read tolerance for harsh reviews as respect [29].
  5. Turn the community's work into content (summaries, recipe collaborations, "made by fans" stories), so a small core produces reach for the whole brand [6][12][15].

Avoid these

  1. Don't use prize gates to grow headline membership. You end up with sweepstakes entrants, inflated sign-ups and a reward dependency that breaks the first time the budget is cut [16][23][29].
  2. Don't run the community entirely through an agency and a rented research platform. If your own staff don't know the community exists, nobody inside will defend it at budget time [9][29].
  3. Don't shut down abruptly. Give long notice, explain why, export or archive members' history, and offer a successor or alumni space. Otherwise your most loyal customers leave with the bonds you helped create, and may take their purchases with them [29][32].
