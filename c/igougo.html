TL;DR

  • What it was. IgoUgo (2000–2014) was a New York startup where members (called "guides") wrote long, multi-entry travel journals, reviews and photos. Staff editors read and rated every entry. In February 2005 Sabre bought it and folded it into Travelocity, and Travelocity shut it in November 2013 after a 13-year run (PhocusWire, Sabre 10-K).
  • Why people came back. Four things: a points currency that could actually be spent (GO Points turned into Amazon gift cards and American Airlines miles); an editor's star rating and feedback on every entry; status (Editor Picks, the U GO! Awards, a Hall of Fame); and real friendships, built at annual member meet-ups. Top members wrote hundreds of journals over ten years.
  • What the company fed it. A paid editorial team that copy-edited and rated everything, writing guidance, newsletter features, award galas and press coverage of members. Later came a community blog and social media. It spent little on SEO or scale.
  • Biggest mistake. After the acquisition, Sabre and Travelocity treated a writing community as a TripAdvisor-style review and ad-traffic play. The founders left, the site was relaunched to look like TripAdvisor, and it kept losing money (about $6–7M a year in 2005–06). It closed with less than a month's notice, so members who hadn't exported their work lost it.
  • The one lesson. Pay-for-quality and recognition built a loyal core of skilled writers. But that core was a few thousand people, which can't support an ad business that needs millions of search visits. Make sure the community's economics match what the parent company needs from it, or the parent will eventually shut it down.

What the community is

IgoUgo ("I go, you go") launched in 2000 as "a unique community of travel enthusiasts sharing experiences through travel journals and photos". Contributors were "guides", their profile was a "Passport", and there was a Message Center, a Photo Gallery with photo contests, and the GO Points rewards program (About page, June 2001). The basic unit was the journal: several entries (overview, lodging, dining, activities, tips) about one destination. Editors rated each entry from 1 to 5 stars (FAQ, 2006).

Spaces over time:

  • Owned site igougo.com: journals, reviews, photos, message boards, later "Travel Forums", a "Travel Blog", "Best of IgoUgo", "U GO! Awards" and a "Member Hall of Fame" (2012 snapshot).
  • IRL: annual member get-togethers. The third, in Vancouver in September 2004, ended with a gala awards dinner (2004 U GO! Awards release). In 2005 there were gatherings in New York and London (member comment, 2014).
  • White-label communities run for partners: Travel Channel "Travelers' Reviews", ASTA "TravelSense", Rough Guides, Cendant's RCI Community and European tourism boards (Nov 2004 release).
  • Social: a Facebook page (10,710 likes) and @igougo on X, run from 2009 by a social media manager (Facebook, X).
  • Unofficial: FlyerTalk threads where mileage collectors swapped referrals and tactics for earning points (FlyerTalk 2002, FlyerTalk 2007), and threads on the Timeshare Users Group (TUG) forum (TUG).

Status: defunct. New submissions stopped in the week of 19 November 2013. The site stayed up read-only until April 2014 (Wayback, 18 Apr 2014), and by 17 May 2014 igougo.com returned a 301 to travelocity.com/Vacation-Packages (Wayback CDX record). The coordinator's "shut around 2014" is right for the final shutdown. The closure was announced and posting ended in November 2013 (Wikipedia: Travelocity). An unrelated igougo.org now uses the name.

Origin and strategy

Founders. Tony Cheng (CEO) and Jim Donnelly (President) met at Citibank, where Donnelly worked on online-banking marketing. They founded IgoUgo in 2000 "as a way to harness the knowledge of everyday travelers" (2004 release; YesPress profile of Donnelly). Lynn Parramore was the founding editor (Truthout bio). Donnelly has said raising money around September 11 was hard and they had to bootstrap (YesPress). In July 2001 Bloomberg called it "a consumer Web site that delivers what it promises, free of strings, free of ads, and free of charge" (Bloomberg, 2001, paywalled snippet).

Business model, phase 1 (2000–2004). There were three revenue lines:

  1. A paid Travel Club tier, with "Travel on Us" stays at 5-star hotels for $25, free B&B nights and companion tickets (homepage, 2002–03; TravelMole review, May 2002). One FlyerTalk member asked whether the $75 fee was required to earn points. It wasn't (FlyerTalk, Apr 2002).
  2. Booking links and portal deals.
  3. Community-as-a-service licensing: customized travel communities for Cendant, Rough Guides, tourism boards, the Travel Channel and ASTA, pitched as helping partners "increase online sales… generate site traffic and strengthen customer loyalty" (2004 release).

Phase 2: Sabre (2005–2013). Sabre bought IgoUgo in February 2005 for an undisclosed sum, a year after Expedia bought TripAdvisor (PhocusWire; Aviation Week). It is already listed in Sabre's 10-K filed 11 March 2005. Sabre "acquired and relaunched IgoUgo.com" in Q1 2005 with a travel-only "keyword" search engine, meant to help Sabre "participate in the growth in paid search advertising". It also planned "Travel Experts", which would turn Sabre-connected travel agents into community members and "generate leads for the agents" (Sabre Q1 2005 8-K; TravelDailyNews). In Q3 2006 Sabre moved IgoUgo from Sabre Travel Network into Travelocity (Sabre 10-Q). From then on it was run as "the travel review Web site" funded by advertisers: in 2008 it "directs consumers to advertisers such as Orbitz and Expedia" (Travel Weekly, Mar 2008). PhocusWire later called it Travelocity's attempt "to compete with TripAdvisor".

Champions. The founders ran the community until they left soon after the acquisition. Cheng founded TripFilms in 2007, and Donnelly left even sooner (PhocusWire). General managers after that: Peter Campion from mid-2007 (Sabre release, now 404; Travel Weekly), then Aditi Gokhale, who led the 2010 rebuild (PhocusWire 2010).

Reading the trajectory. "Members" means registrations. The figure sat at 350,000 from 2004 to 2008, which suggests either a slowdown or a stale marketing number, then rose to 540,000–650,000 after the 2010 relaunch tied in Facebook login. The active core was much smaller. Its scale shows in the awards: each year about five nominees per category (2011 awards). The Hall of Fame added about seven to nine inductees a year from 2003 to 2009 (Hall of Fame 2006). The most prolific members had huge archives: one had 190+ journals, 1,000 reviews and about 6,000 photos (TUG, 2014), another 100 journals of 5+ entries each (Liddle, 2012). Members said new contributions were thinning out well before closure ("ever fewer new contributions", Mindful Traveler, Nov 2013). Traffic of about 1M uniques a month in 2008 was roughly 1/40th of TripAdvisor's in 2010. No Reddit community existed; Arctic Shift and Hacker News have no relevant IgoUgo discussion.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A currency you could spend, tied to quality. The May 2006 scale (GO Points page):
    • Onboarding: 250 points each for registering, completing your profile and adding a photo.
    • Reviews: 50 for 3 stars, 100 for 4–5 stars. Journal overviews: 200 or 350.
    • Photos: 10 for any original photo, 25 for an outstanding one.
    • Bonuses: 1,000 for your first journal. 1,200–1,300 for a "First Five" or "High-Quality" journal (5+ entries, editor average of 3.8+, 3 photos). Higher payouts for "Hot Destination" reviews, so the company could point writers at the topics it needed. "Member Publicity" paid up to 18,000.
    • 10 points every time a signed-in member read your review, for six months after it was posted.
    • Redemption: 25,000 points = 5,000 AAdvantage miles (FlyerTalk 2002), plus Amazon, eBags and Rough Guides rewards. Members reported real payouts: "$600 in Amazon gift certificates… maybe more" in two years (TUG) and "91,000 AA Aadvantage miles" (TripAdvisor forum, 2009).
  2. Editorial feedback on every entry. "All member journals are reviewed by IgoUgo's editorial staff… The editor will fix errors… A star rating (1–5) is then assigned… and feedback is sent" (FAQ 2006). New members got "an email thanking me and suggesting improvements" (Mindful Traveler). That made the site feel like a writing workshop, which is how Travel Weekly framed it in 2008.
  3. A ladder of status. It ran from featured, spotlight and top guide (top guide needed 5+ journals averaging 4+ stars, per the 2001 FAQ) up through "Best of IgoUgo" ratings, Editor Picks, Staff Picks and "Picks of the Week" in the newsletter. One rookie said he'd "frame the newsletter on my wall" (FlyerTalk 2007). Above that sat the U GO! Awards, held every year from 2002 to 2011 or later. Members nominated and voted; categories included Travel Writer, Photographer and New Member of the Year, plus playful ones like the "Om Nom Nom" food award and the "Mint-on-the-Pillow" luxury award (2004, 2011). At the top was the Hall of Fame (2+ years of outstanding contribution). Members quoted these honours years later in author bios (Travelfore; BlogAdda).
  4. Belonging and peer ties. Profiles let you find members with matching interests. A friends network sent "an email whenever a friend writes a new journal" (FAQ 2006). Hall of Famers talked about friendship, not points: "Soon after I create a journal, I'll hear from someone who's been there… It's addictive" (Idler). "IgoUgo has made it possible to have friends all over the world. Some I've met in person" (samepenny) (Hall of Fame 2006).
  5. IRL rituals. Annual get-togethers (by 2004 the third, with a gala) and city gatherings with staff in 2005 (2004 release; Sullivan comment). There's no evidence they continued after about 2005.
  6. Opportunity. Editors passed on requests from book authors and magazines. One member was offered a press trip (Travel Weekly; Mindful Traveler).

The GM's own read. "Many reviewers never cash in the miles… a good part of what drives writers to review is their desire to share and to be recognized… Ego plays a part" (Peter Campion, Travel Weekly 2008). The points pulled people in (FlyerTalk shows that), and recognition kept them.

The content engine

  • Company-made content was thin. The site ran on member content. Staff produced:
    • "Featured" journals chosen by a monthly theme for each region (FAQ 2001).
    • An early magazine section of feature articles (About 2001).
    • Writing tips "from a bevy of editors… and from within the… community itself" (keep good notes, use active voice) (Travel Weekly).
    • Rating guidelines, a weekly newsletter, and from about 2009 a community blog with member interviews, such as one about a member's published novel (Liddle).
  • Member content repackaged as PR. Press releases such as "Will Work for Travel: IgoUgo Members Tell Travelers How to Take Free Vacations" (PRWeb, 2008) and themed lists like "best places for wieners" and "10 great places to spot monkeys" (media-kit URLs in Wayback CDX). The "Member Publicity" bonus of up to 18,000 points paid members who brought the brand press.
  • Syndication. Member journals were syndicated to partner sites (RCI, Rough Guides, Travel Channel). The FAQ said "we may occasionally share your travel journals with some of our partner sites" (FAQ 2006). Members complained about losing attribution (TUG).

People, programs and governance

  • Editorial staff read, copy-edited and rated every submission, with a stated turnaround of "within hours… more likely within 1–2 weeks". Photos were reviewed separately, and re-edited entries were re-rated (FAQ 2006). The 2001–02 "Meet the Team" page shows about a dozen staff headshots (Wayback image filenames). There are no figures for staff headcount after the acquisition.
  • Rules. Only original photos were allowed, and "any photo determined to have been lifted from another source will be promptly and permanently removed". The terms gave IgoUgo rights to member content (FAQ 2006; TUG).
  • "Travel Experts" (2005). Licensed agents got free homepages and leads, a B2B overlay Sabre added after the acquisition (FAQ 2006).
  • No formal ambassador or moderator program was found. The Hall of Fame and awards did that work informally.
  • Feedback into the product. No evidence of an ideas portal. Members raised technical problems through the forum and email ("He's the boss and will fix!", FlyerTalk 2002).

Growth loops

  • Referral bounty. 500 points per referred sign-up. FlyerTalk members posted referral links openly (FlyerTalk 2002).
  • Mileage-community spillover. American Airlines promoted IgoUgo in one early mailing, and FlyerTalk became a recruiting channel (FlyerTalk 2007).
  • Partner distribution. EarthLink (4.9M subscribers, 2001), Rough Guides, the Travel Channel and ASTA brought writers in through white-label sites (Breaking Travel News; Mindful Traveler, who found IgoUgo through Rough Guides).
  • Awards and press. A 2004 Webby for Best U.S. Travel Website, Forbes "Best Travel Sites" (2006) and World Travel Awards "World's Leading Travel Blog/Online Community" 2007–2009 (Sabre release snippet; WTA).
  • SEO was the weak loop. IgoUgo "appeared… to have not enlisted vast teams of SEO nerds", while TripAdvisor became the default search result (PhocusWire). The site also put a lot of things in the way of readers. It was Flash- and Java-heavy (FlyerTalk 2007), and one TUG reader complained: "I keep clicking and if I am lucky after five or six tries I find 2 paragraphs. Is this just a ploy to get me to read their adds" (TUG).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: it lost money. When Sabre moved IgoUgo into Travelocity, the change "reduces operating income for Travelocity by approximately $6 million and $7 million for the years ended December 31, 2006 and 2005" (Sabre 2006 10-K). IgoUgo's intangible amortization was also bundled with Nexion and SynXis at about $1M a quarter (Q1 2005 8-K). This is the clearest number: the unit lost about $6–7M a year of operating income in its first two years under Sabre.
  • Audience quality (company claim). In 2008, 63% of members reported incomes above $125,000 and 80% booked travel online. Revenue came from advertisers drawn to that demographic (Travel Weekly).
  • Strategic value Travelocity still claimed. Travelocity filed a trademark opposition that forced the startup iGoToo to rename itself VadoVia in 2012 (PhocusWire). IgoUgo content was syndicated to American Express's LOCAL COLOR planning site in 2007–08 (AmEx 8-K).
  • No retention, LTV or Travelocity-conversion figures were ever published. The closure came in 2013, the same year Travelocity handed its U.S. booking engine to Expedia (Wikipedia: Travelocity). That points to a cost cut by a parent shrinking its own operations. This is an inference; no closure rationale beyond "difficult decision" was published.

What they did right

  1. Paid for quality, not volume. Points scaled with editor ratings, and the big bonuses required 5+ entries, a 3.8+ average and photos. The result: polished, guidebook-style writing that surprised newcomers ("some looked extremely professional", Mindful Traveler).
  2. A human editor touched every contribution. Personal feedback turned first-time posters into ten-year contributors and award winners (Mindful Traveler; Liddle).
  3. Recognition people cared about. Named, funny award categories, member voting, a gala and a Hall of Fame. Members were still citing these in professional bios in 2020 (Travelfore).
  4. Rewards that showed up in real life. Amazon cards, miles and part of a camera lens (Sullivan comment). The rewards were credible enough that FlyerTalk recruited for the site for free.
  5. IRL meetups turned a website into friendships (Hall of Fame quotes).
  6. Positioning around inspiration rather than complaints. "While other sites seem to focus on where not to go, IgoUgo is all about advice from others who have had great trips" (PhocusWire 2010). This was a real point of difference for writers, but it was weak for travelers searching for hotel reviews.

What they did wrong

  1. The format didn't match the business. Long journals reviewed by editors produce depth, not the millions of indexed hotel pages an ad and lead-gen business needs. In 2012 IgoUgo had 1M reviews against TripAdvisor's 35M (NBC), and it lost $6–7M a year (10-K). Consequence: when Travelocity itself shrank, it shut IgoUgo.
  2. The founders left right after the acquisition, and the GM changed often (Cheng, then Campion in 2007, then Gokhale in 2010). Consequence: members dated the decline to the sale: "after the site was sold, it lost a lot of that sense of community… shifting focus toward being more of a Trip Advisor competitor" (Sullivan).
  3. A relaunch that copied competitors. The 2010 redesign added Facebook and Twitter sharing and an "I've been here" feature, which critics called "a copycat of… TripAdvisor Trip Friends and TravelPost Connections" (TripAdvisorWatch). The 2012–13 homepage led with "Compare Hotel Prices" and a rate finder (Dec 2013), pushing the journals down the page.
  4. Points paid per view invited gaming and lower quality. Members swapped reads ("when you read someone, it's a good idea to send them a note… It is hoped that they return the favor", FlyerTalk 2002). Mileage farmers worked out hourly rates ("$1.67–3.33/hr"), and one reported "over 50,000 points in the past three weeks" (FlyerTalk 2007). Outsiders noticed: "more details makes it into a trip report when you're sharing with friends than when you are earning points" (FlyerTalk 2002). IgoUgo rescaled the program in May 2006.
  5. Unreliable fulfilment and technology. "I've redeemed 3 times… I haven't received anything in 6 months" (FlyerTalk 2002). Drafts were lost to session timeouts, so the official FAQ told members to write in Word first (FAQ 2006; TUG). Members described "technical glitches… along with some really badly administered programs… a love-hate relationship" (TUG).
  6. Content rights without credit. Syndicated journals appeared on RCI under other names, and members couldn't use their logins there (TUG). Published excerpts carried "attribution to their own editorial staff" (TUG).
  7. A careless shutdown. Notice was posted on the site, by email and on Facebook, but not everyone got it. Members said "less than a month's notice" (TUG) and "I never at any time received notification" (P. Gordon, May 2014). There was no export tool. Content was supposed to transfer to Travelocity (PhocusWire), but the domain was simply redirected to a vacation-packages page. Point balances took months to pay out: one Hall of Famer was owed $226 of $325 until April 2014 (Cantin comments).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Have a human review every early contribution and send specific, kind feedback. It turns first posters into long-term contributors.
  2. Reward quality, scaled to a published rubric. Small, real rewards tied to ratings, with big bonuses only for content that meets a clear bar.
  3. Build a status ladder with memorable rituals. Weekly picks, yearly awards with playful names chosen by member votes, a Hall of Fame for multi-year contributors. People keep and display those honours.
  4. Meet in person once a year and pair it with the awards. Friendships made there outlast product changes.
  5. Treat community members as press talent. Pass on media opportunities and turn members' stories into PR. It recognizes them and markets you.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let the community's job drift from what the parent company needs. IgoUgo was a writers' club being judged as a review-traffic business. If the core loop is depth, either fund it as brand or retention spending, or redesign it for scale before someone else does.
  2. Don't pay per view or per post without anti-gaming design. It draws mercenaries and read-swapping rings, and outsiders read the content as paid.
  3. Don't take members' work and then lock them out of it. Credit authors wherever content is syndicated, provide export, and if you shut down, give months of notice by email and pay out balances right away. The last month is what members remember.

Score notes

  • Retention mechanics 4/5. A full stack: spendable points tied to quality, editor ratings, a status ladder, awards, Hall of Fame and IRL meetups. Points paid per view were gameable, which costs it a point.
  • Content engine 3/5. Strong editorial review of member work, but little company-made content, and syndication didn't credit authors.
  • Member ownership 2/5. The company owned the content rights, there was no export, and members had no say in governance or the closure.
  • Staff investment 3/5. Editors read and rated every entry and ran award galas. Investment fell after the founders left in 2005–07, though evidence on post-acquisition staffing is thin.
  • Business impact evidence 2/5. The only hard number is the SEC disclosure of about $6–7M a year in operating losses. Everything else is traffic and demographic claims.
  • Durability 2/5. It lasted 13 years, but declined from the 2005 sale and ended abruptly.
