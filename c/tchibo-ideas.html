How it pays off for the company

Exclusive own-label non-food products for Tchibo's weekly-changing 'Themenwelten' (23 in five years). Cheap consumer insight and member interviews, brand 'openness' and PR, and the company's first community experience, reused on Facebook. Later product-test and market-research panels, and from 2016 a loyalty program tied to the TchiboCard and newsletter sign-ups.

TL;DR

  • What it was. Tchibo, Germany's coffee roaster and weekly-changing general-goods retailer, launched tchibo-ideas.de on 12 May 2008. It was one of Germany's first corporate crowdsourcing platforms. Consumers posted everyday problems ("Aufgaben"), designers and hobby inventors posted solutions ("Lösungen"), and the community voted. Winners got cash every month, and a celebrity jury picked a €10,000 "Lösung des Jahres". By October 2013 it had 11,092 members and 23 products on Tchibo shelves.
  • Why people came back. A monthly contest cycle with cash prizes, the realistic chance of seeing your idea in more than 800 Tchibo stores with your face on the packaging, and a yearly live gala in Hamburg. After 2013 the draw was free products: apply-to-test campaigns, workshops at headquarters and votes, each followed by a published results post.
  • What Tchibo fed it. A small marketing and innovation team (founder Miguel Helfrich, then Turadj Schahbasi), a star jury (Sebastian Conran, Meinhard von Gerkan, Brigitte Huber), an online magazine, an annual event, design-school cooperations, and integration into its stores, customer magazine and webshop.
  • The biggest mistake. It never fixed the throughput problem: about 1.8% of posted problems became products, and only ideas that fit Tchibo's weekly themes and economics could ship. In October 2013 Tchibo responded by removing public submissions and discussion altogether. That turned co-creators into testers and sweepstakes applicants, and in 2016 the brand was folded into the TchiboCard loyalty community.
  • The lesson. Close the loop in public. The inventor's photo on the box was the single most powerful signal Tchibo had. Size the open funnel to your real capacity to build, so that "co-creation" does not decay into "apply to win a free sample".

What the community is

Tchibo ideas ran on tchibo-ideas.de (Germany, with AT/CH entry points) from a blogger beta in April 2008, through an official launch on 12 May 2008, until about September/October 2016 [1][2][3]. It went through three distinct phases:

  1. Open crowdsourcing contest, May 2008 to Oct 2013. Registered members posted problems and solutions, rated them with a "Hot" button, and commented. Monthly winners for problems and solutions advanced through an intermediate and a final round. A jury chose the solution of the year from the 36 monthly winners [4][5][6]. The site had a "Treffpunkt" social layer: the ideasMAGAZIN, Hauspost (announcements), a forum and member profiles [7].
  2. "Mitmachen. Mitreden. Mitgestalten." participation platform, 7 Oct 2013 to 2016. Public problems and solutions were removed. The site became a hub of "Aktionen": product tests mailed home, workshops in Hamburg, surveys and votes, plus a channel for designers to submit ideas privately and the university cooperations [8][9][10].
  3. Folded into the TchiboCard Community, 2016 to today. From about October 2016, tchibo-ideas.de 301-redirects to community.tchibo.de. That site, first archived on 28 Oct 2016, is restricted to TchiboCard holders and offers product tests, a "Community Café", a community cookbook and "Tchibo Momente" photo posts [11][12]. It is still active in 2026, with levels, badges, TreueBohnen loyalty points and a coffee-ambassador program [13][14].

This report covers Tchibo ideas, meaning phases 1 and 2. The TchiboCard Community is treated as its successor, and is arguably worth its own entry.

Origin and strategy

  • The business problem. Tchibo sells a different assortment of non-food products every week (in 2012 it had €3.6bn revenue and more than 1,000 stores) [9]. That model needs a fast flow of new product ideas. Sales chief Stefan Pfander told WirtschaftsWoche in 2008 that product development took a year, which "meant enormous risks regarding order quantities" [15]. The ESMT teaching case quotes Consumer Goods director Dr. Sven Groos on the pressure to "maintain a fast pace of innovation while consistently meeting consumer needs". It says Tchibo pledged in 2007 to adopt new ways of discovering what consumers wanted [16].
  • The champion. Miguel Helfrich, Marketing Manager for Innovation Management Consumer Goods, was called the platform's "Gründervater" (founding father) [7][2]. His team first considered a design award but rejected it: "they exist like sand on the sea… we didn't only want a professional jury, but as many 'normal' people as possible" [17]. The agency .PEPPERZAK created the name, claim ("Die besten Ideen gewinnen"), logo, information architecture and site design [18].
  • Image repair. WirtschaftsWoche framed the launch as part of Tchibo's push "away from its cheap image" [15]. Die Welt linked it to long-running accusations from German brand manufacturers that Tchibo sold copies. Sennheiser and Cloer had won court orders against Tchibo products [19].
  • Later framing. By 2010, Turadj Schahbasi (head of business development at Tchibo direct, and later described as head of online marketing) said sales were not the main goal: "The goal lies in the special form of customer involvement and communication… we see Tchibo ideas as a long-term strategy" [20]. In 2012 he valued it mainly as early community experience "that pays off today in marketing on Facebook" [21]. The 2013 relaunch under Dr. Wolfgang Merkle (Director Consumer & Brand) recast it as an "all-encompassing participation platform" covering assortment, coffee and travel [9].

Trajectory. Membership grew fast in year one (6,000 by July 2009), then slowed to roughly 1,000 net new members a year (8,000 in 2010, ~9,000 in 2011, 10,000+ in 2012, 11,092 in 2013). The 8,721 comments over five years average fewer than 150 a month, which is modest for a big retail brand. The "one million monthly visitors" figure from early 2009 is an unaudited company claim. It sits awkwardly beside 4,000 regular users and should be treated with caution. After the relaunch, engagement per action rose sharply (hundreds of comments on a free-sample test), but those comments are mostly test applications and thank-yous, not discussion between peers.

What keeps people coming back

Phase 1 (2008–2013): contest rituals with a real-world payoff

  • A monthly cycle. Every month the community chose the best problem and the top three solutions. Prizes in 2008 were €1,200 for the problem and €2,000 for the solution [15]. The figure was €1,000 per winner when voting reopened in 2009 [19], and "up to €2,000" in 2010 [4]. Rating criteria were published: "creative, sensible, beautiful, well thought-out, want it" [20].
  • An annual jury award and live event. A jury of product designer Sebastian Conran, architect Meinhard von Gerkan and Brigitte editor Brigitte Huber picked the €10,000 "Lösung des Jahres" from the 36 monthly winners. The 2009 award was presented at a gala in a former Hamburg heating plant, with poetry slam and around 250 guests [5][2]. A "Tchibo Ideentag" with design talks followed in March 2011 [28].
  • Status that shows up in physical stores. The inventor's photo and name went on the packaging, the webshop and a "Hall of Fame" of realized products. Schahbasi said the idea-giver "can find his face in our shelves" [20][4][10]. The 2009 winner, Berndt Klawitter, a design engineer from Hesse, with his cable winder, is the archetype [4].
  • Access to the company. Tchibo invited members to headquarters for interviews about existing products [21]. Successful ideas were turned into products under licence agreements [19].

Phase 2 (2013–2016): giveaways and being listened to

  • Apply-to-participate "Aktionen": 200 BARISTA coffee testers, a rain-gear "Regen-Olympiade" family test, a home makeover by Tchibo's interior experts (filmed for YouTube), baking, sewing and craft workshops in Hamburg, travel testers, and a "Reise-Auktion" where members set the trip price [8][26][29].
  • Every action promised a published results post. Some actions came out of member requests, such as a family travel test "on frequent request from the community" [8][26].

Phase 3 (TchiboCard Community, 2016 onward): loyalty-program gamification

  • Levels earned through activity and spending, with badges (for example "Produkttester") on member profiles. Level-ups grant TreueBohnen points (for example 500 at level 4), and Level 4 unlocks applications to the ambassador program [13][14].
  • Coffee ambassadors such as "superkundin" Angelika, and recipe contests whose winners are sold in stores: "Lena's Nusskuchen" was on Tchibo's coffee counters for a month [27].

What was missing. There is no evidence of peer accountability, member-run spaces, member moderators or a fan-run space. Reddit has no meaningful Tchibo ideas footprint (Arctic Shift search returned nothing usable). The social glue was always contest and reward, not friendship around a craft. The successor's own podcast pitch of "like sitting on the couch with friends" is the first sign of a social identity, and it came after the co-creation part was gone [27].

The content engine

  • Phase 1. The ideasMAGAZIN online magazine (dozens of archived issues, including inventor portraits like "Geschick, Geschmack, Gefühl" about Klawitter, and a print "Tchibo ideas Magazin 3.0") [7][20]. Hauspost announcements gave submission tips, an explainer on patents ("Zum Schutze deiner Idee"), an interview with Tchibo's innovation department, and anniversary recaps [7]. A published "quality test" checklist let members self-screen solutions for novelty, economics and protectability [20]. There was a newsletter, and Tchibo ideas appeared in the customer magazine, at the point of sale and in the webshop [20].
  • Phase 2. A blog-style feed sorted by category (Workshop, Produkttest, Voting, Umfrage, News, Design News), result recaps, YouTube makeover films (Feb 2014), and blogger product boxes such as the "Tchibo ideas Coffee Box" (Aug 2014) [10][29][30].
  • Member-made content. In phase 1, the problems, solutions and comments were the content. All of it was public, so it also worked as an inventory of what German consumers found annoying (grills and toilet brushes kept recurring, Helfrich noted) [17][19]. That archive was not preserved. Wayback captures from Oct 2013 show old idea, magazine and forum URLs already resolving to the new homepage [7]. In phase 3, member content is photos, recipes and test reviews, and members cede all rights to uploads "without time or territorial limits" [12].

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. Helfrich (founder, 2008–09) and Schahbasi (2010–12) as platform leads. A small team that said it was "part of the community" and answered "around the clock" via comments, the forum and the contact form [20]. Merkle led the 2013 relaunch [9]. In the successor, Pascal (team leader, Tchibo Community; Pascal Ritzel is listed on XING as Lead CRM Manager, Tchibo Community) and Patricia appear on the corporate podcast [27][31]. No headcount was published.
  • Jury and partners. The celebrity jury above [2]. Design-school cooperations included Hochschule Coburg (four years of projects by 2012, with three student products in stores at once) [32], Hochschule Wismar (a bathroom door shelf by Rieke Maasch that sold well enough to be re-run in a variant) [33][10], Brand Academy Hamburg (packaging for "Mein Privat Kaffee", 2015) and Leuphana Lüneburg (trend research, 2015) [26].
  • Rules and integrity. Voting was locked for months in late 2008 and early 2009, then reopened with SMS activation "to ensure a fair competition". Die Welt reported that "abuse is now excluded because everyone who votes must first verify themselves" [19][7]. Members were told to protect their ideas, and Tchibo promised help with that and licence contracts when it produced an idea [19][7].
  • Feedback into the product. For phase 1, the documented route is the 23 realized products plus interviews at headquarters [8][21]. For phase 2, results were fed into product development, for example the in-home furniture tests [9]. The successor's terms say it "is not a forum for complaints about Tchibo products" and redirect complaints to customer service [12].

Growth loops

  • Blogger seeding (backfired). In the week of 21 April 2008, an agency sent anonymous numbered parcels to roughly 100–150 German bloggers, each holding a Motorola F3 phone, a toothbrush, a wet razor and a logo card. A reveal letter from "Dein Tchibo ideas Team" followed a week later [22][23][34]. The buzz was real, but Martin Oetting's survey of 12 recipients found none would recommend the site: NPS −100 [22].
  • Retail-media loop. The strongest acquisition loop was Tchibo's own reach. Each realized product carried the story and the inventor's face into stores and the webshop, and the customer magazine and POS promoted the platform [20][4]. Trade press and business-school use amplified it (ESMT case ES1291, 2012; Gassmann's crowdsourcing book, 2010; numerous theses) [16][35].
  • Facebook and sweepstakes funnels (phase 2+). Six actions were piloted on Facebook before the 2013 relaunch [8]. Later product-test calls spread through freebie sites ("Tchibo sucht Produkttester") [36]. The successor requires a webshop account, a TchiboCard and a newsletter subscription to join, so the community doubles as a CRM acquisition funnel [12].

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard numbers. 23 products in five years (Oct 2013) [8]. Schahbasi said in 2010 that some Tchibo ideas products sold better than usual and others as well as normal Tchibo products, and "overall, the numbers match the normal average" [20]. Individual hits were re-run: the ergonomic ice-cream scoop was "sold out" and brought back, and the door shelf was re-offered in a new variant due to "high demand" [9][10]. No revenue, margin, retention or loyalty figures were ever disclosed (Tchibo is private and files nothing with the SEC).
  • The company's own scorecard. Schahbasi in 2012 "reluctantly" measured success by product count. The trade journalist's verdict was blunt: "With 20 products in four years, the portal would probably be a commercial failure". The defense was learning value for Facebook marketing, plus member interviews [21].
  • Cost (estimate). Prize money alone at 2008–10 rates (about €2,000–4,000 a month plus €10,000 a year) suggests roughly €35,000–60,000 a year before staff, agencies (.PEPPERZAK, event agencies) and the gala. That is an estimate derived from the published prize amounts [4][15][19].
  • Academic framing. The ESMT case presents it as genuinely contested: "some see it as a genuine attempt… some see it as a marketing gimmick", with value "unclear" [16].

What they did right

  1. A two-sided brief that lowered the bar. Separating "problems" (anyone can post) from "solutions" (designers and tinkerers) and rewarding both made non-inventors useful. Usability researcher Johanna Möller singled this out as making clear "how relevant recognizing problems and asking the right questions is" [37].
  2. Physical, public proof that ideas ship. The inventor's face on the box, a Hall of Fame and in-store launches answered the usual "idea graveyard" critique of Dell IdeaStorm and My Starbucks Idea. Yannig Roth contrasted Tchibo favorably on exactly this point in 2011 [6][20].
  3. Rituals with stakes. The monthly vote, the annual jury and a real gala gave a cadence and a reason to return [5].
  4. Feeding the funnel from design schools. Semester-long projects with Coburg, Wismar and others delivered professional-grade concepts and shelf-ready products. They outlived the open contest [32][33].
  5. Honest internal metrics and fast adaptation. Tchibo admitted that product sales were average and moved to cheaper, higher-volume participation formats (tests, surveys, votes) that people clearly wanted. There were 500+ applicants for one workshop and 591 comments on one coffee test [8][26].
  6. Lineage. The participation idea survives 18 years on, in the TchiboCard Community [12][27].

What they did wrong

  1. Launch stunt over co-design (2008). The anonymous parcels felt like spam to the people a co-creation platform most needed. Recipients said Tchibo should have involved bloggers in designing the platform [22][15]. Consequence: a negative launch narrative ("Tchibo has an idea: copy Starbucks", "AAL: Andere arbeiten lassen", German for "let others do the work") [34].
  2. Cash prizes plus public voting led to manipulation. Voting had to be shut for months and rebuilt around SMS verification in March 2009 [19][7]. Consequence: lost momentum in year one, and a structural incentive to farm votes rather than improve ideas.
  3. A narrow buyer behind an open funnel. Only ideas that fit the weekly themes and Tchibo's economics could ship [21]. Some 1,262 problems and 763 solutions produced 23 products, a ~1.8% conversion from problem to product. Most contributors never saw an outcome beyond a monthly vote.
  4. A credibility gap on IP. A retailer sued by Sennheiser and Cloer over look-alikes now asked the public for its ideas. Critics questioned the rights terms ("how fast one may sign away one's rights for the chance of €10,000") [19][38].
  5. The 2013 relaunch removed the community's core. "There will be no more public problems and solutions". Discussion among users "falls away" [10]. Matias Roskos called it "strategically a big mistake… Tchibo ideas will become a pure test platform" [10]. Old member content disappeared from the web [7]. Consequence: comment sections filled with applications to win things. The first relaunch post drew pleas such as "I could really use help… my room is living room, bedroom and sewing room in one" [8].
  6. Dissolved into a loyalty program (2016). The successor gates participation behind a TchiboCard, a webshop account and a newsletter subscription, and bans complaints. That is good CRM but no longer co-creation [12].

Lessons for building a product community

Score rationale.

  • Retention mechanics, 3. The monthly and annual contest cadence and the in-store payoff worked, but there were no peer bonds.
  • Content engine, 3. A magazine, announcements, recaps and events, all modest.
  • Member ownership, 2. Members originated ideas but had no say over what shipped, and their public space was removed in 2013.
  • Staff investment, 3. A dedicated team, jury, prizes, agencies and a gala, at small scale.
  • Business impact evidence, 2. 23 products with "average" sales, and no financial data.
  • Durability, 3. Eight years under the brand, and a still-active successor.

Copy these

  1. Make shipped member work visible where customers shop. Put the contributor's name and face on the thing (packaging, changelog, release notes), and keep a public Hall of Fame.
  2. Split "problem posters" from "solution builders" and reward both. It lets ordinary users contribute without being inventors, and gives your product team a ranked problem backlog.
  3. Run a predictable cadence with stakes. Monthly winners and an annual live event with credible outside judges created a calendar people returned for.
  4. Publish your selection criteria up front. A self-check (novel, buildable, economic, protectable) reduces disappointment and raises submission quality.
  5. Always close the loop on every call to action. Tchibo's habit of posting results after each test, survey or workshop kept phase 2 engagement high even without peer discussion.

Avoid these

  1. Don't build a wide-open idea funnel in front of a narrow build capacity. Throughput of 1.8% means most members never see an outcome. Scope the call (themed challenges tied to the roadmap) or raise capacity.
  2. Don't pay cash for public votes without identity and anti-gaming design from day one. Tchibo had to freeze voting for months.
  3. Don't "relaunch" by deleting the community's public work. Removing public submissions and the archive turned co-creators into giveaway applicants, and the platform never recovered its original identity.
