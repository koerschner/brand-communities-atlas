How it pays off for the company

Free remixable hosting was the funnel. Money was supposed to come from companies paying to put starter apps in front of developers (Glitch for Platforms/Teams, e.g. Slack, Google) and from Glitch Pro ($10/mo 'boosted' apps, April 2020). Under Fastly, Glitch was meant to build developer brand awareness and feed Compute@Edge. Fastly called Glitch's results 'not material'.

TL;DR

  • What it was: Glitch (Fog Creek Software → Glitch, Inc. → Fastly) was a browser IDE plus free instant hosting, wrapped in a social layer: remix, profiles, "thanks", collections and later playlists, featured apps, and a Discourse forum. It sold itself as "the friendly community where everyone builds the web." It ran from 2016 (as HyperDev) and 2017 (as Glitch) until hosting and profiles went dark on July 8, 2025 (Glitch blog).
  • Why people came back: their live apps (Discord bots, class projects, art, bots, BTS chart trackers) ran on glitch.me URLs, so the hosted app was itself the retention hook. On top of that came remix culture, real-time "raise your hand" help, a thanks button, and steady editorial curation that gave beginners a shot at being featured.
  • What the company fed it: a full-time Director of Community from launch (Jenn Schiffer), weekly and then monthly curation ("This Week on Glitch", "Apps We Love", "Last Month on Glitch"), starter kits and zines, a Vox-distributed podcast, Friday livestreams, and monthly Code Jams in 2023–24.
  • Biggest mistake: it never matched the community to a business model that could pay for millions of always-on free containers. In June 2020 the free-hosting "deal" was cut (pinging services banned 11 days after staff said they'd rather not ban them), alienating the large Discord-bot cohort. Staff then fell from ~50 to 14 (The Verge), the beloved "Ask for help" feature was quietly dropped, and Fastly ended hosting on ~6.5 weeks' notice.
  • The one lesson: when the community's core artifact (a live, hosted creation) costs you money per member, community warmth isn't a business model. Price, cap and fund the artifact early and openly, or the community dies with the infrastructure.

What the community is

Glitch's "community" was mostly in-product, not a separate forum:

  • glitch.com itself: every project had a public page, "View source" and a Remix button that cloned it into your account with live hosting. Users had profiles and team pages, could thank creators, and from 2017 to 2021 could curate Collections, which became Playlists in September 2021 ("over 100,000 to explore", Glitch blog). Glitch staff curated featured apps on the homepage.
  • Ask for help (🙋 "raise your hand"): from the editor, a beginner could post a help request on the homepage, and any community member could jump into their project in real time to help (Glitch blog, Mar 2018). It was "temporarily disabled" in February 2019 and gone by November 2022 (forum).
  • Glitch Community Forum (support.glitch.com, Discourse): began as the HyperDev support forum (its welcome topic is dated March 9, 2016) and was renamed from "Glitch Support" to "Glitch Community Forum" by 2024. It had categories for help, feature ideas, "The Gallery" showcase, "Building Glitch" (feedback on the new Glitch), and weekly "Community Open" threads (DEV, Nov 2023).
  • Events and streams: monthly Glitch Community Code Jams (first one November 2023, theme "self-portrait"), with a weekly Friday YouTube stream, "Glitch Code Jams Live" / "Glitch Jams Live" (YouTube playlist).
  • Unofficial spaces: "Glitchcord", which billed itself as "the first glitch.com discord server! WE ARE NOT OFFICIAL" (top.gg). There's no meaningful subreddit: Arctic Shift lists r/glitchdotcom with 40 posts in total (checked 2026-11-06). Large outside communities used Glitch as infrastructure: freeCodeCamp's back-end curriculum (Glitch/Medium), A-Frame/WebXR, Discord-bot guides, teachers, and the BTS ARMY (keith.is).

Status: defunct. The dashboard and code download were promised through the end of 2025, and subdomain redirects "at least through the end of 2026" (Glitch blog). On 2026-11-06, glitch.com returned a 307 redirect to blog.glitch.com, and support.glitch.com didn't respond (checked from this machine).

Origin and strategy

  • Origin: a hack project inside Fog Creek Software, the company behind Trello and co-creator of Stack Overflow. It became HyperDev, then Gomix in 2016, then Glitch in March 2017 (TechCrunch; Verge 2017). Anil Dash became CEO in December 2016. Glitch dropped its beta label in March 2018 and open-sourced its community site (Engadget). Fog Creek renamed itself Glitch in 2018 and raised a $30M Series A from Tiger Global (closed November 2018, announced July 2019).
  • Strategy: "YouTube, but for apps, bots, web art and VR" (Dash, June 2018). The community was the product positioning. Friendliness was a deliberate brand promise: a code-of-conduct template for apps (Glitch blog, Dec 2017), a thanks button, and featuring under-represented creators on the homepage.
  • Business model: free for individuals, with money from (1) companies that wanted to reach developers: "it does charge companies that are looking to reach developers, like Slack and Google" (TechCrunch), through "Glitch for Your API" / "Glitch for Platforms" starter apps (Glitch blog) and Glitch Teams; and (2) from April 2020, Glitch Pro at $10/month or $96/year to "boost" up to five apps so they never sleep (The Verge). Glitch was "completely free to use nearly all of the past three years" before that, and the subscription launch was described as "underwhelming" (The Verge, May 2020).
  • Fastly's rationale (2022): "expand the Company's brand awareness within the developer community" and let Glitch users deploy to Compute@Edge (Fastly 10-K FY2022). In other words, the community was bought as a developer-marketing and acquisition channel.
  • Champions: Anil Dash (CEO, later Fastly VP of Developer Experience) and Jenn Schiffer (Director of Community "from its launch and through its acquisition by Fastly", jennschiffer.com).
  • An ironic promise: "We're not building Glitch just so we can sell it to some big company in a year and let the community fall apart" (Dash, June 2018).

Trajectory: fast growth from 2017 to 2020, with forum activity peaking right as the platform buckled under load in spring 2020. After the June 2020 pinging ban, the layoffs and the union saga, forum activity fell about 80% by early 2024 (30-day posts went from 7,004 to 449). The late-2023 to 2024 revival (Code Jams, livestreams, the new-Glitch preview) roughly doubled it before the shutdown.

What keeps people coming back

  1. The hosted artifact (the real hook). Each project lived at a live glitch.me URL, so users came back to keep their bot, class site or art piece running. This was also the community's fatal flaw. Free apps slept after 5 minutes. Users kept them awake with UptimeRobot pings, which staff themselves recommended in 2018 (forum, Sept 2018), until the cost forced a permanent ban on June 22, 2020 (forum). In July 2020 came "Project Hours": 1,000 hours a month of free uptime and always-on free static sites (Glitch blog).
  2. Remix as onboarding. No blank page and no account needed to start. Partners (Slack/Botkit, Google, freeCodeCamp, A-Frame, DataStax) shipped starter apps people remixed. Remix gave newcomers instant success and tied them to the original creators.
  3. Peer help in real time. "Raise your hand" put help requests on the homepage, and helpers jumped into the code live. Members remember it as what "made Glitch a hit" (forum, Oct 2024), and some helped strangers for fun when bored (forum, Nov 2022).
  4. Recognition. The thanks button ("No thanks: the biggest missing feature in coding culture"), homepage features, "This Week on Glitch" / "App of the Week" (with a submission form, DEV), awards like "Most Shared on Social Media" (BTS graphs, two years running, X), and shout-outs on the livestream.
  5. Rituals (late era). Monthly Code Jams with themes (self-portrait, gift-giving, "your dream default stylesheet", cool bugs, rainbows), the weekly Friday stream, weekly "Community Open" threads, and a monthly newsletter.
  6. Identity. The 🎏 koi mascot, the "friendly community" slogan, diversity-forward staffing and featuring (TechCrunch), Glitch in Bio link pages (November 2021), and glitch.me subdomains used as Bluesky/Mastodon handles (keith.is).

What was missing: no streaks, levels, badges on glitch.com, or leaderboards (Discourse trust levels existed only on the forum). Glitch never added project comments (forum, Oct 2024), so feedback between creators flowed only through the forum or social media.

Score, retention mechanics: 3/5. There were strong hooks (hosted apps, remix, live help), but the strongest one became a cost center and was throttled. Social features stayed thin, and live help was removed.

The content engine

  • Curation as content: "This Week on Glitch" (2018, Medium, with guest illustrators), "Apps We Love" / "The Playlist" blog category, "Last Month on Glitch" (2023), App of the Week, and staff playlists ("Tools to make your meetings better").
  • Education: website and React starter kits, the "You Got This" zine, classroom guides (How Glitch Is Used in the Classroom), and help-center docs.
  • Thought leadership: Function with Anil Dash, "produced by Glitch and the Vox Media Podcast Network" (Apple Podcasts), plus Dash's high media profile ("constantly punched above our weight", keith.is).
  • Partner content: "Glitch for Your API" starter apps embedded in partners' READMEs and galleries. That was sponsored content and revenue at the same time.
  • Live: Friday YouTube streams hosted by Jenn Schiffer (about 20+ episodes in 2023–24).
  • Member-made content: remixable starters and playlists ("being a playlist curator is a great way to be a creator on Glitch", Glitch blog). The newsletter amplified members' own blog posts and forum showcases.

Score, content engine: 4/5. The engine was distinctive, steady and community-centered. It loses a point because curation and in-product discovery stayed weak ("maybe you can help us improve our search!", Dash, 2024).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: Director of Community from launch. In 2019 the forum listed about 8 admins and about 20 staff moderators, including Dash himself (Wayback 2019). By 2020 that was 6 admins and 6 moderators. The 2023–25 moderator roster included community members: Pomax was an admin and moderator in July 2025, alongside staff such as Sue Smith, Jenn Turner and Austin Spires (Wayback 2025). Jenn Turner described herself as "curator for Glitch content the past few years" (forum).
  • Super-users: no formal ambassador/MVP program was found (no evidence found). In practice, prolific volunteer helpers (Pufferfish101007, wh0, EddiesTech, khalby786, javaarchive) kept wiki PSAs such as "Glitch does not allow pinging services" (forum) and answered most help threads.
  • Rules: a code of conduct, forum "best practices" for help requests (forum), report-abuse buttons, and a dedicated Abuse Team account.
  • Feedback loop: forum categories for Feature Ideas and "Building Glitch". The community site was once open source and was promised to be open-sourced again in 2024. A public preview of the "new Glitch" was opened to the community in late 2023.
  • Labor context: workers unionized with CWA in March 2020 and signed white-collar tech's first collective bargaining agreement in 2021. The union dissolved in early 2022 with three members left (Verge).

Scores, member ownership: 3/5. Members did the support work and curation, and a community member became admin, but they had no say over the platform's fate. Staff investment: 3/5. Glitch had senior community leadership from day one, but the team shrank to about a dozen people.

Growth loops

  • Remix and share: every app was a public, shareable URL with "Remix your own", so a viral app recruited new creators. The BTS ARMY apps brought "thousands of fans" (keith.is).
  • Partner and curriculum distribution: freeCodeCamp campers had to build their APIs on Glitch. The millionth app came from a freeCodeCamp student (Medium). API companies embedded Glitch starters, and teachers and workshops ("does not require setting up accounts", webxr-workshops) spread it in classrooms.
  • Discord-bot tutorials made Glitch the default free bot host until the 2020 ban ("Bye bye Discord community").
  • Embeds and press: Glitch embeds on blogs, plus Dash's profile in the press.
  • The same loop fed abuse. Free, reputable subdomains plus remix made Glitch a phishing staple: BleepingComputer, Nov 2021; a 2025 campaign against 830+ organizations "remixing templates… spawning numerous fraudulent subdomains" (Cyberpress, June 2025).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: Fastly paid $34.9M in cash (May 18, 2022). Of that, $32.6M was booked as goodwill, and identifiable intangibles were just $2.0M, including only $0.76M for "customer relationships". "The financial results of Glitch… are not material" (Fastly 10-K FY2022). Before the deal, the Glitch × Compute@Edge beta "saw more than 2,000 enterprise developer sign-ups in the first week" (Fastly Q4 2021 release). That is the clearest proof that the community worked as an acquisition channel.
  • Monetization evidence: the Pro launch was "underwhelming", and about a third of staff were laid off a month later (Verge). CWA said downsizing "made it clear that the business was unlikely to recover" (Verge).
  • Company claims: "the friendliest dev community in the world"; millions of users and tens of millions of apps. Retention, churn, LTV and NPS: no evidence found.
  • A former engineering lead put it this way: "we proved that you can't build a sustainable platform around helping people create rather than consume… The math just doesn't work when hosting costs real money" (keith.is).

Score, business impact evidence: 1/5. There's no public proof that the community made money. The filings say Glitch was immaterial.

What they did right

  1. Lowered the first step to zero. No setup, no account and instant hosting made remixing a working app the first experience, which is the strongest activation design in this category. Partners like freeCodeCamp built curricula on it (Medium).
  2. Designed friendliness into the product, not just the forum: hand-raise help, a thanks button, codes of conduct on apps, featured under-represented creators (Glitch blog). Even on shutdown day, users thanked the team ("congrats on operating 9 years").
  3. Curated relentlessly. Weekly and monthly showcases, App of the Week and playlists made ordinary members feel seen and gave outsiders a reason to browse.
  4. Gave late-era rituals a real cadence. Monthly jams plus a weekly stream plus Community Open threads roughly doubled forum activity from the February 2024 trough (449 → ~1,000 posts per 30 days).
  5. Handled the exit relatively decently: code export, subdomain redirects through 2026, Pro refunds, a migration thread. Parts of HN praised it as "excellent communication and respect for their users" (HN).

What they did wrong

  1. The free-forever artifact had no economics. Three years free, then a $10 tier that "just doesn't compete with cheap VPS solutions" (member, June 2020). The consequence was layoffs in 2020, a 14-person company in 2022, and a sale at roughly the size of its funding.
  2. Whiplash on core rules. On June 11, 2020, staff wrote "We're not banning the use of pinging… and we'd like to not have to" (Glitch blog). On June 22 the ban was permanent (forum). Staff-authored advice had told users to ping. The Discord-bot cohort left, and the forum's 30-day posts fell from 7,004 (May 2020) to 1,445 (January 2022).
  3. Removed the signature community feature. "Ask for help" was deprecated because it was "very difficult to keep it working whilst making all the editor changes" (Jenn, Dec 2022), and it never came back despite repeated requests.
  4. Tech debt starved community work. The prototype launched as-is and "we were constantly at war with our own technology" (keith.is). Node versions went out of date ("we've had to sacrifice moving at the speed of the community", Dash, 2024).
  5. The acquirer never invested. Fastly bought brand awareness and a Compute@Edge funnel. A former Director of Community wrote: "they generously took in a beautiful flower… The problem is they chose to never water it" (livelaugh.blog). The "community discovery platform" pivot, previewed publicly for over a year, was abandoned.
  6. Opaque, short shutdown. About 6.5 weeks' notice, the word "sunsetting", and profiles deleted along with hosting. Members called it "deliberately being vague" (forum, May 22, 2025), and The Verge headlined it "Glitch is basically shutting down" (Verge).
  7. Trust and safety debt. Remix plus free subdomains made Glitch a phishing staple from 2021 to 2025, and Glitch cited abuse costs as a reason to close (BleepingComputer).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make "remix a working example" the first step. A finished, forkable thing beats a tutorial, and partner-made starters double as distribution.
  2. Build help into the product. A hand-raise that lets peers step into a beginner's work turns helpers into regulars. Glitch's members still mourned it two years after it was removed.
  3. Ship gratitude and curation features. Thanks buttons, featured work and member-curated playlists give non-coders a creator role too.
  4. Run a visible cadence: a monthly themed jam plus a weekly host-led stream that showcases member work. It measurably revived a declining forum.
  5. Hire a community lead at launch and give them editorial authority. That role set the "friendly" culture that outlived the company.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let the community's core artifact be an unpriced cost. Set usage limits and paid tiers before habits form. Never recommend a workaround (pinging) that you'll later ban.
  2. Don't remove the signature social feature for engineering convenience. If you must, say why and offer a replacement.
  3. Don't let the community's fate depend on an acquirer's roadmap without a plan. Glitch had no member-owned exit (export-to-community, open-sourced site) until the end, and shutdown messaging that dodges the word "shutdown" burns the trust you built.
