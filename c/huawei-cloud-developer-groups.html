TL;DR

  • What it is: Huawei Cloud's own developer-relations ladder, separate from (and parallel to) the consumer-side HDG/HDE programs. It has three rungs: certified experts (HCDE, launched 2018 as "Huawei Cloud MVP", renamed in 2022), volunteer-run city chapters (HCDG, first chapter Fuzhou Dec 2019) and campus ambassadors (HCSD, 2022). Under them sits a blogger tier, 云享专家 ("Cloud Sharing Experts"), and paid content-creator schemes on the Huawei Cloud community site. It is almost entirely Chinese, with a thin international layer. [1][2][3][4][5][12]
  • Why people come back: status and business, not peer support. Members get a one-year Huawei-certified title, a profile page, a stage at Huawei Developer Conference (HDC) and city "developer days", closed-door roundtables with product teams, flag and plaque ceremonies, and "priority business cooperation" (partner status, marketplace listings, introductions to local enterprises). Many members are CTOs, consultants, trainers and ISV staff who sell services on Huawei Cloud. [1][2][6][7]
  • What Huawei feeds it: staff hosts, Huawei speakers and solution architects, venues in government-backed Huawei innovation centres, an annual awards gala, points and vouchers, and money: "tens of millions" of yuan in expert incentives in 2019, a US$100M developer plan in 2021. [6][13][14]
  • Biggest mistake: confusing a marketing calendar with a community. Most HCDG "salons" are co-organized by Huawei innovation centres and district governments, pitched at enterprise CXOs, and draw 20–80 people. Meanwhile the developer-facing surfaces (SDKs, issues) were left neglected, which blew up publicly in Feb 2026 when a GitHub issue calling the C++ SDK "garbage" drew 541 comments and 1,348 reactions. [3][15]
  • The one lesson: an expert title creates loyalty only when it is scarce and tied to real influence on the product. Huawei Cloud made it renewable and nomination-gated (good), but the same years brought relabeling, shifting numbers and a cancelled incentive plan, so members see a sales channel more than a community.

Origin and strategy

  • 2018: an MVP program copied from Microsoft. The 2018 FAQ defines Huawei Cloud MVP as "an award Huawei grants to opinion leaders and technical professionals", whose job is to "help others understand and use Huawei Cloud, and to optimize the product experience on users' behalf". Benefits were commercial from day one: closed-door meetings with Huawei experts, discount vouchers for DevCloud/PaaS/IaaS, priority for marketplace listings and migration consulting deals, and a role as "product co-builder". MVPs were selected quarterly and needed only one qualifying contribution in the past year (a bug report, an article, a talk). [8][9]
  • 2019: buying scale. At an "inclusive AI open day" in March 2019 Huawei Cloud announced an expert incentive plan of "tens of millions of yuan" to "develop 1,500 experts", and gave "2018 Outstanding Contribution" awards to MVPs and Cloud Sharing Experts. A featured MVP that year was the IoT director of a Xi'an firm that had moved its workloads to Huawei Cloud, an early sign that the program recruits customers' technical leads as advocates. [6]
  • Context: Huawei Cloud was a late entrant; an internal Huawei post in Dec 2018 that leaked publicly complained about confused positioning and management of the cloud business. [22] The developer programs are part of the catch-up push, alongside the US$100M 沃土云创 (Fertile Soil Cloud Innovation) plan of April 2021. [14]
  • 2019–20: city chapters. HCDG Fuzhou held "the country's first HCDG flag-presentation ceremony" on 8 Dec 2019, described as "the first community organization founded and run by local core members". Beijing (Oct 2019), Shenzhen (5 Jan 2020, a blockchain session), Nanjing (Mar 2020), Chongqing (May 2020, inside a government big-data park), Guangzhou (Jul 2020) and Shanghai (Dec 2020) followed. [3]
  • 2022: rebrand to HCDE and "global". In 2022 the MVP program was "comprehensively upgraded" and renamed HCDE "to bring more high-quality benefits to influential developer experts worldwide". HCSD launched the same year. [2][5]
  • 2024–26: AI and Developer Space. Programs were pointed at Huawei's newest products: Pangu models, Ascend AI cloud, the free "Huawei Developer Space" cloud workstation (launched Jun 2024), and the CodeArts coding agent (Feb 2026). HCDE roundtables at HDC 2025 collected HCDE feedback on Developer Space, and a "2025 Developer Space evangelist plan" was launched for HCDEs. [7][23][1]

What keeps people coming back

  1. A title that must be renewed. HCDE and MVP status lasts one year and is re-evaluated "based on contributions and achievements during the period". New lists are announced monthly. [1][8] The renewal turns the title into a yearly obligation: talks, articles, event support.
  2. Contribution points. Experts earn "contribution value" for each task (articles, micro-topics, livestreams, interviews, course or lab development, product tests, staffing a Huawei booth), with rewards for top quarterly and annual totals. [9] The general developer site adds a points mall that restocks vouchers daily at 16:00 and warns against multi-account farming. [28]
  3. Business access, the strongest hook. HCDE benefits include "priority in obtaining Huawei Cloud business cooperation rights… becoming a Huawei Cloud partner" and connecting member companies to Huawei resources. HCDG offers "visits to core members' companies to create business opportunities" and "personal and company brand packaging". [2][4][20] For a CTO or a Huawei-focused consultancy this is worth more than swag, and it explains why HCDE lists are full of company executives.
  4. Stage time and ceremony. Flag ceremonies for new chapters, plaque ceremonies for HCDEs at city developer days, "Most Influential Technical Expert" awards at HDC 2025, and an annual developer gala with award categories such as "outstanding evangelism" and "outstanding community value". [7][29][30]
  5. A ladder. Volunteer → HCDG core member → HCDE "without first-round review", or a recommendation into the Luban Club bootcamp. HCSD ambassadors move from junior to mid to leader level and can be "recommended as a Huawei evangelist". [4][5]
  6. Quotas. HCDG chapters must run at least one event per quarter. HCSD ambassadors must run two events per semester of at least 30/80/150 people (by level), post a news write-up, collect 10/20/40 product suggestions, and publish 2–4 technical articles. [3][5] Quotas keep the calendar full; they also make the program feel like unpaid work.

Missing: peer-to-peer help, in-product social features, member-owned spaces. Members do not appear to gather anywhere Huawei doesn't host.

The content engine

  • Huawei-made: CodeLabs (40 at HUAWEI CONNECT 2023), KooLabs sandbox labs, "扫地僧" (sweeping-monk) meet-the-engineer sessions, livestreams, certification courses (HCCDA etc.), the Huawei Developer Competition, and city "HDC.Cloud Day" events (600+ at the 2023 year-end summit). [23][31][19]
  • Expert stories as marketing: HCDE interviews and profiles ("AI deployment: steady beats fast"), and case stories in which an expert's company adopts ModelArts or CodeArts with quantified gains. [1][2][29]
  • Paid and gamified UGC: 云驻计划 (Cloud Residency Plan) monthly contests ("publish at least 3 original articles about Huawei Cloud products this month… articles with Huawei product links score extra… 200-yuan developer gift pack"), and "content co-creation" tasks that pay signed authors a fee. [17][18] This produces volume and SEO, and it rewards product mentions over insight.
  • Expert-made: HCDE missions include speaking, practice write-ups and organizing salons; Cloud Sharing Experts must blog. The expert page promotes member-written e-books (edge computing, IDEA plugins). [2][10]
  • Online series: "HCDG研习社" (HCDG study society) livestreams, at least 43 episodes by Aug 2022, often product launches (an AOC feature launch) or competition kick-offs. [11]

People, programs and governance

  • Team: no headcount is published. Named roles include the president of the Huawei Cloud Developer Alliance (also head of the app marketplace), directors of developer-ecosystem operations, a developer support and operations department head, and an HCDE "technical brand manager". [23][7] DTSE (developer technical support engineers) number "1,000+ expert teams" across 15 countries. [32]
  • Admission: HCDE today requires 8+ years in the field plus nomination, then a 7-working-day review. [1] That is tighter than the 2018 MVP rule (one qualifying contribution) and it shifts the program toward senior, commercially useful people.
  • Rules: Cloud Sharing Experts lose the title for repeated advertising, >50% plagiarism, or breaking the Huawei Cloud user agreement. [10]
  • Feedback into product: experts are framed as "product co-builders"; HCDE roundtables collect feature requests (Developer Space, 2025). HCSD ambassadors must submit suggestions by quota. [7][5] There is a public suggestion portal (云声·建议). No evidence found of experts' feedback changing a specific product, and the Feb 2026 SDK episode suggests public bug reports did not reach engineers: one commenter advised "file a ticket in the console… without a ticket they won't change the code". [15]

Growth loops

  • Government-backed salons. Many HCDG events are co-hosted with Huawei innovation centres and district governments (Longgang, Nanshan, Foshan Nanhai, Fuzhou Software Park) and pitched at local enterprises ("30+ company representatives", "30+ CTOs"). [3] The loop is enterprise lead generation rather than developer word of mouth.
  • Experts as references. HCDE profiles double as customer case studies; HCDEs speak at Huawei events and bring their companies' adoption stories. [2][29]
  • Campus to certification. HCSD salons pitch Huawei Cloud certifications as "a key to career success"; certification exam drives fill the international events calendar (Türkiye 52, Egypt 27, Thailand 20 listed events, mostly exam reservations). [33][20]
  • Voucher and referral schemes. Points, free certification vouchers and "bring a friend" rewards attract deal-hunters (V2EX threads walk through collecting six certification vouchers per account). [34] They inflate sign-ups more than they build engagement.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. Huawei is private, and no attribution, retention or revenue data for HCDG/HCDE was found.
  • Company claims: registered Huawei Cloud developers grew from 7.5M (Jun 2024) to 10M+ (end 2025); Huawei credits the developer ecosystem broadly. [12][25] Expert case stories claim productivity gains (e.g. "development efficiency +30%" for an HCDE's HiLens project). [29]
  • Counter-signal: in 2026 Huawei Cloud shut down the US$100M 沃土云创 plan "to concentrate resources on core areas", ending new vouchers and exam subsidies. [13] Expert counts on Huawei's own pages fell from "1,000+" to "800+". Business-impact score 1/5: only self-reported activity.

What they did right

  1. Made the title renewable and gated. One-year validity with re-evaluation, and (since the HCDE change) a nomination plus seniority bar, keep the badge from being a lifetime freebie. [1][8]
  2. Paid in the currency members value. Partner status, marketplace priority, introductions to enterprises and brand packaging fit an audience of CTOs and consultants better than T-shirts. [2][8] It is the main reason experts stay.
  3. Built a visible ladder. Volunteer → core member → HCDE → Luban Club, and junior → mid → leader for students, with explicit criteria for each step. [4][5]
  4. Put members on stage. Ceremonies, awards and speaking slots at HDC and city developer days give recognition members can use in their careers. [7][29]
  5. Wrote cadence into the role. A quarterly minimum for chapters and semester quotas for students kept a calendar of events going for six-plus years. [3][5]

What they did wrong

  1. Called corporate events "self-organized community". The HCDG FAQ says chapters are "completely self-built and led by local organizers"; the event list shows most salons co-hosted by Huawei innovation centres or local governments, with Huawei product managers as speakers. [3][4] Consequence: chapters rarely outlive Huawei's local sales push (Changsha has one organizer; many chapter pages stop in 2024).
  2. Turned volunteers into quota-filling staff. Event-size minimums, required news write-ups and product-suggestion quotas for students, and points for staffing Huawei booths make membership look like unpaid marketing work. [5][9]
  3. Rewarded volume and product mentions. Monthly blogging contests score extra for Huawei product links and pay per task, which floods the community with promotional posts. [17][18]
  4. Neglected the real developer surface. In Feb 2026 a GitHub issue on Huawei Cloud's C++ SDK ("Does your boss know how garbage Huawei Cloud's business is?") listed broken READMEs, code that would not compile and hidden dependencies; it went viral, reached 541 comments and 1,348 reactions, and Huawei closed it on 10 Feb after a staff apology, prompting a follow-up PR accusing Huawei of silencing critics. [15] No HCDE or HCDG voice appears in that thread; the expert ladder had no role in fixing developer pain.
  5. Kept relabeling and shifting numbers. MVP → HCDE, "20 cities" vs "30+" vs "40", "1,000+" vs "800+" experts, and the 2026 shutdown of 沃土云创, give members no stable picture of what they belong to. [1][2][4][13][27]
  6. No international follow-through. English pages promise a "global" program, but international activity amounts to certificates handed out at regional competitions and conferences and occasional co-hosted meetups. [19][20][21]

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make expert status annual and earned. Re-evaluate every year on contributions, and announce the list on a fixed rhythm.
  2. Offer benefits your experts' businesses can use: partner status, referrals, marketplace placement, co-marketing. Status that pays keeps senior people around.
  3. Publish the ladder and its criteria, from volunteer to organizer to expert, with a shortcut for proven organizers.
  4. Set a minimum event cadence for chapters (Huawei's floor is one per quarter) so a chapter is a calendar, not a logo.
  5. Give experts a direct line to product teams (closed-door roundtables before launches), and show them what changed because of it.

Avoid these

  1. Don't dress up company events as member-run chapters. If your staff and sales partners plan the agenda, call it a roadshow; real chapters need members choosing topics.
  2. Don't pay for volume. Rewards for article counts and product links buy promotional noise and crowd out useful posts.
  3. Don't build the expert stage while ignoring the issue tracker. Developers judge you by your SDKs, docs and replies; one ignored GitHub issue did more visible damage than years of salons could repair.

Score notes: retention mechanics 2/5 (renewal, points and business access, but no peer or in-product hooks); content engine 3/5 (lots of Huawei-made labs, events and interviews, plus paid UGC that skews promotional); member ownership 2/5 (Huawei certifies, hosts and co-organizes almost everything); staff investment 4/5 (dedicated teams, budgets, government-backed venues, 1,000+ DTSE teams); business-impact evidence 1/5 (self-reported counts only, with contradictory numbers); durability 3/5 (eight years with continuous activity, but repeated relabeling and a cancelled incentive plan).
