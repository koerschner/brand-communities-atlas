TL;DR

  • What it is. CDAC (community.developer.atlassian.com) is Atlassian's Discourse forum for people who build apps on Jira, Confluence, Bitbucket and Trello, and above all for the roughly 2,000 Marketplace Partners who sell those apps. It launched in February 2017, when Atlassian split developers off from its user community, and it is still on Discourse. Correction to the coordinator's note: the September 2026 move that was rolled back was the Developer & Marketplace support ticketing portal (ECOHELP moving to support.atlassian.com), not the forum (thread).
  • Why people come back. Mostly money. Partners' revenue depends on Atlassian's API deprecations, platform moves (Server → Data Center → Cloud; Connect → Forge), revenue-share rates and rate limits, and CDAC is where those changes are announced, explained and fought over. Peer Q&A, expert badges, and access to staff and early-access programs (EAPs) are secondary pulls.
  • What Atlassian feeds it. About 40 staff moderators and developer advocates who post themselves, product-specific announcement categories, a formal RFC program (RFC-1 in Feb 2023, past RFC-148 by Sep 2026), EAP categories, Forge Quest learning badges, plus events outside the forum: Codegeist, Atlas Camp, App Week, Developer Day and Forge Dev Den.
  • Biggest mistake. Business deadlines that ran ahead of the platform's readiness, such as penalty revenue-share rates for Connect apps while Forge still lacked features those apps needed, along with native features that competed with partners' apps and short-notice changes. The forum turned into a record of eroding trust. Its most-liked thread ever (Dec 2025, 1,223 likes) is a protest against new rate limits announced with 48 days' notice.
  • The one lesson. A developer community's health follows the platform's credibility, not its engagement features. CDAC's best tool is the RFC process, which consults members before a decision. It works only when Atlassian visibly changes course in response, and members say that happens too rarely.

What the community is

  • Owned forum. community.developer.atlassian.com runs Discourse (version 2026.11.0, site created 7 Feb 2017) (about.json). Its top-level categories are Announcements; Forge (with subcategories for UI Kit/Custom UI, Platform and Tools, Modules, Adopting Forge from Connect, Forge Rookies and Forge Dev Den); Rovo; Rovo Dev; per-product development categories (Jira, JSM, Confluence and Bitbucket, each split into Cloud, Server and Data Center and paired with an announcements subcategory); Trello; Compass; Marketplace (with a General Discussion subcategory); Atlassian Ecosystem Design; Developer Tools; General, including Request for Comments (RFC); Data Residency; Cloud Migrations; and Early Access Programs, with one subcategory per EAP (site.json).
  • Run by Atlassian's developer advocacy and ecosystem teams (contact: dev-advocates@atlassian.com). The forum lists 44 moderators and 34 admins, almost all titled "Atlassian Staff" (about.json).
  • Adjacent official spaces: the developer docs and changelog on developer.atlassian.com; the ECOHELP support desk; the private Partner Portal; Codegeist hackathons on Devpost; Atlas Camp; App Week; virtual Developer Day (2020–2022); Forge Dev Den livestreams on the Atlassian Developer YouTube channel; and an "Atlassian Developers" chapter on Atlassian Community Events with 3,689 members (ACE).
  • Unofficial spaces: a partner-run "Marketplace Vendor Slack" existed by March 2017 and was still handing out invites through forum members in 2021–22 (thread). A developer started a Discord in April 2019; its invite had expired by December 2022 and members pointed newcomers back to the Slack (thread). Developers have no meaningful subreddit; r/atlassian (about 7.2k subscribers, 3,616 posts all-time, per Arctic Shift) is user-focused.
  • Status: active but shrinking. New public topics fell from about 300 a month (Oct 2022) to 98 (Oct 2026), and 30-day active users fell from 2,152 (Apr 2025) to 1,085 (Nov 2026). Details below.

Origin and strategy

Before 2017, Atlassian developers asked questions in the developer-tagged parts of Atlassian Answers, and the developer ecosystem gathered at AtlasCamp, a developer-only conference that dates to about 2008 (the 2010 edition at Half Moon Bay was billed as the "third annual") (Atlassian blog archive; Gliffy, 2010). Paid Marketplace apps began in 2012 (Atlassian, 2026).

In early 2017 Atlassian moved its user community from Answers to Lithium (later Khoros). At the same time it "separated our developer and user communities so as to be better able to tailor each community to the needs of its members" and put developers on Discourse (announcement). The staff welcome post (22 Feb 2017) listed what the new forum was for: asking questions, polling other vendors, asking "the Atlassian Ecosystem and Marketplace teams direct questions", giving feedback to Atlassian teams, networking and early access (welcome post). The split caused friction. Users asked why the two communities ran on different software (thread), and the decision to "launch the community fresh" without old developer answers drew objections. Atlassian reversed it (see the Atlassian Community report).

The business logic is ecosystem economics. Marketplace apps reach more than 40% of Atlassian customers, and Atlassian keeps a commission on every sale. In 2026 that is 16–17% for Forge apps and 20–25% for Connect apps (revenue-share post; Atlassian, 2026). The forum lowers support costs, because staff and expert partners answer technical questions in public. It gives Atlassian one place to announce and defend platform changes. Since 2019 it has also been the main funnel for moving developers onto Forge, Atlassian's hosted app platform, which Atlassian is making mandatory: Connect reaches end of support on 31 Jan 2027 (announcement).

Internal champions visible in the record: "bentley" (staff), who wrote the welcome post and was the de facto chief moderator; Ralph Whitbeck, ecosystem developer advocate and an early admin; and Ian Buchanan, who in Oct 2024 described himself as having helped "found this developer community" and having served as its "unelected and unassigned 'chief community moderator'", with more than 500 replies that year. He called the RFC program his "biggest point of pride" before moving to a Rovo role (post). Tim Pettersen (Head of Developer Experience in 2022) and advocates such as Lars Klint, Ian Ragudo and Caterina Curti are the current faces (ACE).

Trajectory. New topics held at 170–190 a month from 2017 to 2020. They climbed to about 300 in 2022–23, when the Forge push and the Server end-of-life were creating new questions, and then fell steadily to 98 in Oct 2026, about a third of the peak. Posts per topic grew, so discussion has gathered into fewer, longer, more contentious threads. Of the 20 most-liked topics of all time, about 15 are partner complaints or demands; the top one is the Dec 2025 rate-limits thread (174 posts, 11,637 views, 1,223 likes) (top.json). Only 288 people posted in the last 30 days, so the active core is a few hundred partner engineers and founders. My explanation for the decline, which is an untested hypothesis: Server and Data Center questions are fading out (Data Center reaches end of life 28 Mar 2029, and sales to new customers ended 30 Mar 2026), routine how-to questions are moving to AI assistants and documentation, and partners may be quietly disengaging.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Economic necessity (the dominant driver). Partners must follow CDAC because changes that decide their revenue appear there first or are explained there: Connect end of support (announced Mar 2025, extended to 31 Jan 2027), the 2026 revenue-share rates, Data Center end of life (Sep 2025), point-based rate limits (Dec 2025), trust-program requirements (RFC-124 and RFC-133, 2026), and the "Atlassian Enterprise Certified" tier (Oct 2026) (top.json yearly). This is a switching cost for the vendor, not a habit, and it is why heavy users keep posting even when they are angry.
  2. Direct access to Atlassian engineers and PMs. Staff answer in public. In the rate-limits thread Atlassian staff wrote 31 of the 174 posts and eventually confirmed that Forge front-end traffic would be exempt (Apr 2026) (thread). A 2020 "Appreciation post for all you Atlassians that are active on CDAC" thanked named staff for "keeping us in the loop" (thread).
  3. RFCs and EAPs: a seat at the table. Since Feb 2023 Atlassian has posted proposals with fixed Publish, Discuss and Resolve dates. RFC-1 states the problem plainly: "Atlassian ships too many problems to our ecosystem" (RFC-1). Early-access programs each get a private-feeling subcategory (Forge Cache EAP, Confluence Space Aliases EAP, and others), which gives partners an advantage in what they know.
  4. Status and progress. Discourse badges have been extended with product-specific expert levels: "Jira Expert - Level 1" (588 holders) up to "Level 5" (1), "Forge Expert" (390 at L1), "Atlassian guru" and "Atlassian All Star" (1 each), and a "Marketplace Partner" badge ("I have an app in the Marketplace!", 1,375). Habit badges barely register: 21 people have "Aficionado" for 100 consecutive days and 4 have "Devotee" for 365 (badges.json). Partner Marketplace tiers (Platinum, Gold, Silver, published since Jan 2023) give a commercial form of status (tier update).
  5. Learning challenges with badges. Forge Quest (2024–) is an afternoon-long self-guided coding challenge with badges and swag. Finishers post in completion threads: "Your First Quest" drew 146 participants and "Apprentice" 70 (thread; thread; blog).
  6. Annual rituals. Codegeist each autumn and Atlas Camp each February (Vienna 2019; virtual Developer Days 2020–22; "Atlas Camp is back" in Brussels 2025; Amsterdam 2026; Bengaluru June 2026) give the year a rhythm, with in-person time with Atlassian engineers (call for speakers).
  7. Recognition rituals, now mostly dormant. "New User of the Month" (172 grants) and a one-off "May 2021 Community Recognition" badge (21) show that monthly shout-outs were tried (badges.json). I found no sign of them running recently.
  8. Peer solidarity. In practice, being "the people who all have the same problem with Atlassian" is a strong bond. Partners co-sign each other's threads, list each other's blocking FRGE/ECO tickets, and coordinate pressure, as in the 46-post "Forge delivery vs. the upcoming Atlassian Connect penalty" thread with 495 likes (thread).

The content engine

  • Atlassian-produced: reference docs and a developer changelog; per-product announcement subcategories; developer blog posts; quarterly Forge roadmap webinars (from 2022, recorded to YouTube); Forge Dev Den livestreams ("live code with us"), including a university capstone showcase (YouTube playlist); a 2022 Developer AMA series with partners such as Daniel Wester and Appfire alongside Atlassian's Tim Pettersen (2022 review); a public Trello board for the Forge roadmap; Forge Quest; a "Get the most out of Forge" learning path (Oct 2025) (blog); and Atlas Camp keynotes and workshops (2026: Forge-hosted LLMs, Forge Container Service, Teamwork Graph connectors) (Atlas Camp 2026).
  • The RFC series is the most distinctive format. These are long, structured proposals that partners read closely: RFC-124 on the Marketplace Trust Program drew 102 posts and 5,412 views, and RFC-68 on native Jira "action items" drew 438 likes (RFC-124; RFC-68).
  • Member-made content: Codegeist entries (25+ Codegeist 2022 apps reached the Marketplace) (2022 review); partner lightning talks at App Week; the "Resolution & Jexo Forge Hackathon Findings" report (306 likes) and "I launched 20 Forge apps. Here's some feedback" (top.json); and independent data work, such as one partner scraping the Marketplace API in 2022 to show that only 116 Forge apps were paid and 94 of those had fewer than 100 installs (thread). Rewards for this work are prizes (Codegeist: $15K first place in 2025, plus TEAM tickets and "recognition at Atlas Camp") (Devpost), speaking slots and blog features. The forum itself does little to curate or reward it.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: 44 moderators and 34 admins, nearly all Atlassian employees, plus category moderators for Forge and RFCs. The site's contact is the developer-advocates alias (about.json). Staff product managers and engineers post announcements and answer in threads, and an Ecosystem Support Manager joins incident threads (thread).
  • Champions: CDAC has no developer MVP program of its own. The company-wide Atlassian Champions program (formerly Community Leaders) runs ACE chapters, and in 2025 Champions organised the first event with a dedicated Forge track, plus a Pune build day where 80 developers each built two apps in five hours (blog; blog).
  • Rules: RFC threads restate community guidelines: "comment on the idea not the people (especially the author); keep it tidy… keep comments constructive" (RFC-124). Long threads auto-close, which partners resent; Scott Dudley opened a new thread "because the earlier one was auto-closed" (thread).
  • Feedback paths: forum threads lead to public Jira tickets (FRGE, ECO, ROADMAP projects on ecosystem.atlassian.net) where partners vote. RFCs carry Resolve dates. Partners in the Partner Program also have Technical Partner Managers (TPMs). Partners say these paths often stall. One wrote that 0 of the 11 FRGE tickets they had flagged were resolved in 7 months (thread), and another that reaching the responsible PM "through our TPM… turned to be impossible" (RFC-68).

Growth loops

  • Platform → forum. Every developer who builds on Forge, Connect or Data Center ends up on CDAC through docs links, error searches and changelog links. Accounts grew from about 7.9k (2019) to 28.7k (2026). Sign-ups were 510 a month in 2019 and 294 a month in late 2026.
  • Hackathons as acquisition. Codegeist on Devpost draws thousands of new developers, some of whom list apps and become partners. Participation fell from 3,000+ (2022) to 1,300+ (2024), and prize pools from $300K to $170K to $120K (2025) (Devpost 2024; Devpost 2025).
  • Marketplace pitch. Atlassian's recruiting copy now sends new builders to "connect with the Atlassian Developer Community" next to the $7B figure (Atlassian, 2026).
  • SEO. Nine years of solved technical threads rank for Jira and Confluence API queries. I found no traffic data, so this is unquantified.
  • Negative loop. Public grievance threads reach Hacker News, as with "Atlassian's new terms forbid benchmarking" in 2018 (332 points, 195 comments) (HN), and they reach customers: in Oct 2026 a consultant reported that an enterprise hesitated to buy popular apps after seeing Connect end-of-support banners (thread).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard numbers, Marketplace level (not attributable to the forum): lifetime app sales of $1B (Dec 2019), $2B (Oct 2021), more than $3B (FY2023), $6B (late 2025) and $7B (Oct 2026), with the latest $1B in nine months. More than 40% of customers have at least one app, and there are 6,000+ Cloud apps, 3,700+ public Forge apps and 10,000+ custom Forge apps (June 2026) (Atlassian, 2026; tier update 2023).
  • Forge adoption is the metric the community most directly serves: from 116 paid Forge apps (Jul 2022, partner count) to 3,700+ public Forge apps (2026). The 2026 revenue-share penalty for Connect probably did more to drive this than the forum did.
  • Support deflection: 726 people have had a reply marked as a solution, and dozens hold expert levels (badges.json). No ticket-deflection numbers are published.
  • No evidence found of Atlassian tying CDAC to partner retention, partner NPS or revenue in filings or case studies. Treat the forum's business value as plausible but unmeasured in public.

What they did right

  1. Kept one durable, open platform. Discourse since 2017 with no forum migration, while the user community moved from Answers to Lithium/Khoros and then to a new platform in 2026. Nine years of threads are intact and searchable (about.json).
  2. Put real staff in the threads. The roster of staff moderators is unusually large, staff answer in public, and members thank them by name (appreciation thread). Even in hostile threads staff stay and sometimes give ground: rate-limit exemptions for front-end traffic, and an extra month on the Connect deadline (thread; extension).
  3. Formalised consultation through RFCs. Publish, Discuss and Resolve dates, written problem statements, and an opening admission that "Atlassian ships too many problems to our ecosystem" (RFC-1). Buchanan's own verdict was that the RFC program brought "some important course corrections" and that "the general awareness of big changes happens sooner" (post). RFC-133 (2026) explicitly follows up on feedback to RFC-124.
  4. Tied badges to useful behaviour. Accepted-solution levels per product and the Forge Quest completion badges reward answering and learning, not mere visiting.
  5. Combined online and offline. Codegeist, App Week (product teams building alongside partners for a week), Atlas Camp and ACE developer chapters give partners face time that the forum cannot. Atlas Camp's return in 2025 after the virtual years was welcomed (call for speakers).

What they did wrong

  1. Platform deadlines ahead of platform readiness. The 2026 revenue-share update raised the cut on Connect apps to 20% and then 25% (Forge: 16%, then 17%) while partners documented Forge gaps with no workaround. Partners called it "a 5–10% penalty for using a feature that has no alternative" (thread) and "one hell of a sales pitch" (same). The follow-up thread drew 495 likes (thread). Consequence: lasting distrust, talk of EU Digital Markets Act complaints, and a recurring "why participate" sentiment.
  2. Competing with partners after asking for their comments. RFC-68 (native Jira action items, Oct 2024) asked checklist vendors for comment on a feature that partners said would "kill many apps". One partner said the change could halve their business, and members cited RFC-32, RFC-37 and RFC-64 as a pattern (RFC-68). Consequence: the RFC program is seen by some as box-ticking ("Do you really want to get a feedback…?").
  3. Short-notice, poorly communicated changes. Rate limits with 48 days' notice over the holidays (thread). Connect end-of-support warning banners shown in production "by mistake" on 20 May 2026 before partners were told (thread). The Sep 2026 support-portal migration came with "one day notice via CDAC and negative one day notice via changelog", froze tickets for days, broke app-version publishing, and was then rolled back (thread).
  4. Neglected shared assets. In 2020 the AtlasKit design system went partly internal-only and stopped taking community contributions, which prompted the "no-bullshit update" demand (324 likes) (thread). In 2018 new terms forbade benchmarking and became a Hacker News story (HN).
  5. Strategy surprises that stranded investment. The Data Center end-of-life announcement (Sep 2025) came after partners had been told to invest in DC versions: "New plugin company with few new dc app ! All effort and money down the drain!" (thread).
  6. Thin community-management structure. Moderation leaned on one person's informal role ("unelected and unassigned"), and when he moved on in 2024 no named successor appeared in public (post). Recognition programs (New User of the Month, monthly recognition) appear to have lapsed. The informal partner Slack grew up because the forum is "regulated by Atlassian" (thread), and Atlassian never brought partners' backchannel into an official space.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Publish proposals before you decide (RFCs with dates). A written problem, a Discuss deadline and a Resolve date turn complaints into structured input and show members their opinion is wanted while it can still matter.
  2. Put the people who make decisions in the threads. Developers come back for answers from the engineer or PM who owns the API. Name them, let them answer in public, and let them concede points in public.
  3. Give each EAP its own space. A subcategory per early-access program makes members feel like insiders and gathers feedback where the builders are.
  4. Reward competence, not attendance. Badges for accepted solutions per product area and badges for finishing hands-on quests signal real expertise. Streak badges went almost unclaimed (21 and 4 holders).
  5. Mark the year with an anchor hackathon and an in-person camp. Codegeist and Atlas Camp give newcomers a way in and give veterans a reason to show up, and both feed back into the forum.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let business deadlines get ahead of platform readiness. When the community's purpose is migrating to your platform, penalising members for gaps you haven't closed turns the forum into a dispute log.
  2. Don't ask for comments on features that compete with your members' products unless you're willing to change the outcome. Consultation without visible changes erodes trust faster than not consulting at all.
  3. Don't treat the forum as the only notice channel, or give short notice. Changes that affect revenue need advance notice on every channel (changelog, status page, email). Otherwise the forum becomes the place where members find out they were surprised.

Score justifications

  • Retention mechanics 3: good expertise badges and quests, but return visits are driven mainly by economic necessity, with few rituals inside the forum itself.
  • Content engine 4: a broad and steady stream of docs, changelog, RFCs, webinars, livestreams, a hackathon and a conference. Member content is common but not systematically curated.
  • Member ownership 3: partners set the agenda of the biggest threads and run their own Slack, but moderation and structure are almost entirely staff.
  • Staff investment 4: about 40 staff moderators, advocates who post in public, and PMs who answer, though there is no visible dedicated community manager after 2024.
  • Business impact evidence 2: the Marketplace's $7B is well documented, but nothing public ties the forum to retention or revenue.
  • Durability 3: nine years on one platform and still the official channel, but new topics are down about two-thirds from the 2022 peak and active users have halved since Apr 2025.
