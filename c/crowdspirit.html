Peak

About 500 registrations in the one-day closed-beta window (Aug 2007), then 6,000 visitors, about 100 ideas and 600+ contributions by Nov 2007, with a 20-person core team on one product (Chanal & Caron-Fasan 2010; CrowdSpirit blog, 7 Nov 2007)

Now

Gone. crowdspirit.com showed a bare Apache test page by July 2011 (Wayback), Yannig Roth reported it offline in May 2011, and Crunchbase lists the company as closed.

How it pays off for the company

Planned: the community would replace R&D, market research, marketing and first-line support for cheap electronics sold under the CrowdSpirit brand, with points converted into a share of gross margin. Actual: no product shipped and no revenue from product sales is on record. After Oct 2008 the community became the supply side of a paid challenge service for companies (free public challenges, pay-per-use private ones).

TL;DR

  • What it is: a French startup (concept public in Nov 2006, company formed May 2007, official launch at TechCrunch40 in Sept 2007) that meant to crowdsource the whole life of cheap consumer electronics (under €150): members submit ideas, vote, write specs, invest, test, promote and support, and get paid points that turn into a share of margin. It ran a Digg-style voting site (IdeaVox), a phpBB forum per idea (IdeaEngine), a Facebook network and a founder blog (TechCrunch 2007; Chanal & Caron-Fasan 2010).
  • Why people came back (briefly): the hope of seeing their idea become a real gadget, a vote tally, points toward a future payout, and the chance to join a closed "core team". It worked once: the community chose a Digital Wall Calendar with 70% positive votes and a 20-person core team finished its spec by December 2007.
  • What the company fed it: one founder (Lionel David) posting news and daily gadget inspiration, 14 how-to videos, video teasers, a mind-map tool and heavy press (TechCrunch, BusinessWeek, FT, Le Monde, Les Echos). There was no community manager, events or paid recognition.
  • Biggest mistake: the business model, not the community, failed first. When it came time to manufacture the calendar, David worked out that even 10,000 units at €10 net margin meant only about €100K, so he postponed it, closed the platform in February 2008 and relaunched in October 2008 as a French-first challenge marketplace for companies. The members who had been promised a product and a revenue share got neither, and the "spirit" never came back (andersdenken, Oct 2008; comment by "Jeff", 2014).
  • The one lesson: a co-creation community runs on the promise that its work will ship. Prove the unit economics before you ask the crowd to choose, then finish the first loop (idea → product in hand) even if it is small. One shipped product would have done more for retention than every gamification feature CrowdSpirit added.

What the community is

CrowdSpirit was not a community around an existing product. The community was the product-development department of a company that had no products yet. The 2007 site described four steps: "The community sends ideas, fine tunes them & votes for the best one"; specs are defined with partners while "Community Investors start financing the product development"; the community tests the prototype; customers buy through "the CrowdSpirit Supply chain" while "the community ensures the product support and recommends products to retailers" (How it works, June 2007, Wayback). Target products were MP4 players, DVD players, webcams, joysticks and headphones under €150 / $190 (P2P Foundation wiki, citing Springwise; TechCrunch 2007).

Origin and strategy

Founder and team. Lionel David, a supply-chain program manager with HP's PC after-sales service background (Viadeo profile; Yannig Roth, 2012), led a four-person volunteer core team in mid-2007: Lionel (project lead and supply chain, Grenoble), Raphaël (technology, Paris), Craig (information lead, Edinburgh) and Sébastien (marketing, Grenoble) (About page, June 2007). The site went live on 27–28 November 2006 and was billed as "an international venture based in Scotland (Edinburgh) and France (Paris and Grenoble)" with Grenoble École de Management (Silicon Glen, Nov 2006). It joined the GRAIN incubator in July 2007 (blog archive index, Wayback).

Why build a community. David's pitch was to remove the marketing department: "our aim is to bypass the marketing department and go directly to the source of the need. I mean YOU" (openbusiness.cc interview, Jan 2007, quoted by andersdenken). He argued the community would also replace investors, ambassadors and support staff, so CrowdSpirit would keep about twice the margin of a traditional firm (same source).

The business-model link. Two Grenoble researchers, Valérie Chanal and Marie-Laurence Caron-Fasan, ran eight strategy workshops with David from May 2007 to June 2008 and published the most detailed record of the company (M@n@gement, 2010). Their account:

  • The original concept: "Selling innovative consumer electronics products, under the CrowdSpirit brand, which perfectly fit end-user needs due to their involvement throughout the innovation process."
  • Contributors earned points per action, convertible to cash "only if and when the product is commercialized", out of a share of gross margin. Members had to transfer all IP to CrowdSpirit.
  • The researchers flagged early on that the startup "lacked both the marketing competencies and identified distribution channels for the end-customer", and that a long-tail model doesn't work for tangible goods with "high fixed costs and relatively low margins".

Pivot. From Nov 2007 they designed an "IP-oriented" intermediary model. The V1 platform closed in February 2008, and V2 launched on 6 October 2008: "CrowdSpirit n'est plus un industriel mais est devenu un intermédiaire de service" (CrowdSpirit is no longer a manufacturer but a service intermediary) (blog, 6 Oct 2008, Wayback). Public challenges were free for companies; private challenges were charged per use and were to be "the platform's main source of revenue" (Chanal & Caron-Fasan 2010).

Trajectory: a burst of press-driven sign-ups (500 in a day) that never grew into a crowd. Within six weeks of TechCrunch40 the forum had 53 members and the highest-voted idea had 31 votes. After the pivot, activity was a few dozen named, mostly French participants per challenge. The trajectory was a press spike, a plateau, then a slow fade, with no organic growth phase.

What keeps people coming back

CrowdSpirit had all the theoretical retention hooks of a co-creation platform. Almost none of them paid off, because the main one (seeing a product ship and earning from it) never arrived.

  • The core promise: your idea becomes a real product. This was the real draw. In November 2007 the team wrote: "So far, it's a green light all the way for the digital wall calendar… very confident that we're on the right track to make this digital wall calendar a reality in the near future" (blog). The calendar was put on hold a month later. A later contributor wrote that the company "should have taken the risk to launch the wall calendar first and go through the whole process once, this would have shown a success and allow for more involvement of the people" ("Jeff", 11 Aug 2014).
  • Deferred money: points that turn into revenue share. Points were earned per action and paid out only if a product sold. The V2 terms went further: rewards were split by "un algorithme confidentiel" (a confidential algorithm) that CrowdSpirit could change "à tout moment… avec ou sans préavis" (at any time, with or without notice), with "aucune garantie" (no guarantee) of payment (V2 terms of use, Oct 2008, Wayback). The researchers noted that "the hope of having one's own idea selected is not sufficient to maintain a high level of contribution as we saw in the first phase" (2010).
  • Status. V2 added a "TOP CONTRIBUTOR" goal and tasks "afin de gagner un MAX de points" (to earn a MAX of points) (31 Jul 2008). It also had "Best innovators of the week / month / year" panels (the top people had 50 points) and a "popularity" score from task ratings (users page, Dec 2008; blog, 24 Oct 2008).
  • Selection into a closed core team. Only members with enough points could join the product core team, which worked "in a confidential environment" with the chosen manufacturer (2010 paper; blog, Nov 2007). This motivated about 20 people but moved them out of public view.
  • Ownership roles. The person who submitted an idea became moderator of that idea's forum by default (IdeaEngine post, 25 Sep 2007). Named credit: the blog thanked 21 calendar contributors by handle.
  • Cash prizes (V2). The bike challenge paid €1,500 / €1,000 / €500 / €500. This is a one-off incentive, not a ritual (results post, 7 Jan 2009).
  • Missing: recurring rituals (no weekly show, meetups or seasons), staff hosts other than the founder, peer accountability, and any switching cost. No evidence was found of email digests or notifications beyond RSS.

The content engine

  • Founder blog (Nov 2006 to Mar 2009). At first it ran press round-ups ("Please vote for us on Digg and Reddit", 28 Nov 2006) and launch news. From late Oct 2007 it posted near-daily gadget-inspiration posts (USB drink chiller, OLED watch, Bluetooth snowboard helmet) to seed ideas. In 2008 David switched it to French "Réflexion" essays on crowdsourcing, trust and open source (blog archive, Wayback; Direction, 21 Oct 2008). The 2008 blog comments were mostly spam.
  • How-to content. There were 14 numbered Dailymotion tutorials (sign in, post an idea, vote, "AddRiskOnOpportunity", bug report and so on) (home page, June 2008) and an "Ideavox Tutorials" forum (2 topics). That the platform needed 14 videos says something about how complicated it was.
  • Tools as content. A mind-map feature (26 Sep 2007), Animoto video teasers for ideas (28 Sep 2007) and marketplace "fan" pages to signal intent to buy (blog index).
  • Press as content. TechCrunch (2006, 2007), BusinessWeek (Jan 2007), the Financial Times, Le Monde (Sep 2007), Les Echos (Oct 2007), France Inter (2008), and a five-part openbusiness.cc series (Jan 2007). The press built the launch spike but not lasting participation.
  • Member-made content. Idea write-ups, mock-ups (Will McLeod's calendar mock-up was featured), specs and V2 challenge write-ups. There was no system to curate or reward this beyond points.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: about four people in 2007, at least some part-time (About, 2007). In practice David was moderator, blogger and recruiter. The forum's "last post by ldavid" appears on most idea boards (forum, Oct 2007).
  • Planned member roles: the researchers mapped eight: idea initiator, design team member, investor, tester, ambassador, project leader, reseller and customer (2010). Only initiator, voter and core-team member were ever exercised.
  • IP and rules: "contributors give up all intellectual property rights when they submit an idea or product, or when they help define a product" (P2P Foundation / Springwise). That alarmed observers from day one; Trendwatching warned that "if you don't pay a fair share, talented members of the global brain will take their business elsewhere."
  • Feedback loop into the product: a strong point. There was an "Improvement of CrowdSpirit" forum (11 topics, 48 posts by Oct 2007), a standing "Bug / amélioration de la plateforme" idea in V2, and a public challenge asking members to redesign the V2 sign-up ("Participez à la nouvelle…"), where David responded in the comments.

Growth loops

  • Press and launch events were the only real loop: TechCrunch40 and the Internet Retailer 2007 award brought sign-ups but no compounding growth ("the number of visits… had not increased since the beta test", 2010).
  • Borrowed audiences (V2): posting in outside forums such as the French e-bike forum Velectris, and a one-off mailing to the 4,000 researchers of the CEA (France's atomic and alternative energy commission) for the bike challenge (blog, 31 Oct 2008). The Velectris outreach met suspicion. David wrote that forum members "ne me connaissent pas et m'accorde naturellement peu de crédit" (don't know me and naturally give me little credit), making it "quasiment impossible d'éviter les spéculations quant à mes intentions" (almost impossible to avoid speculation about my intentions) (blog, 24 Oct 2008).
  • Social: a Facebook network and a Coroflot designer group (Oct 2007). No evidence of their size.
  • The loop that never ran: the planned ambassadors and resellers needed a product to sell.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: no shipped product, no published revenue, no funding round found (Crunchbase lists none; status closed). David's own back-of-envelope figure: the calendar "would have may be generated not more than 100K€ of revenues… it was clearly a wrong business model" (andersdenken, 17 Oct 2008).
  • Company claims: V2 had "nos premiers clients avant même le lancement officiel" (our first clients even before the official launch) (6 Oct 2008). The researchers report pilots "for two important corporate customers" with private and public phases (2010). Visible clients: CEA's LID lab (bike challenge), AbsolutelyNew (a US product company, private challenge posted Dec 2008 and still on the home page in Jul 2010), Milpix (image-search technology challenge), I-Nova (2010). No contract values are known.
  • Academic legacy: CrowdSpirit became a standard failure case in crowdsourcing research (Chanal & Caron-Fasan 2008, 2010; Pélissier 2008). Later work lists it with Myoo Create as rare documented failures (ResearchGate listing).

Score notes: retention_mechanics 2 (points, leaderboards and core-team selection existed but rested on a payout that never came); content_engine 2 (an active founder blog and tutorials, but no rituals or member-content program); member_ownership 2 (the crowd really did pick the product, but it signed away its IP and had no say in the shelving); staff_investment 2 (one founder doing everything, no community manager); business_impact_evidence 1 (no revenue on record); durability 1 (about 15 months of real community life).

What they did right

  1. The crowd's selection worked. About 100 ideas went to one clear winner (the Digital Wall Calendar, 70% positive votes). A self-selected core team of 20 produced industrialization-ready specs within about three months of launch (2010). Gizmodo covered the concept (Nov 2007), and the product category later became a real market (Skylight and others). The co-creation process itself was sound; David said so: "the concept of asking a community to 'crowdsource' product was fine and did work fine."
  2. Tiered participation. An open crowd voted and commented while a points-qualified core team did the deep work. The researchers called this a new "collective-private" incentive model and recommended it (2010).
  3. Public credit. Contributors were thanked by handle, the initiator moderated their own idea, and mock-ups were featured on the blog.
  4. Openness about the pivot and the platform. There were public improvement forums, a challenge on the redesign itself, an apology after the bike contest ("nous excusons des désagréments", we apologize for the inconvenience), and an unusually candid founder interview admitting "our business model was totally wrong."
  5. A smart IP fix for V2: open challenges for non-technical ideas and private spaces for confidential technical work, worked out after lawyers disagreed on whether ideas published online could be patented (2010).

What they did wrong

  1. They asked the crowd to choose before checking whether anything it chose could make money. The unit-economics check came after the community had picked and specified the product, and it killed the product. Consequence: members' core reward (a real gadget plus revenue share) disappeared at the moment of success, and the platform closed in Feb 2008.
  2. They never closed one loop. Not shipping even a small run of the calendar meant there was never a success story to recruit with. "When the company decided to turn to a new model it was already too late. The 'spirit' was gone" ("Jeff", 2014).
  3. The incentives were deferred, uncertain and extractive. Members gave up all IP, and points paid out only on future sales by an algorithm that could change "avec ou sans préavis". Observers flagged the fairness problem in 2006, and the researchers listed motivation as the central unsolved issue.
  4. The platform was too complex. There were two separate registrations for IdeaVox and IdeaEngine ("We do apologize for the inconvenience due to the double registration"), 14 tutorial videos, and tools that the researchers judged "too complicated and in others not sophisticated enough to support real collaborative work." The V2 bike contest's voting phase "n'ayant pas fonctionné" (did not work).
  5. They switched who the community served. V1 was English-language, global and for geeks building their own gadgets. V2 was French-first and served corporate clients' challenges, often private. The existing members' reason to belong (building products they wanted) was gone, and outsiders (Velectris) suspected their motives.
  6. The team was thin and relied on press. One founder carried moderation, content and sales. Traffic depended on press spikes and "had not increased since the beta test." A former team member blamed leadership and a France-only focus. David disputed this, saying the site was English-only and global from the start (see Member voice). Both agree the team was small.
  7. Neglect at the end. The home page showed the same private challenge for about 10 months (2009–2010), and the domain went to a default server page by mid-2011 without any public shutdown note found.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Tier participation. Keep voting and commenting open to everyone and give a small, earned core team the deep work. The structure produced a finished spec fast.
  2. Give the idea's author a role. Making the submitter moderator of their idea's space and crediting contributors by name builds ownership cheaply.
  3. Let the community vote on the platform itself. Improvement forums and a challenge on the redesign surfaced friction (double sign-up, voting bugs) quickly.
  4. Separate public and private spaces by sensitivity, not by member tier. Open spaces for ideas and use cases, private rooms for confidential technical work. This solved a real IP problem.
  5. Be candid when things change. The founder's open "the business model was wrong" interview earned goodwill from observers. The lesson is to say it to members first, before the pivot ships.

Avoid these

  1. Don't ask the community to make a choice you can't afford to carry out. Run the margin and channel math (and ideally a preorder test) before voting starts, and say up front what happens if a winner isn't viable.
  2. Don't make rewards depend on future revenue at your discretion while taking all the IP. Members need something they can see now (credit, early units, guaranteed prizes, a transparent formula).
  3. Don't change who the community serves overnight. Moving from "build gadgets you want" to "solve companies' challenges" without the members' consent cost CrowdSpirit its members and its credibility with new audiences.
