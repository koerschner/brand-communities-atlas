How it pays off for the company

Three hoped-for revenue lines: (1) a talent funnel of pre-workshopped genre writers for Penguin imprints and other publishers (8 members sold books to traditional publishers by Jul 2013); (2) fees and royalty cuts from self-publishing packages ($99-$549 in 2011; free-$399 plus a 15% cut from 2013); (3) after Pearson bought Author Solutions in 2012, a branded front door and customer pool for ASI's self-publishing services. No revenue figures were ever published.

TL;DR

  • What it is: Penguin Group (USA) opened Book Country, a free online workshop for genre-fiction writers, in public beta in late April 2011. Members posted manuscripts, critiqued each other, talked in forums, and from November 2011 could pay to self-publish through the site. It reached 8,300 active members by July 2013 and "over 20,000 writers" by January 2016. In September 2016 Penguin Random House ended peer review and publishing packages. By March 2017 the forums and new registration were gone too (PW, Apr 2011; PW, Jul 2013; Silag, Jan 2016; IPM, Oct 2016; Wayback, Mar 2017).
  • Why people came back: to get critiques on the next draft of a work in progress. To get reviews you had to give them (three critiques before your first upload at launch, later one-for-one). Members found their tribe through a "genre map", built up reviewer reputation, and kept revising: on average a writer posted six drafts, and one posted 58 (Publishing Perspectives, Jul 2013).
  • What the company fed it: a named community manager (former literary agent Colleen Lindsay), an editorial coordinator, later a director. Around them ran a craft-and-business blog, a biweekly #bookcountry Twitter chat with authors and industry professionals, YouTube tutorials, conference booths, a matched "Buddy Program", and publicized book deals for members.
  • Biggest mistake: tying a trust-based peer community to a monetization layer that writers distrusted. First came 2011 packages that self-publishing leaders called a "rip-off". Then in 2012 the site was moved under Author Solutions, the industry's most criticized vanity publisher. When ASI was sold in December 2015 and the last director left in January 2016, Penguin Random House had no reason left to fund the community. It shut the part members valued most, peer review, first.
  • The one lesson: a give-to-get critique loop with a clear identity (genre) and staff who show up can build a healthy, kind community on a small budget. But if the sponsor's business case is a side product (funnel or upsell) and not the community itself, the community dies whenever that side product's owner changes.

What the community is

Book Country was a website (bookcountry.com, built on ASP.NET with Ektron discussion boards) where aspiring and published writers uploaded manuscripts for peer critique. Penguin positioned it as "a free online writing community and publishing services venture" for genre fiction: "romance, fantasy, science fiction and thrillers to a range of contemporary hybrid genres like paranormal romance, urban fantasy and Steampunk" (PW, Apr 2011). It was "organized as a separate company" (Book Country LLC, "an affiliate of Penguin Group (USA) Inc." per its terms of use), but critics noted that Penguin funded it, Penguin's logo was "all over the site", and demos were held in Penguin's offices (Gaughran, Nov 2011).

The spaces:

  • Read & Review. Manuscript pages with chapter uploads, author's notes and structured peer reviews. Copy-paste, download and email-forwarding were disabled to reduce plagiarism fears (PW, Apr 2011).
  • Genre Map. An interactive "map" of genres and subgenres, each anchored by "landmark titles" (for example Walter Mosley's Devil in a Blue Dress for hard-boiled noir), used to match readers and writers. Book covers on the site were colour-coded by genre and tone (Wayback homepage, Aug 2011).
  • Discussion boards. Craft boards (character, dialogue, POV, sex scenes, fight scenes), one board per genre, business boards (query workshop, finding an agent, marketing, self-publishing), event boards (NaNoWriMo, Summer Writers Club 2014, conferences) and a later board for "Authonomy Members on Book Country" (Wayback forum index, Oct 2016).
  • Blog, Twitter, YouTube. blog.bookcountry.com (2011–2016), @BookCountry (about 4,000 followers, "joined August 2010", last post October 2016 per X) and the BookCountryTV channel of how-to videos.
  • Publish and bookstore. Paid and later free ebook packages with distribution to Amazon, Apple, B&N, Kobo, Google and others, plus an on-site ebookstore from July 2013.
  • IRL. Staff ran booths and sessions at genre and regional conferences: romance events in New Orleans and Atlanta, Bouchercon 2014, Pacific Northwest Writers Association 2015, and meetups in Boston, San Francisco, Seattle and New York (Silag, Jan 2016).

Origin and strategy

The founder was Molly Barton, a former editor at Oxford University Press and Viking who had moved into Penguin business development. She pitched the idea to the Pearson Innovation Fund, a seed fund run by Penguin's parent company, more than a year before launch (PW, Apr 2011). Her own account of the motive is about access: her husband's New York writers' group was so exclusive that "someone would have to die or leave New York" to open a seat. She wanted writers "at the end of a long dirt road" to get the same early readers and to start "build[ing] the natural audience for their work while they were still in the writing process" (Barton, HuffPost, Jun 2013). She picked genre writers first because "writers of categories would be more open to collaborating with one another on the Internet… they really want to satisfy and entertain their readers, so I think they're more open to feedback" (Publishing Perspectives, Jul 2013).

The business model ran through two channels from the start:

  1. Talent discovery. Barton said she "expects to find new authors for Penguin" while keeping the site "publisher agnostic", with agents and editors from any house welcome. Contact requests went through staff (PW, Apr 2011). The FAQ also said the site "is not a channel for the submission of unsolicited manuscripts to Penguin editors", which Jim C. Hines called a mixed message because discoverability was still sold as a perk (Hines, May 2011).
  2. Self-publishing fees. Penguin "expects to generate revenue from both the fee services program as well as from signing Book Country authors to book deals, and hopes the deal component will provide the bulk of sales" (PW, Nov 2011). CEO David Shanks framed it as protecting "Penguin's leadership position in digital publishing" (PR Newswire, Nov 2011).

After Pearson bought Author Solutions (ASI) for $116 million in July 2012 (LA Times), ASI's technology team rebuilt the site (PW, Jan 2013). ASI's marketing chief said Book Country was "a great site for curating content and community involvement" that Booktango might "bring in" (MediaShift, Aug 2012). Industry watchers concluded that ASI had become "the publishing engine for Penguin's Book Country community" (IPM, Oct 2016). The site's contact address moved to ASI's headquarters at 1663 Liberty Drive, Bloomington, Indiana (Contact page).

Champions: Barton (founder and president, until she left Penguin in early 2014 per IPM, 2014); Colleen Lindsay (community manager, 2011–2013); Danielle Poiesz (editorial coordinator); Brandi Larsen (director from January 2013, a former Book Country fan from the Chicago Tribune, per Ohio Center for the Book); and Lucy Silag (forum account from June 2013, director until 29 January 2016).

Trajectory. Growth was steady but small: roughly 4,000 to 8,300 active members over 20 months. The "20,000" figure in 2016 is cumulative and probably counts lapsed accounts. The forum totals suggest a modest conversation layer: about 20,000 posts in all, roughly 10 a day averaged over the site's life (an estimate, since the count may not include deleted or migrated boards). By October 2016, a month after the peer-review shutdown notice, most boards had not had a post since spring. That fits a community that had already lost its staff host in January 2016.

What keeps people coming back

  1. The give-to-get gate. At launch "all Book Country members must first comment and review at least three works by different writers before they can start uploading their own works" (PW, Apr 2011). Later this became a "one-for-one contribution to post your book for review" (Help page). Every new chapter or draft created a fresh obligation to review someone else, so members came back on a revision cadence. The average of six drafts per writer and the one writer with 58 public revisions (Publishing Perspectives) show the loop worked for its most committed users.
  2. Reputation for reviewers, not just writers. The review system was "designed to not only highlight the best writers but also to track and rate the most effective reviewers, those whose feedback has been deemed by the community to be the most useful". Members also earned "merit badges for a variety of contributions" (PW, Apr 2011). This is the key design difference from Authonomy, where status came from votes for your own book.
  3. Biweekly showcases. "Book Country Favorites" (highest-rated by reviews) and "Buzz Books" (most recommended to connections and shared off-site) were refreshed per genre "every two weeks" (Wayback, Aug 2011). That gave members a regular ritual without a single winner-take-all prize.
  4. Genre identity. The genre map, colour-coded covers and per-genre boards let a cozy-mystery or steampunk writer find "their kind of work… as on a shelf" (Larsen in Publishing Perspectives). Opening up to 60+ categories in July 2013 (literary, YA, nonfiction, new adult) added members but blurred that identity.
  5. Structured commitment programs. The Book Country Buddy Program (2015) matched writers in the same genre for a 12-week schedule of three reviews and two revisions, with staff-sent deadlines (Buddy Program post). NaNoWriMo boards (314 posts), a "Summer Writers Club 2014" and "feedback challenges" gave seasonal pushes (Silag, Jan 2016).
  6. Success stories as proof. Kerry Schafer was "the first author to be plucked off Book Country", with a two-book deal from Penguin's Ace imprint, and she "returned to the community to work on new material there" (on-a-limb, Mar 2012; Publishing Perspectives). Andrea Dunlop's The Sojourn sold to Simon & Schuster's Atria in October 2014 and was announced by press release (PRWeb, Oct 2014). A "Member News & Announcements" board (111 topics) celebrated smaller wins.
  7. Private messaging and connections. Private messages were "what members asked for most frequently" and arrived in July 2013 (Publishing Perspectives). Following writers and "Connections" were in from day one.
  8. Staff as hosts. Lindsay personally answered criticism in blog comment threads (Hines, May 2011). The director's account had 1,356 forum posts by August 2015 (Authonomy welcome thread).

What was missing: no evidence of streaks, levels, member moderators or an ambassador program. The only switching cost was a member's posted drafts and review history, which disappeared when peer review was switched off.

The content engine

  • Blog (2011–2016). Craft posts ("3 Golden Rules of Writing Romantic Suspense", "5 Mistakes Every Writer Should Avoid"), business posts ("10 Tips for a Great Author Facebook Fan Page"), conference takeaways, member spotlights and guest posts from agents, editors and marketers. The Wayback CDX index shows monthly archives from March 2011 to January 2016 and nothing after the director left (CDX listing). Silag described it as "the latest news and trends about the craft of writing and business of publishing… from in-the-know authors, agents, editors, book marketers" (Authonomy welcome thread).
  • #bookcountry Twitter chat. Held "every other Thursday at 9 PM Eastern", usually with "one big name writer and one publishing professional discussing one writer-focused topic" (Publishing Talk); one example was a December 2011 chat on subsidiary rights (Kate Hart, Dec 2011).
  • Education built into the product. Each genre page had an explainer and a "virtual shelf" of landmark titles ("What are the titles I need to read, as an author, in order to be well-versed in my own genre?"), plus video tutorials such as How to Write and Post Peer Book Reviews (June 2013).
  • Published authors as content. Established authors posted on the boards; for example, Elizabeth Moon's thread "Weak Characters, Strong Characters" (2013) was still being cited in 2019 (learnwritingcraft, 2019).
  • Member-made content. The manuscripts and critiques were the main UGC. Staff curated it through Favorites and Buzz Books, featured manuscripts, member spotlights and a Kickstarter page of members' projects (Kickstarter).

People, programs and governance

  • Team. Small and from publishing: a president who also ran Penguin's global digital business, a community manager and moderator (Lindsay), an editorial coordinator (Poiesz, "responsible for all editorial aspects of the Book Country site", Reedsy), then a director (Larsen 2013–14, Silag to January 2016). No team-size figure was published. Technical work moved to ASI from 2012.
  • Rules. Community guidelines set age minimums. Staff posted etiquette threads such as "Don't harass Book Country members for reciprocal ratings!" and "Thank Your Fellow Book Country Members!", which shows that review-swapping pressure existed even without a vote-for-prize system.
  • Feedback into the product. The company "built a database of suggestions and bugs" from members (PW, Jan 2013). The 2013 relaunch shipped the most-requested items: more genres, private messages and a cover tool. Members also noticed when a "site ideas and improvements" thread "has been closed to new replies apparently" (Suggestions thread).
  • No superuser or ambassador program found. The Buddy Program was staff-matched, not member-led.

Growth loops

  • Absorbing dying communities. Book Country welcomed AuthorStand's writers in March 2014 (blog, Mar 2014) and Authonomy's refugees in August 2015 with a dedicated board (welcome thread).
  • Conference circuit and press. Booths and sessions at genre conventions, steady coverage in PW and Publishing Perspectives, and press releases for every member deal.
  • Twitter and the publishing blogosphere. Lindsay was a "popular Twitter personality"; writer-bloggers posted favourable write-ups (Wise Ink, Jul 2013).
  • Off-site sharing built in. "Buzz Books" ranked manuscripts by how often members shared them outside Book Country.
  • SEO. Forum threads still ranked in search results years after closing, but no evidence was found that this was a deliberate strategy.

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence is thin. The only outcome numbers are writer outcomes: 2 book deals and about 20 agented writers by January 2013, 8 traditional sales by July 2013, plus named deals for Schafer (Ace, Penguin's own imprint) and Dunlop (Atria, a competitor). Penguin never disclosed revenue from packages, ebookstore sales or ASI upsells. The free tier was the most popular package (Publishing Perspectives), which suggests little fee revenue. The self-publishing side was taken offline from June 2012 to January 2013 to fix "technology issues", and print-on-demand was dropped "because of lack of demand" (PW, Jan 2013). No SEC filings apply (Pearson and Bertelsmann are not US filers), and no academic studies were found. The decisive evidence is the shutdown itself: within a year of PRH selling ASI, the company ended peer review and packages and stopped staffing the site. That suggests it never became self-justifying.

What they did right

  1. Give-to-get before you post. The three-review (later one-for-one) gate seeded reciprocity and produced an average of about six reviews per manuscript (PW, Jul 2013).
  2. Rewarding good reviewers. Rating reviewers on usefulness pointed status at helpfulness rather than popularity. Members called it "a caring, comprehensive community" with "a good attitude" (quotes below), unlike the swap-backing culture at Authonomy.
  3. A clear initial niche. Genre fiction only, organized by a playful genre map, matched Barton's point that genre writers are feedback-hungry and collaborative.
  4. Credible, visible staff. Hiring a well-known former agent as community manager earned early trust; even critic Jim C. Hines said it "adds to their credibility" (Hines).
  5. Programs that create a cadence. Biweekly showcases, biweekly Twitter chats, NaNoWriMo, summer clubs and the 12-week Buddy Program all gave members dated reasons to return.
  6. Listening and shipping. The 2013 relaunch delivered what members had asked for: more categories, private messages, a cover tool and lower fees (PW, Jan 2013).

What they did wrong

  1. Monetizing trust before earning it. Seven months after launch, packages costing $99–$549 plus a 30% cut of net retailer revenue set off "an explosion of negative commentary" (Writer Beware, Nov 2011). J.A. Konrath wrote that his blog "gets more traffic than Book Country does" and that he was posting to warn newcomers off it (Konrath, Nov 2011). Consequence: the brand became linked with "vanity" publishing in self-publishing circles, the audience it most needed.
  2. Becoming the front door for Author Solutions. After 2012, packages included ASI's "Author Learning Center" and "cherry-picked" ASI services (IPM, Jan 2013; PW, Jul 2013) just as ASI faced a class-action suit. Publishing Perspectives noted "some authors" claimed Book Country was "yet another Author Solutions-powered site" (Publishing Perspectives). Consequence: the community's fate became tied to ASI's ownership.
  3. Buggy technology and long outages. The publishing side was dark for seven months in 2012. A 2014 user wrote that the site was "so poorly designed that the frustration is outweighing the benefit" (SmartCustomer, Jan 2014).
  4. Leadership churn. The founder left in early 2014, director Larsen moved to another Penguin Random House role in 2014 (Larsen profile; RocketReach), and director Silag left on 29 January 2016. Her farewell post cancelled a planned conference appearance and sent members to a customer-support email address (Silag, Jan 2016). There is no evidence of a successor. Consequence: forum activity faded through 2016.
  5. Shutting the core and keeping the store. In September 2016 members were told peer review and packages would end while "the bookstore and community forums" continued (IPM, Oct 2016). By March 2017 the forums and registration were also gone. No public announcement or data-export plan was found, and the dead homepage stayed up for nine years. Consequence: members' review histories were lost and a remnant regrouped elsewhere (Writers Co-op).
  6. Diluting the niche. Opening to 60+ categories in 2013 boosted member numbers, but by 2016 many genre boards had only a handful of topics (Western 1, War/Military 1, Women's Fiction 5).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Gate posting behind giving. Require a contribution (a review, an answer) before members can ask for one. This seeds reciprocity from a member's first day and sets a cadence: every new draft or question triggers a new obligation.
  2. Give status for being helpful, not for being popular. Rate reviewers and helpers on usefulness as judged by the people they helped. This keeps the culture kind and resists vote-swapping.
  3. Start with a sharp identity and a map of it. A visual taxonomy (the genre map) helps newcomers find their sub-tribe at once and makes the space feel like theirs.
  4. Run time-boxed commitment programs. Matched 12-week partnerships, seasonal challenges and biweekly showcases turn a free-form forum into a calendar.
  5. Publicize member wins loudly, including wins elsewhere. A "publisher-agnostic" stance (celebrating a deal at a rival house) made success stories believable and kept aspiration alive without promising a pipeline.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let the community be a side product of a funnel or upsell. If the budget depends on talent scouting or a services business, the community dies when that strategy or its owner changes. Fund it on its own metrics, or don't launch.
  2. Don't attach a distrusted partner or a hard upsell to a trust-based space. Keep monetization clearly separate and fairly priced, or the community inherits the partner's reputation.
  3. Don't let the one staff host leave without a successor or a shutdown plan. Here a single departure led to an eight-month fade, a quiet shutdown and no data export. Plan for handover, member-led moderation or an archive.

Related communities worth adding

  • Writers Co-op — former Book Country members — group blog and forum founded in 2016 by people who met on Book Country; a small successor community that is still active.
  • AuthorStand — independent — writing community whose writers moved to Book Country in March 2014; an example of a failed peer-critique site.
  • Booktango — Author Solutions / Penguin — DIY ebook platform whose design Book Country adopted in 2013; part of a publisher self-publishing push with almost no community layer.
  • iPublish — Time Warner Books — 2000–2001 online writing community and digital imprint that closed with losses; Barton consulted a former iPublish editor.
  • Scribophile — independent — karma-based peer-critique site that outlived the publisher-run ones.
  • Critique Circle — independent — long-running credit-for-critique workshop (same give-to-get model).
  • Online Writers Workshop (OWW) — independent — paid sci-fi/fantasy critique community named by members as an alternative.
  • YouWriteOn — Arts Council England–backed peer-review site that faded around 2015–2016.
  • Swoon Reads — Macmillan — crowd-voted YA romance imprint and community (2014–2020s); a later publisher-funded attempt.
