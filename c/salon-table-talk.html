TL;DR

  • What it was: Table Talk was the threaded discussion forum Salon launched with the magazine on 20 November 1995. Its design copied The WELL. It ran on WebCrossing software, grew to about 104,000 registered users by April 1999, and Forum One ranked it the second most popular discussion area on the Web. Salon closed it on 10 June 2011 with one month's notice and deleted every message [1][2][4][5].
  • Why people came back: each other. Members ignored the plan to discuss Salon articles and built friendships, support groups and running jokes in folders such as Private Life, Mothers Who Think (later Families Who Think), White House, Television and Imagination. They became groups with their own names (Buffistas, the Disco Ball crowd, the Tarts). Members met in person, married, and talked one another through suicide crises [3][4][6].
  • What Salon fed it: one devoted host, Mary Elizabeth Williams, who ran it from 1995 to about 2009. Salon writers and authors came in as guests, and staff featured discussions on the front page each week. Late on, a "Best of Table Talk" column republished member posts on Salon.com. Editorial staff otherwise stayed away. The community was "flying under your radar since 1995" [3][4][8][14].
  • Biggest mistakes: Salon never connected the forum to its journalism or its revenue. In 2001 it put posting behind a $10-a-month fee, and whole groups left to start their own sites. In 2011 it shut the forum and deleted 16 years of member writing with 30 days' notice and no export or archive [4][5][6][7].
  • The lesson: treat community as a product line with its own business model, not an add-on to content. If you must close it, give members their data and time to move.

What the community is

Table Talk (TT, at tabletalk.salon.com) was a free-standing forum split into "topics" or "folders" (Books, Politics, White House, Private Life, Television, Mothers Who Think and others), each holding threaded "discussions." Full threads were moved to an "attic" [2]. It ran from Salon's launch in November 1995 to 10 June 2011. That night its URL began redirecting to Mary Elizabeth Williams' farewell essay [2][3].

Salon owned and staffed it. The first and longtime host was Mary Elizabeth Williams (the "sysop," nicknamed "Big Nurse" and the "Bossy Stick"). From about 1999 to 2012, Gail Ann Williams was Salon's Director of Communities, over both TT and The WELL [2][3][9]. Salon bought The WELL in April 1999 and sold it to its members in September 2012. For a while The WELL was TT's paid sibling: in 2001, a WELL membership was what bought TT posting rights [7][9][15].

Unofficial spaces: TT spun off several independent communities. The best documented is Buffistas.org, a group of Buffy the Vampire Slayer fans who left when TT went paid in August 2001. They stayed for a while at WorldCrossing.com and moved to their own site on 17 September 2002 [10]. A Buffistas thread in 2004 led to the creation of TV Tropes [16]. Other refugees went to The American Prospect's forums in 2001 and to The Atlantic's WebCrossing forums, then on to WorldCrossing, and finally to self-hosted domains [6][7]. A Flickr group, "The Table Talk Pool," existed for TT posters [17]. Status today: defunct. Salon Media Group stopped publishing altogether on 5 November 2026 [18].

Origin and strategy

  • Community was part of the launch. Gail Williams wrote that "the grand opening had to be delayed slightly while the forums were readied. It was critical that there be a way to make the new web-based publication a living gathering place, in the way that traditional media had never been" [2].
  • The plan: talk about stories. Scott Rosenberg, who managed the build, explains that every Salon article was supposed to link to a TT thread. Several founders were WELL members, so they built TT on the WELL model instead: "a separate space with threaded discussions that anyone could add." The first software was written by Cornell students, who later took it to found theglobe.com. Salon threw that version out within weeks and started over [1]. TT later ran on WebCrossing, which the New York Times and the Star-Tribune also used in 1996 [1][19].
  • The business thesis was ads. In January 1999, Wired described CEO David Talbot's strategy as "the Three C's – content, community, and commerce": content builds community, advertisers target the community, and the community buys online [8]. The 1999 IPO prospectus calls TT and The WELL "interactive Web communities." It lists their registration numbers as a key metric and plans to package them under one gateway and "custom-build forums for corporate, professional and educational clients" [5].
  • Advertisers didn't buy it. By 2001, Rosenberg was telling members that "advertisers, unfortunately, do not tend to want to pay for ads in community and discussion spaces ... every dollar spent on keeping TT running is coming directly from the rest of Salon's revenue" [7]. He later added that TT "generated large numbers of page views from a relatively small number of users and advertisers were not excited by that" [1].
  • Strategy two: subscription. In July 2001 posting moved behind a paywall. It first came with a WELL membership at about $10 a month, then, by fiscal 2005, as a free perk of Salon Premium ($30 to $45 a year). Anyone could still read [7][11][12][13]. Rosenberg: "That insured its survival but also assured its marginality" [1].
  • Who championed it: founders Rosenberg and Talbot, host Mary Elizabeth Williams, and Gail Williams. Gail Williams says that a couple of years into her tenure, management proposed closing TT "on Monday" with no notice, and she talked them out of it [9]. Rosenberg says management "considered, over and over, whether to shut it down" through 2007 [1].

What keeps people coming back

  • Friendship and peer support, not the articles. "We'd originally envisioned Table Talk as a place to talk about Salon stories. The TTers saw it, rightly, as a place to talk to each other" [3]. A 1990s thread, "Intelligent woman, home w/children seeking same…", "opened the floodgates for women in TT," and Mothers Who Think became one of its strongest groups [3]. Members describe pregnancy and new-baby threads, advice about spouses and careers, people who met and fell in love, and quick help for suicidal members: "There are people living now in the world because of this funny place" [3][6].
  • Named tribes and in-jokes. Buffistas, the Tarts, the Disco Ball, and threads that became lore such as "Dogs in Elk" gave members an identity that outlived the site [3][6].
  • Appointment rituals. "Our 'Buffy the Vampire Slayer' and 'Big Brother' and 'Sopranos' addicts made TT an appointment spot for post-show conversation" [3]. News events gave the same pull. Members followed the Lewinsky scandal, Princess Diana's death, 9/11 and Obama's election together there [3].
  • Guests and featured events. The front page promoted a few hosted discussions each week. In October 1999 Salon TV critic Joyce Millman was in Television for the new season, author Beth Witrogen McLeod was in Mothers Who Think, and a thread discussed Frank Gehry [20]. TT also hosted early author Q&As with Rebecca Wells, Paulo Coelho and Jane Goodall [3]. Salon's filings promoted "featured guests, including well-known commentators and writers" [11].
  • Privacy as a retention feature. When search engines began showing forum results, members asked Salon to block indexing, and it did, "while that preference cut both traffic and possible ad revenue, it made Table Talk feel comfortable to many" [2]. A semi-private, unsearchable room let people write intimately.
  • Switching costs from accumulated life history. Members used a member-made tool, Threadsuck, to save their threads. One wrote: "I have an amazing record of the experience of my first pregnancy and first daughter's infancy" [6]. Because so much personal history lived there, the deletion hurt badly.
  • Pay-to-post as a filter. After 2001, gift subscriptions became a way to show affection: "Someone who appreciates your posts" [7]. The fee probably kept the remaining group close and civil, but it ended growth (see below).
  • Weak spots: no designed progress or status mechanics (no badges, levels or karma) and no in-product hooks beyond WebCrossing's thread tracking. No evidence of notifications or email digests was found.

The content engine

  • Company-made: a host-curated front page with weekly featured discussions; staff writers (critics, columnists) posting as guests; author Q&As; and, in 2007–2008, a "Best of Table Talk" column. That column published standout member posts as Salon.com articles, tagged "an ongoing feature of Salon's vibrant community forum," with a "Want to join the discussion?" link. The 24 July 2007 Harry Potter edition is one example [14][24]. Cary Tennis's advice column had linked TT threads where readers argued over the letters. A 2005 Salon staffer described monitoring those threads, and the forums generally, for Salon Premium support complaints [25].
  • What was missing: sustained editorial presence. "Salon's editors and writers rarely hung out in TT," and the relationship turned "dysfunctional ... simultaneously craving our attention and resenting our presence" [1]. Salon put its later community effort into newer formats instead: blogs (2002), automated letters/comments (2005), and Open Salon (2008) [1].
  • Member-made: almost all of the value: long-running threads, role-playing in Imagination, the Threadsuck archiving tool, and fan communities that later became sites of their own [6][10].
  • Score, content_engine 2/5: a host and occasional guests, and a curation column only near the end. The company produced little for the community.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: one host for most of its life (Mary Elizabeth Williams, hired from CNET's community team, moving to Salon editorial about 2009) and a Director of Communities (Gail Ann Williams) shared with The WELL [3][9]. Rosenberg managed it early on as managing editor [1][7]. No evidence was found of a volunteer moderator or ambassador program, though WebCrossing let discussion hosts run threads.
  • Rules: at first nothing was deleted. After "an anguished meeting," Salon adopted Community Standards and enforced them, which led to "our first surge of angry walkouts, a handful of bannings, and at least one master's thesis project" [3]. Williams described the personal cost of being flamed in a 1996 Salon essay [26]. Automated rate limits blocked "aggressive search spiders, hyperactive bots or automated spammers" [2].
  • Feedback loop: the Salon Central folder held "Table Talk policy, community issues, and general Salon feedback" [20]. Members' feedback changed policy at least once, on search indexing [2]. No evidence was found that TT shaped Salon's editorial product.
  • Score, staff_investment 2/5: real affection from a few people, but a thin team, and management tolerated the forum rather than investing in it. "For a long time it was possible for Salon to let the community drift pleasantly in the background" [2].

Growth loops

  • Media brand to forum: traffic came from Salon's 1–3.8 million monthly readers and its links from portals and articles [5][13]. This was TT's main acquisition channel. Salon's ten section sites linked into TT [5].
  • SEO, deliberately turned off: members asked for de-indexing and Salon agreed [2]. That gave up the forum-search growth loop that sustained other forums of the 2000s.
  • Exports, not imports, after 2001: the paywall turned TT into a source of members for other sites (Buffistas, The Atlantic, WorldCrossing, The American Prospect). It no longer drew people in [6][7][10].
  • Paid conversion: Salon tried to use TT to sell subscriptions, first through The WELL and then by bundling it into Premium. Salon's CEO said Salon would "aggressively market to our base of 3.8 million monthly Salon users and convert as many as possible to Salon Premium, Table Talk and The Well" [13]. No evidence was found that TT drove a meaningful share of Premium sign-ups.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard numbers (SEC filings): 86,000 to 104,000 TT registrations from 1998 to 1999 [5]; TT and The WELL together had about 4,400 paying subscribers in 2002 and about 3,900 in 2004 [12][21]; separate TT subscription revenue was eliminated in FY2005 [22]. TT revenue was never reported on its own.
  • Executive statements: TT had "essentially no revenue" in the 2001 ad downturn [7]. Rosenberg said it was a money-loser that management repeatedly considered closing [1].
  • Company claims: filings repeated that Salon's "highly regarded interactive communities allow Salon to attract and retain users who are younger, more affluent, better educated" [5]. That claim was never quantified.
  • Third-party: the Forum One ranking (1999) [8].
  • Score, business_impact_evidence 2/5: good activity data from filings, but no retention, LTV or revenue evidence. What evidence exists suggests TT cost more than it earned.

What they did right

  1. Built community in from day one, alongside the content. The launch waited for the forums [2], and TT became one of the most-used forums on the early Web [8].
  2. Hired an experienced, devoted host. Mary Elizabeth Williams came from The WELL and CNET and stayed about 14 years. The goodwill shows: members sent her a gift when she was diagnosed with cancer [3].
  3. Wrote community standards early and enforced them. It was painful, but it kept a long-lived civil culture in which women and parents took over the forum's busiest areas [3][20].
  4. Listened on privacy. De-indexing at members' request built trust and intimacy, at a known cost [2].
  5. Republished members' best posts. "Best of Table Talk" (2007–2008) showed the forum's best writing to the magazine's audience and recognized members publicly [14][24].
  6. Gave notice the first time management wanted to close it. Gail Williams blocked a no-notice shutdown, and the 2011 closure came with a month's warning [9][2].

What they did wrong

  1. Treated community as a side feature of content. Editors stayed away, and the relationship became "dysfunctional." Rosenberg's postmortem: "What we never managed to do was find a way to knit the energy and talent of Table Talk's remarkable community with the skills and money being invested into Salon.com itself" [1]. Result: TT drifted apart, and newer Salon community products (comments, Open Salon) competed with it instead of building on it.
  2. Chose an ad model that didn't fit forum traffic. Many page views from few users is a poor ad audience [1][7]. Result: by 2001 TT had "essentially no revenue."
  3. A sudden paywall in mid-2001 at WELL prices. Result: entire subcommunities left (Buffistas, Disco Ball, the White House group), live discussions roughly halved by 2002 (a Wayback count, with caveats), and the paying base settled at a few thousand [6][7][10][12][20]. Rosenberg: it "assured its marginality" [1].
  4. Outages and aging software. TT was down two weeks in March 1999 and again "for weeks and weeks, around 2001," which broke the habits of casual members [5][6]. WebCrossing fell "increasingly out of step with the direction the Web was moving," and the 2011 infrastructure overhaul was the stated reason for closing [1][2].
  5. Deletion with no export or archive. Members got 30 days, no data export, and robots.txt still blocked the Wayback Machine. Salon was called "hysterically hostile to outside forces" by Archive Team, whose rescue had to fight Salon's bot blocking [2][23]. Result: public criticism on MetaFilter, Hacker News and from Jason Scott ("toxic attitude on user content"), and lasting damage to Salon's reputation with its most loyal readers [6][27].
  6. Neglect as policy. Salon admitted it had let the community "drift pleasantly in the background" for years [2]. Mary Elizabeth Williams remembered a holiday party where every division except community was praised [3].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Ship community with version 1 and give it a named, senior host. TT's early lead came from a launch-day forum and an experienced host who stayed 14 years.
  2. Let members steer the topics, then build structure around what they actually do. The busiest areas (Private Life, Families Who Think, Imagination, White House) were not the planned "discuss our stories" use. Give emergent groups their own folders and names.
  3. Create recurring appointments. Post-episode TV threads, election and news-night threads, and weekly featured guests (staff experts, authors) gave people a reason to return at set times.
  4. Bring members' best work to the main product. "Best of Table Talk" recognized members and promoted the forum to readers. Do it continuously, not as a late experiment.
  5. Write community standards early and protect privacy when members ask. Both built the trust that kept people sharing personal lives for years.

Avoid these

  1. Don't fund community from an ad model it can't support, then add a paywall in a hurry. Decide early whether community is a retention feature (bundle it free with the paid product) or a product itself (price it to its value). TT ended up as the first and was briefly the second.
  2. Don't let community drift apart from the core team. If staff never show up, members resent the company, and new community products end up competing with the old one.
  3. Never shut down without export, archive and a long runway. Members' history is their switching cost and your reputation. Deleting it turned 16 years of goodwill into a widely cited cautionary tale.

Related communities worth adding

  • The WELL — Salon Media Group 1999–2012, then sold to members — pioneering paid community. Salon bundled TT posting into WELL memberships; survived by member buyout (mixed)
  • Open Salon — Salon Media Group — curated blog network, 2008–2015, National Magazine Award nominee, closed after "editorial neglect" (failure)
  • Guardian Talkboards — Guardian Media Group — closed abruptly in Feb 2011, cited by MetaFilter alongside TT (already a candidate)
  • The Atlantic's Post & Riposte forums (WebCrossing) — Atlantic Media — took in TT refugees in 2001, then began charging (unknown outcome)
  • WorldCrossing.com — Lundeen & Associates / WebCrossing — the software vendor's demo community, home to TT exiles, shut about 2011 (failure)
  • Slate's The Fray — Slate/Microsoft — rival early magazine forum of the same era (outcome to research)
  • Buffistas.org / TV Tropes — fan-run spinoffs of TT; TV Tropes grew out of a Buffistas thread (shows a community outliving its sponsor)
  • theglobe.com — built from TT's discarded first codebase; dot-com community IPO and collapse (failure)
