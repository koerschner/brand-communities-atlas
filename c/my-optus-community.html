How it pays off for the company

Call deflection: Optus set call-reduction targets for the community, fed them into contact-centre headcount planning, and reported 500,000+ incremental calls avoided in FY16 and 1.52M in FY17. It also served as a launch-support channel for new products (WiFi Talk, Optus Sport/Premier League), an SEO surface for support content, and a source of product feedback.

TL;DR

  • What it is: Optus's official peer-support forum. It launched as My Optus Community on 20 January 2012 (not 2022: the Marketing Mag article the coordinator cited is dated 24 January 2012 in its metadata), ran on Lithium (later Khoros), was renamed Yes Crowd in 2016, and closed on 1 November 2021. It is the same community as "Optus Yes Crowd", so a separate catalogue entry isn't needed.
  • Why people came back: to get answers to billing, NBN, modem, Fetch TV and Optus Sport problems faster than Optus's call centre could give them. A small group of hand-picked volunteer "Crowd Champions" did much of the answering. Kudos, ranks, badges and a monthly member recognition kept those regulars visible. Most members were one-time visitors with a problem.
  • What Optus fed it: after admitting that the forum had stagnated at about 20,000 members by 2015, Optus set up a 3-person community team. It added community widgets across optus.com.au, a staff blog (June 2015), a wiki that members could nominate threads into (September 2015), and launch hubs for new products. Optus also made the community "THE default support channel" for product launches. Optus reported 500,000+ calls deflected in FY16 and 1.52 million in FY17.
  • Biggest mistake: the community was justified only by call deflection. Once Optus moved support into 24/7 messaging in the My Optus app (2019–2020) and a "Community of Experts" contact-centre model (late 2020), the forum lost its reason to exist. Optus gave members a short closure notice and deleted ten years of member answers instead of archiving them.
  • The one lesson: if your community's only metric is avoided support tickets, it will be cut the moment a cheaper support channel shows up. Build the asset (archives, member identity, product feedback) so it is worth more than its deflection number, and never delete the answers your volunteers wrote.

What the community is

My Optus Community was a branded Lithium forum at community.optus.com.au. It launched with boards for Mobile, Broadband & Telephony, Networks & Coverage and a separate Optus Business area (Wayback, 24 Feb 2012; Delimiter, 20 Jan 2012). The site later added a blog, a wiki, "Optus Gurus" device-help videos, a member leaderboard and product hubs. In 2016 it was renamed Yes Crowd ("People-Powered Support") and moved to yescrowd.optus.com.au. The old domain had 301-redirected there since April 2016 (CDX history; Yes Crowd login post, "previously known as My Optus Community"). The new name was part of Optus's 2016 "Yes" brand push: Singtel had trademarked "Yes Crowd", "Yes TV" and similar names while it considered renaming Optus itself (SMH, Mar 2016).

Optus staff from the social media team moderated it. The volunteer "Crowd Champions" were "members of the public who are hand-picked by the Yes Crowd team… they're not Optus employees" (How to use Yes Crowd, 2020 snapshot). A banner in October 2021 said: "Yes Crowd will close and be redirected to Help & Support on the 1st November 2021" (Yes Crowd homepage, as indexed). Optus's support article now reads: "Our much-loved community no longer exists :( You can still get to saved bookmarks until 15 November 2021. After that date, you'll no longer be able to access any pages" (Optus support: Yes Crowd forums).

Unofficial spaces: the independent Whirlpool Forums (Optus Broadband board) was always the main place Optus customers talked to each other without Optus in control. The most active Yes Crowd poster moved there after the closure (Whirlpool, "Optus Yes Crowd Closed"). Reddit barely matters here: r/Optus has 105 subscribers (Arctic Shift).

Origin and strategy

2012 launch: a social-care extension. Optus Digital Media director Austin R. Bryan pitched the forum as a feedback and rapport channel: "Our customers possess a wealth of first-hand knowledge… With the introduction of a community forum we hope to build rapport with our customers and provide them with more choice and control in how they interact with us." It was staffed by Optus's "award-winning social media team of 13 specialists", and Optus promised an "ideation crowd sourcing capability" within months (Optus media release, 20 Jan 2012). Optus was following rivals: Telstra CrowdSupport had launched in July 2011 and Vodafone's community in 2011 (Marketing Mag, 24 Jan 2012). Asked whether the forum would cut service jobs, Optus said it would "work in tandem with our traditional customer service channels." I found no evidence that the ideas portal ever shipped.

2015 relaunch: an explicit cost play. In its own 2016 award entry, Optus admitted that "after the hard yards of community establishment were carried out, the focus on fully leveraging the asset was lost, resulting in stagnation and faltering interest from its 20,000 members." A new digital-first Customer Service Strategy aimed "to deflect an incremental 350,000 calls from the call center during FY16." Community manager Karen Le's team secured executive sponsorship "by constructing a narrative around best practice, both within and outside telco," and kept the core team "intentionally small (3 FTE)," which the entry called "the single most important investment that we've made" (Lithys 2016: Optus – Social ROI Titan). The call-reduction projections were built into contact-centre workforce plans: "used to plan for our voice channel workforce… headcount is accurate and adjustments are made accordingly" (Lithys 2016: Optus – Support Savings MVP). For FY17 the target rose to 1,000,000 deflected calls (Lithys 2017: Optus – Support Savings Titan, search snippet).

2019–2021: the strategy moved elsewhere. In October 2019 Optus launched 24/7 asynchronous Messaging ("Unlike Live Chat, Messaging allows customers to 'come and go'") (iTWire, 14 Oct 2019). Messaging replaced Live Chat in July 2020, and Optus announced that on Yes Crowd itself (Optus Messaging explained, Jul 2020). In late 2020 Optus restructured its contact centres into geographic "Community of Experts" teams (iTnews; Telecompaper, Dec 2020). "Community of Experts" is a staffing model, not a customer community. From 2020 every Yes Crowd page carried a banner pointing users away from the forum: "For 24/7 support message our team of experts via the My Optus app" (Yes Crowd Live Chat thread, 2020). Within about a year the forum was closed.

Trajectory: flat for 2012–2014, then fast growth from 2015 to 2018, when Optus pushed traffic in from optus.com.au, product launches and single sign-on. The member count drops from 311K (Sep 2018) to 171K (Dec 2019). That coincides with a platform and theme redesign and was most likely a purge of inactive or spam accounts, but I found no explanation. Membership then crept up by about 1,000 a month until closure. The forum was still alive when it was shut: 300+ users online and new members joining daily in its last week. Signed-up members always far outnumbered active ones. FY16 resolution of 37% means most questions still weren't resolved by the community.

What keeps people coming back

  • Need, not belonging. Almost all traffic came from search or from optus.com.au and was about a problem (orders, iPhone stock, NBN connections, Fetch firmware, Premier League streaming). Homepage feeds in 2014–2016 were dominated by iPhone 6 order threads and broadband faults (Wayback 2014). Members came back when they had another problem, not out of habit.
  • Status for the few (score 2/5). The forum had Lithium kudos, accepted solutions and ranks (Regular, Respected, Esteemed and Valued Contributor, "Crowd Champion"), and badges such as "Back in Action" and one awarded for the 150th time a member thanked others (Davelew profile; Js25 badges). There was a "Member Spotlight / Member of the Month" box from 2016 and monthly "Yes Crowd Member Recognitions" threads (Wayback Mar 2016; Wayback Sep 2018). Optus's FY16 "Premium Gamification" rollout plus a formal super-user program lifted super-user contributions "by almost 300% year on year… almost 1,500 hours being spent in the community by our top 5 users" (Lithys 2016 MVP).
  • Personal ties with super users. The Champions program "runs on a 6 month cycle, is built on deep personal relationships (that extends into the 'real' world) and actively recruits users from other communities based on a set of attributes" (same source). Champions signed posts "I'm a Yes Crowd Champion (not an Optus employee)" (example thread). This was the real retention engine for the supply side: a handful of people (caaf, Yeldarb, SamSam, Davelew, petergdownload) appear on the leaderboards year after year.
  • A guaranteed staff backstop. "If your question doesn't get answered within around 24 hours, one of the Optus team will help you" (How to use Yes Crowd). Unanswered posts were routed into the social team's Lithium Social Web queue.
  • Lower friction. Single sign-on with Optus My Account (2016–17) more than doubled registrations. That is a lesson in friction, not a switching cost. No evidence was found of any community-created switching cost: no saved content, no reputation that carried over to the product.

The content engine

Score 3/5. Plenty of output and good integration, but it drifted into marketing.

  • Blog (from June 2015): "a steady rhythm of content production with over 125 articles… over 500,000 page views and… a significant proportion of search referrals" within its first year (Lithys 2016). Regular formats included fortnightly or weekly "Android Software Updates" posts (52,741 views by Oct 2020), Premier League fixture schedules, Fetch firmware notes, scam-proofing guides, "How to improve your Optus broadband at home" during COVID (46K views), and 5G Home explainers (Wayback Oct 2020). The "How to use Yes Crowd" onboarding post had 101,390 views by October 2021 (Wayback Oct 2021). By 2020 much of the blog was product promotion ("Is now the time to buy a smart watch?", "App Spotlight") rather than support.
  • Member-nominated wiki (from September/October 2015): Optus called it "a world first". It customised Lithium's knowledge-base module so that "threads can be nominated as Wiki articles by the community, who can also make updates", giving 63 articles and 26,000 views in about 7 months (Lithys 2016 MVP; launch post "My Optus Community just levelled up!"). Members wrote modem and NBN bridging guides that got thousands of views ("Bridging your NBN Modem/Router" by Darrell: 3,894 views by Aug 2016). Staff wrote evergreen articles such as "Can I use my BYO MODEM on the NBN?" (14,594 views by Sep 2018).
  • Launch hubs: for WiFi Talk (September 2015), every customer broadcast linked to one community blog post: "Over 67,000 people viewed the post, resulting in 230 comments… only 20 support calls were logged about WiFi Talk issues each week" (Lithys 2016 MVP). A Pre-Paid Welcome Hub was built into prepaid onboarding. A Premier League hub (2016) combined articles, Ask-the-community, Live Chat and the forum (Lithys 2017 Digital CX). Separate hubs served SMB (Optus Business Care, Optus Loop Live) and handsets (Loop Live hub). Optus also posted executive "thought leader" pieces, such as Optus Sport's December 2016 "What we've learnt from the launch of the Premier League" (12,011 views) (Wayback).
  • Distribution: "contextually relevant widgets that surface community conversation on the optus.com.au website, including the homepage, help & support, product and sales pages," plus community calls to action in every electronic communication (Lithys 2016 MVP).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff (3/5): 13 social media specialists moderated at launch (2012). By 2015–16 there was a dedicated 3-FTE community team, and Optus's social response team had 18 agents handling "in excess of half a million interactions per annum" (Shorty Awards entry). Optus said members' greater participation cut the need for moderator input by 40% and let it absorb "an 82% increase in engagement volume across social with minimal increase in resource (4 FTE)" (Lithys 2016 MVP). Named community managers over time were Karen Le (2015–16), "Matt – Community Manager" (2016) and Ray_YC and Dan_C (2018–21). Staff posted under an "Optus" account and personal handles. A Whirlpool user noted that "They dont have non Optus staff on Yescrowd as tagged moderators" (Whirlpool).
  • Super users: Crowd Champions were hand-picked, ran on 6-month cycles and had real-world relationships with staff. Perks beyond rank and recognition are not documented. No evidence was found of free devices or trips like Telstra's Mobile Insiders.
  • Rules: guidelines were updated in 2018 (guidelines). There was a strict no-personal-details rule because the forum was public and staff "cannot access or view customer accounts/orders" there (Wayback Oct 2020). Anything involving an account had to go off-platform.
  • Feedback loop: weak evidence. One visible example is Optus's April 2021 Visual Voicemail launch post, which opened: "For years, we've seen a lot of discussion here on Yes Crowd from Optus customers voicing their dissa[tisfaction]" (Yes Crowd homepage, Oct 2021). The promised ideas portal never appeared.

Growth loops

  • SEO: the blog and wiki drove "a significant proportion of search referrals". Google still indexes Yes Crowd thread titles years after the deletion, which shows how much search value was thrown away.
  • Owned-channel funnelling: this was the main loop. Optus put widgets on optus.com.au, used the community as the support link in product-launch broadcasts, embedded it in prepaid onboarding, and added single sign-on. Traffic grew because Optus pushed it there, not because members invited others.
  • Competitive social listening (adjacent): Optus's social team (on Lithium Social Web / Khoros Care) engaged "almost 5,000 prospects" who were complaining about rivals, with "13% sales conversion… more than $400K of annualized revenue." That was Twitter and Facebook work, not the forum (Lithys 2016; Khoros case study PDF).

What they did right

  1. Admitted the stall and relaunched with a mandate. In 2015 Optus turned a neglected 20K-member forum into a funded program with an executive sponsor, a target tied to workforce planning and a small dedicated team. Members went from 32K to 311K in about three years (table above).
  2. Made the community the default launch-support channel. WiFi Talk, Optus Sport and prepaid onboarding all sent customers to one canonical community page. That gave a single source of truth, and the comment threads under it became the FAQ (Lithys 2016 MVP).
  3. Let members turn threads into documentation. The member-nominated wiki took good answers out of the forum stream and gave volunteers a lasting artefact (same source).
  4. Removed login friction. Single sign-on with My Account (+188% registrations) and early mobile-responsive design (more than 50% of traffic was mobile by 2016) (Lithys 2017 snippet).
  5. Invested in a few super users personally, with 6-month Champion cycles and real-world relationships. This produced roughly 1,500 hours a year from five people.
  6. Guaranteed staff follow-up within about 24 hours, so asking was never a dead end.

What they did wrong

  1. Launched and then left it (2012–2014). A forum staffed by an existing social team, with no strategy, stalled at about 20,000 members. Optus says so itself (Lithys 2016). Consequence: three lost years and a relaunch that had to be sold internally as a cost project.
  2. Promised an ideas portal and never delivered it (Optus release, 2012). Consequence: there was no product co-creation, which would have given the community a value beyond deflection.
  3. Tied its identity to a campaign name. It became "Yes Crowd" because of a 2016 rebrand idea that Optus publicly denied (ZDNet, Mar 2016). The URL changed and every existing link had to be redirected.
  4. Measured only deflection. When Messaging (2019–20) and the Community of Experts (2020) became the support strategy, Optus put its own app's messaging banner on every forum page, sending people away. Consequence: closure within about a year.
  5. Gave short notice and destroyed the knowledge. Optus showed a closure banner, kept bookmarks working for two weeks, then removed everything. "They shut it down with about three days notice last October… all the answers people gave over the years have disappeared" (Whirlpool). Consequence: volunteers' work and the SEO value were gone. Users still searched for the answers a year later ("Optus deleted Yes Crowd some time ago", Whirlpool), and Telstra users cited it as precedent when CrowdSupport was deleted (Whirlpool).
  6. Gave the community no power over accounts. Staff couldn't see accounts in the forum, so the hardest problems (billing, cancellations) always ended with "contact Optus directly". That pushed angry threads ("is there a worse company for 'customer service'?") into the most public channel (Yes Crowd, Feb 2019).
  7. Bad timing (judgment). Within two years of closing, Optus had its September 2022 data breach (about 9.8M records, APRA) and its 8 November 2023 national outage (Senate inquiry). In both crises Optus had no owned space where trusted volunteers and staff could answer customers in public. Customers instead complained about "chatbots and 'rubbish' communication" (Guardian via Wikipedia).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Route launches through one community page. Point every launch email, SMS and in-app message at a single community post, and let the comments become the living FAQ (WiFi Talk: 67K views and about 20 calls a week).
  2. Let members promote answers into documentation (nominate a thread, edit the wiki). It turns one-off help into durable content and gives volunteers authorship.
  3. Remove the login wall. Use one identity across your product and community. Optus's registrations more than doubled with single sign-on.
  4. Invest personally in your top 5–20 helpers: hand-picked status, time-boxed cohorts, real relationships with staff, a visible "not an employee" signature. That is where the hours come from.
  5. Promise a staff backstop (for example, "answered within 24 hours or we step in") so asking in public is never riskier than calling.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let one avoided-cost metric be the community's only justification. Optus's forum survived exactly as long as it was the cheapest support channel. Track feedback shipped, retention of members versus non-members, and search value too.
  2. Never delete the corpus. If you must close, freeze it read-only, hand super users a public thank-you and a migration path, and give months of notice, not days.
  3. Don't put a campaign name on the community, or let your other channels quietly pull its traffic away (for example, a "message us in the app" banner on every forum page) without deciding the community's future on purpose.
