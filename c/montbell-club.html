How it pays off for the company

The ¥1,500 annual fee pays for the magazine and mailings (about ¥405M a year at 270,000 members in 2011; about ¥1.9bn at 1.25M members, an estimate). Points of 5-9% bring buyers back to mont-bell's own 130+ stores and web store; more than 80% renew each year. The 1.2M-member audience lets mont-bell sign up municipalities and partner facilities that market to members for free, and those partners also open stores and joint events.

TL;DR

  • What it is: mont-bell Club is the paid membership (¥1,500 a year, unchanged since launch) of Japan's largest outdoor brand. Founder Isamu Tatsuno started it in 1986, modelled on his amateur alpine club's mimeographed newsletter [2][12]. Members get a quarterly magazine (OUTWARD), 5-9% points, discounts at about 2,300 partner "Friend Shops", and entry to members-only Friend Fair expos. Membership passed 1 million in April 2021 and was about 1.25 million in 2026 [3][7][8].
  • Why people come back: a fee that is easy to earn back (5% points plus a 500-point renewal bonus, free shipping), status cards that improve with every year of tenure, a magazine people keep, and an outdoor "passport" that pays off when they travel. More than 80% renew each year [1][21].
  • What the company feeds it: staff-written content (OUTWARD is edited in-house, never outsourced) [9], twice-yearly fairs staffed entirely by employees with the founder on stage [36], tours and environmental sports events, and a nationwide web of 130 Friend Areas and 202 municipal and institutional agreements that turns towns into partners [1][19].
  • Biggest mistake: the club is a hub-and-spoke relationship (brand ↔ member, brand ↔ region) with almost nothing for members to do together. Status means tenure and spending, not contribution, and the main in-person ritual disappeared for five years (2020-24) [16][33][34]. The 2025 end of the printed catalog also removed a tangible perk [24].
  • The one lesson: charge a small fee from day one and use it to fund things members are proud of (a real magazine, disaster relief, partners in places they love). The fee filters for fans and makes renewal an annual vote of confidence. Tatsuno calls it "our annual examination" [5].

What the community is

mont-bell is a privately held maker and retailer of outdoor gear based in Osaka, founded in 1975 by Isamu Tatsuno, who made the second Japanese ascent of the Eiger North Face in 1969 [2][5]. Group revenue was about ¥210bn in fiscal 2025 [37], and it runs 130+ Japanese stores [36][38].

Origin and strategy

Two motives appear in the sources, and they fit together.

  1. Getting around the wholesale system. In the mid-1980s mont-bell sold through agents, wholesalers and retailers, which stocked only proven sellers. Executive director Shiro Takeyama recalls "frustration" and says the club began as a way to mail mont-bell's own catalog and magazine straight to consumers [3]. Forbes Japan's sponsored feature ties the launch to mont-bell's start in mail order [10]. The first directly run store opened only in 1991 [13].
  2. Building an alpine-club-style fellowship. Tatsuno's memoir: his amateur club, the Osaka Anahori-kai, "published a handmade, mimeographed newsletter... sharing members' climbing records and mountain information strengthened our solidarity. At mont-bell, too, I wanted to exchange information and share values with customers, so in 1986 I launched mont-bell Club." At first there was "no benefit or service besides the catalog. Even so, people joined one by one." He named the magazine OUTWARD ("let's head outside") [2] (translated).

Why charge a fee. The fee was set from what printing and postage cost, and it was paid "so the club would last" [1][3]. Takeyama: "We never thought of making it free to grow membership. What mattered was how to make the club sustainable" [3] (translated). Tatsuno treats the fee as a referendum: "Politicians have elections; for MontBell we have our Club members. As long as the membership grows, it shows we're doing the right thing. If it decreases... we need to change the system... it's a very good barometer of our actions—our annual examination" [5]. In 2013: "We have 400,000 'friends' who are willing to pay this... more important than the vote a politician receives" [6].

The champion and the goal. Tatsuno set the target himself. At the 30th-anniversary party in 2005, with about 70,000-78,000 members, he told staff to aim for 1 million, which "surprised everyone". Every department then reworked how stores invited people to join. The club reached 1 million in April 2021, sixteen years later [1][3]. In 2011 he had said "My goal is one million members in 10 years. Well, maybe 30 years" [5].

How it ties to the business model. mont-bell holds prices down by selling direct and does not run seasonal sales [9][11]. The club is the main way it gives regular customers something back without discounting: points, outlet access at the fair, partner discounts. It is also the channel through which the company explains unpopular decisions. In 2026 Tatsuno said mont-bell explains its move away from fluorine-based water repellents, which lowers performance and raises prices, "through the Montbell Club... through this platform, we explain our mission and beliefs" [7].

Membership has grown every year in the company's telling [1]. Growth sped up after 2011 (270,000 to 1M in about 10 years), tracking store openings and Japan's outdoor boom. Revenue rose from ¥84bn (2019) to ¥94bn (FY Aug 2022) to about ¥210bn group (FY2025) [3][38][37], with ¥180bn+ forecast for the parent's FY Aug 2026 [39]. Member age skews 40-50s, with more young women recently [1].

What keeps people coming back

1. A fee that turns renewal into a habit and a decision. The fee is low and easy to earn back. Each join or renewal gives 500 points; year-1 points are 5%, so about ¥20,000-30,000 of purchases covers the fee [10][21]. Online shipping is free for members regardless of order size [21][45]. Members renew because the maths works, and the yearly payment keeps the brand on their mind. Since 2024 mont-bell no longer mails a new card at each renewal, only when status changes [23].

2. Tenure-based status. Card color and point rate climb with years of continuous membership (or cumulative spend): Blue 5% (year 1), Silver 6% (2-4 years), Gold 7% (5-9 years), Platinum 8% (10-19 years), Diamond 9% (20+ years or ¥1M) [21]. Since May 2017 one status-based rate applies to products, M.O.C. tours and mont-bell restaurants alike [22][25]. Members get an original pin badge when they join and at every status step-up [27]. Because status is tied to continuous years, lapsing hurts, but the rules soften it: rejoining within a year counts as continuous and restores your points [45]. Blogs proudly announce "I became Diamond" [60]. Mocking these tiers is also a theme (see Member voice).

3. A magazine members keep. OUTWARD is quarterly, members-only, with covers by wildlife photographer Mitsuaki Iwago, founder dialogues, expert columns, Friend Area features and reports on what the Club Fund supported [9][10]. Takeyama calls it the most popular perk. At 1M+ copies it is "said to be among the world's largest outdoor magazines," and it is planned and edited by staff, not an agency, because "we are users ourselves, so we know what they want" [9] (translated). Back issues are resold as sets [61].

4. The twice-yearly Friend Fair. It is members-only, but members can bring family and friends and can join at the door [28][32]. It mixes an outlet sale (the only time mont-bell discounts much), about 100 regional partner booths, kayak and climbing tryouts, a raffle, talks and live music [1][28]. Attendees stay long [1]. Every role is filled by employees. Tatsuno in 2019: "It doesn't make money, but everyone says they want to do it, so it continues. I'm going to play the flute on stage now" [36] (translated). He also gives talks at the fairs (the 2026 Aomori fair, for one) [35] and personally guides some M.O.C. hikes [11]. Fair-goers count their visits ("my 9th fair") [55].

5. A reason to travel. Friend Shops and Friend Areas give discounts at huts, ryokan, ropeways and campgrounds across Japan. That makes the card useful on trips, so members "go out more often to earn back the fee" [9]. Fair booths send members to those regions, and "a good cycle" follows [1].

6. Pride in where the fee goes. ¥50 of every fee goes to the Club Fund, which pays for the Outdoor Gientai disaster-relief corps (Kobe 1995, Tohoku 2011, Noto 2024) and adventure grants [4][16][40]. SankeiBiz reports the company saying this "had no small effect on renewal rates and new sign-ups" [4]. Overseas members can donate points to Gientai [26]. The fund page says plainly that it exists so members "feel proud to be a mont-bell Club member" [40].

What is missing: streaks, leaderboards, member profiles, peer groups, user-generated content and recognition for contribution. The only "social" mechanics are in person (fairs, tours, SEA TO SUMMIT).

The content engine

  • OUTWARD (since 1996): quarterly, 52 pages, roughly doubled to about 100 pages from spring 2026, when mont-bell ended its printed general catalog [24][59]. A digital edition for members started in the 2020s [58].
  • Catalogs: from the 1980s to the autumn/winter 2025 issue, members got the gear and wear catalogs twice a year (worth about ¥500 each, according to one blogger) [42]. A catalog app followed in 2011 [15].
  • Events as content: M.O.C. runs beginner hikes, rafting, tent-pitching clinics at stores, and "Bōken-juku" adventure schools [15][53]. SEA TO SUMMIT (2009-) turns Friend Areas into human-powered kayak-bike-hike routes with a symposium on nature [14][20]. Japan Eco Track (2014-) promotes self-guided routes [15].
  • Publishing: mont-bell took over the mountaineering magazine Gakujin in 2014 and runs mont-bell Books [15].
  • Online fairs: twice a year since 2020, with member pre-sales of outlet stock, 10% points, prize draws for 500 people, and quizzes about Friend Areas (40-50 questions, 175-178 prizes) [29].
  • Social: X (99K followers), Instagram (~152K), a YouTube channel; mostly announcements [49][50].
  • Member-made content: no evidence of any program to encourage, curate or reward it. Fan content (fair reports, "is the club worth it" posts, Diamond-status posts) is plentiful and does the club's SEO for free, but the company does not curate it.

Score 4/5: a rare in-house print magazine with a 30-year cadence, plus events, but no UGC loop.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: a "mont-bell Club secretariat" handles membership [23]. Public relations is led by executive director Shiro Takeyama [3][20]. Fairs and tours are run by regular staff, not agencies [36]. Team size is not disclosed.
  • Founder: Tatsuno (born 1947) is chairman and CEO and the sole owner, which he says lets the company ignore short-term profit [5]. He sets the goals (1M members), names the magazine, holds dialogues in it, and appears at fairs [2][35][36].
  • Partners instead of ambassadors: there is no ambassador or MVP program. The "superusers" are institutions: about 2,300 Friend Shops, about 130 Friend Areas and 202 signed agreements. Facilities ask to join because they get "pinpoint" access to 1.2M outdoor-segmented members [1]. Academic analysis (Sawabata) describes the benefits for mont-bell as "activating club members, spreading the management philosophy" and for municipalities as tourism promotion [17].
  • Feedback into product: Tatsuno is explicit that this is not the club's job: "When it comes to making things, don't take orders from customers" [36] (translated). New product ideas come from employees, who are users themselves [36]. This is the opposite of Snow Peak, where campfire feedback reshaped the company [56].
  • Rules: membership terms cover points and benefits only [63]. There is no community moderation because there is no community space to moderate.

Growth loops

  1. The checkout ask. Every store invites customers to join at the register (people report being asked on every visit) [44]. After 2005 every department retrained on how to make that ask [1].
  2. Fair door sign-ups. Non-members can join on the day; members bring family and friends [28][32].
  3. Partners advertise the club. Municipal websites (e.g. Nikko) explain the club to residents and tourists [54]. Prefectures sign agreements in public ceremonies at fairs (Aomori, Feb 2026) [35]. Regional booths at fairs then draw members to visit.
  4. Store network as distribution. Partner regions often host mont-bell stores and facilities (Daisen, Tateyama, Gokayama) [14][15][16], so each new agreement can add a place to sign people up.
  5. Fan SEO. Hundreds of "is mont-bell Club worth ¥1,500?" articles, many positive, rank for the obvious query [21][43].
  6. Household benefit. Family members living with a cardholder get the same benefits, which spreads the fee across a household [62].

Evidence of business impact

Hard-ish (company-stated, not audited):

  • Renewal rate above 80% a year (PR team to FASHIONSNAP, 2025) [1].
  • Fee revenue: ¥405M a year at 270,000 members (Tatsuno, 2011) [5]. At 1.25M members that would be about ¥1.9bn (estimate), most of it spent on the magazine and mailings [1]. ¥50 per member goes to the fund (about ¥60M a year, estimate).
  • Members ≈ 10% of Japan's hiking population (2021) [3].
  • 202 institutional agreements and 2,300 partner facilities exist largely because of the audience the club delivers [1][19].

Not disclosed: members' share of sales ("not public, but clearly driving solid results," FASHIONSNAP) [1], frequency or spend uplift, NPS, churn by tier. mont-bell is private, so there are no filings.

Independent research: one peer-reviewed-style case study (Sawabata, J-STAGE, c. 2024-25) of mont-bell's municipal agreements. It names club-member activation as a benefit to the company but does not measure it [17].

Several glowing Forbes Japan pieces are BRANDVOICE (paid) content [10][11], so treat them as company claims. Score 3/5.

What they did right

  1. Charging from day one, and never raising the fee. It filtered for fans, paid for the magazine, and made "grow paid membership" an honest score of brand health [1][3][5]. The fee has been ¥1,500 for 40 years [1].
  2. A clear, founder-owned goal that ran for 16 years. The 2005 "1 million members" target redirected store behavior and was hit in 2021 [1][3].
  3. A magazine people value, made in-house. It explains the company's values, products and causes in one place. The company doubled it when cutting the catalog instead of cutting both [9][24].
  4. Rewarding tenure over spending alone. Point rates climb for loyalty even without big spending, and the one-year grace rule forgives lapses [21][45].
  5. Making the card a passport. Partner discounts and Friend Areas tie membership to going outside, which fits the brand's purpose, gives a reason to renew even without buying gear, and recruits towns as co-marketers [1][9][20].
  6. Fee-funded causes members can see. Disaster relief bought with members' fees, reported in OUTWARD, builds pride and (per the company) renewals [1][4].
  7. Staff-run, founder-attended events. Fairs are cheap for members (free entry), heavy on experiences, and staffed by enthusiasts [1][36].

What they did wrong

  1. No peer layer. There is no space where members meet, help or recognize each other outside a fair hall. Nobody "owns" anything, and status means tenure, not contribution. Unlike Snow Peak Club's 2005-2015 site or Snow Peak Way's fireside ritual [56], mont-bell built no member-to-member bonds. The cost: the club is easy to treat as a points card, and people join only in big-purchase years. A top Chiebukuro answer advises "join only in the year you buy a tent" [44]. Score 2/5 for member ownership.
  2. Five years without the flagship ritual. Big-venue fairs stopped after autumn 2019; spring 2020 was cancelled [33]. Online fairs (a sale plus quizzes) and small fairs in Friend Areas (2022-24) filled in [16]. Osaka and Yokohama returned only in 2025 [34]. FASHIONSNAP presents the return as an anniversary revival [1]. A returning member found the 2025 layout "almost the same as six years ago" [41].
  3. Cutting tangible perks. The 2021-22 catalogs were sharply thinned ("saddened over a million mont-bell fans") [42], and the printed catalog ended with the autumn/winter 2025 issue, with no digital catalog [24]. The magazine expansion partly offsets this, but members lost something they had used to justify the fee.
  4. Status that outsiders mock. Tiers based only on years paid invite the jab (in a related Q&A listed on a Chiebukuro page) that Platinum "only shows you've paid the fee a long time" [43]. A "mont-bell ojisan is uncool" debate in 2023 tied the brand to middle-aged men, and the members' core age band is 40-50s [1][48].
  5. The fee as a wall for casual and foreign buyers. On the Japanese site, foreign shoppers find they must join the paid club to order [47]. Montbell Club Global is free but has no magazine, fair or community, so the values club does not travel abroad [26].
  6. The outlet dominates the fair. Bloggers write "complete guides" to the outlet scramble, and the outlet hall is the most crowded part [41]. The sale risks becoming the reason to come, ahead of partners and talks.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Charge a small, stable fee and publish what it buys. A fee you can earn back in one or two purchases selects for fans, funds real member value, and turns renewal into a yearly vote you can track (mont-bell: 80%+ renew) [1][5].
  2. Reward tenure, and forgive lapses. Status that climbs with continuous years, a physical token at each step (pins), and a one-year grace period that restores status and points [21][27][45].
  3. Make one owned, staff-made publication the backbone. A quarterly that people keep does more than a feed, especially for explaining hard decisions (price rises, material changes) [7][9].
  4. Turn partners into the community's destinations. Give members something to do with membership out in the world, and give partners access to your members in return. They will promote you for free [1][19].
  5. Tie a slice of the fee to a cause and show it. ¥50 per member, visible in the magazine, gives members pride and the brand a story [4][40].

Avoid these

  1. Don't stop at a hub-and-spoke club. Without peer spaces or recognition for contribution, members treat membership as a coupon and churn around purchases [44]. Add at least one member-to-member ritual (Snow Peak's fireside talk is the domestic counterexample [56]).
  2. Don't let the flagship ritual lapse for years or freeze its format. An online sale is not a fair. Plan a degraded but social version, and refresh the format [33][41].
  3. Don't cut the tangible perks members used to justify the fee without a clear replacement [24][42].
