TL;DR

  • What it was: an invitation-style community of crafters, mostly scrapbookers, built for Fiskars by the agency Brains on Fire. It launched in mid-2006 around four paid, part-time "Lead Fiskateers", with a blog, message board, member gallery and lots of in-person "crops" and store demos. It grew from a goal of 200 members to member #10,000 by September 2012 (SMT 2008; deRosier 2012).
  • Why people came back: identity more than product. Each member got a number for life, engraved on a free pair of orange-and-green scissors sold nowhere else. On top of that came a steady stream of online crops, challenges with prizes, committees, member spotlights and face-to-face events. Members talked about friendship, not scissors.
  • What Fiskars put in: paid leads (planned at 15–20 hours a week, actually far more), a three-day training on transparency, welcome kits, trips and events, prize product, live chats with engineers, and real product changes made on members' feedback (MarketingProfs 2007; Rettberg 2009).
  • The biggest mistake: once the internal champion left, Fiskars cut the leads from four or five down to one. In December 2013 it shut the forums and gallery and kept only a product blog with no comments plus a Facebook page, then ended the program in March 2015 (Social Seeder 2015; Fiskafriends 2015).
  • The lesson: a small, costly status token (a numbered, engraved object) plus a human host who answers every newcomer can make an ordinary product into a tribe. But that tribe depends on an internal sponsor and two-way spaces, and a company that changes it into a one-way content channel will lose it.

What the community is

Fiskateers ("Fisk-a-teers") was Fiskars Americas' brand-ambassador community for paper crafters, which in practice meant scrapbookers, stampers and card makers, with sewing and gardening added later. It ran on Fiskars' own site, fiskateers.com. The first captures in the Wayback Machine date from June 2006 (CDX), and Brand Autopsy wrote about "Fiskars Crafting Ambassadors" that same month (SMT). The site had a blog written mostly by the Leads, a members-only message board and gallery, online crops (timed bursts of crafting challenges), and later an idea-submission and rating area (HubSpot/Gillin 2009).

Who ran it: Fiskars' PR and communications function, not advertising. The program reported to the PR director (MarketingProfs). Its champion was Suzanne Fanning, Director of Communications, Fiskars Americas, working under division president Chad Vincent (BRAVA). Brains on Fire (Geno Church, Spike Jones) designed it and ran research and onboarding.

Status: defunct. The forums and gallery closed in December 2013 (deRosier 2015). The site was a WordPress product blog through mid-2015, returned 403 from August 2015 and now 301-redirects to fiskars.com (checked 2026-11-06). The official end was announced in March 2015 (Social Seeder), and the Facebook page also ran "until 2015, when the company changed directions" (Craft Industry Alliance 2021). The unofficial successor is Fiskafriends, a secret Facebook group that "over 140 numbered Fiskateers" started in March 2014 and that admits any numbered Fiskateer (Fiskafriends).

Origin and strategy

  • The business problem. Fiskars' School, Office & Craft division was "a significant profit driver" but was losing share to "good looking, adequately performing" low-cost competitors (Chad Vincent). In a brand audit, people said that if Fiskars were a drink it would be milk, and if it were a snack, crackers (Rettberg). In Geno Church's words, "Fiskars customers had no emotional connection to their tools" (MarketingProfs).
  • The insight. Brains on Fire ran about 150 interviews with members of Yahoo crafting groups, held a four-city casting call, and had Umbria mine craft blogs. It found that crafters weren't passionate about tools but about "sharing their lives", and that online crafters skewed younger than the 40-to-60 audience Fiskars had been targeting (MarketingProfs; Social Seeder). Vincent's own version is that the trigger was noticing fans online defending Fiskars and teaching other people to use its new, complex tools. The company began training those helpers, and they asked for a place to gather (Chad Vincent).
  • The tie to the business model. The original goals were awareness, credibility and online conversation, plus "hundreds of empowered, kindred spirits" (MarketingProfs). Over time the program took on two other jobs. It became a retail channel, with Leads and certified demonstrators teaching in independent and big-box craft stores; Francois Gossieaux reported in 2008 that it was "now fully funded by Big Box craft stores" (an unverified secondhand claim, quoted in SMT). It also became Fiskars' main research panel: Fiskars "no longer conducts expensive focus groups" (Gillin 2009). It began as a PR line item and later "has now taken on a larger part of the budget and mix" (SMT 2008).
  • The deliberate unbranding. "If it was branded as Fiskars, it would not be as authentic," Church told PRWeek. The community was "sponsored" by Fiskars, which served as a "conduit" (PRWeek).

What keeps people coming back

  1. A number for life, engraved on a physical object. Members got sequential numbers, shown in usernames ("Tania #4626") and engraved on a free pair of two-tone orange-and-green scissors that were not sold in stores (Capadia 2012; Rettberg). Low numbers carried status: Fiskateer #002 still signed with her number in the 2015 Fiskafriends blog hop (deRosier 2015). The scissors also started conversations at crops: "people always ask where people got their scissors" (Rettberg). Members were still using them and posting about it in 2022 (2Peas Refugees).
  2. A personal host at the door. To join, you picked a Lead by photo and sent her a personal email, and the Leads answered personally (Rettberg; Brand Autopsy). Fiskars presented this as a "built-in barrier" ("You must be invited, and certified"), though it "rarely turns down applicants" (SMT; Gillin).
  3. Rituals and a steady drumbeat. These included online crops with dozens of numbered challenges (38 in one August 2009 crop, My Little Scrap Shop), weekly challenges with winners, "TUEStorials", Fiskars Fridays, sneak peeks before the CHA trade show, a book club with author chats, and an orange challenge at every crop (deRosier 2012).
  4. Recognition ladders. The ladder ran from Fiskateer of the Year to the Nifty 50, then Certified Fiskars Demonstrator, Lead, and finally "Fiskateer Legend" when a Lead's two-year term ended (Social Seeder; Wayback 2009). The blog regularly featured members by number.
  5. Member-run sub-groups. "Fiskamittees" formed around charity, kids and pets, along with a Thrifty Fiskateer Club and the Random Acts of Crafting committee, which got 50+ members to make more than 1,200 bookmarks to hand out at CHA (Wayback 2009; Simple Marketing).
  6. In-person bonds. Events included the 2007 San Antonio Roundup for the Nifty 50, the 2008 regional Fiskafriendzies, the 360th-birthday party in 2009 (100 honored guests, all Fiskateers invited), the 2011 "United States of Fiskateers" (local events in all 50 states, led by local members), and local days with an engineer in the room (Social Seeder; Gillin). Fanning: in-person events "sustain and strengthen online programs" (Simple Marketing).
  7. Being heard. Engineers renamed themselves "Fiskaneers" and held live chats. A disliked paper trimmer was redesigned, and a curling Heidi Grace paper was reformulated before a big print run (MarketingProfs).
  8. Prizes and "happy mail". Prize product went out often (Scrapbook.com 2007).

There were no in-product hooks; the product is a pair of scissors. The switching cost was purely social: your number, your gallery and your friends.

The content engine

  • Company and Lead content. A near-daily blog by the paid Leads (several posts a day in early 2009, by CDX count) mixing projects, life updates, member spotlights, product sneak peeks and challenge prompts. Leads were explicitly encouraged to "talk about anything that was going on in their lives" (Social Seeder). They were trained not to be "pimping" Fiskars, and to talk about competitors' tools too (MarketingProfs; Rettberg). A "TV show on the site draws about 25,000 viewers per month," shaped by member feedback (PRWeek). Live chats were held with engineers and authors.
  • Member content. Gallery uploads (members-only), tutorials republished on the main blog with credit and number, video testimonials ("Why I love being a Fiskateer"), and crop and challenge entries. Fiskars also mailed blank journals with prompts to enthusiasts and used the art that came back in print ads (Vincent).
  • Fanning's pyramid. At the base were broad social channels (Facebook, Twitter, Flickr, YouTube). In the middle sat fiskarscrafts.com with Fiskars TV and contests. At the top were Fiskateers and the gardening equivalent, Project Orange Thumb (Simple Marketing).

Score, content engine 4: the cadence was high and the content was human and member-led, but it depended on the Leads and collapsed when their number fell.

People, programs and governance

  • Lead Fiskateers. Four were picked from dozens of in-person interviews in 2006, part-time and paid, planned at 15–20 hours a week ("in practice they probably spent about 80 hours a week"). Their pay disclosure was on their blogs and in the job descriptions. Their jobs: blogging, the message board, live chats, trade shows, classes in stores, and relationships with store owners (MarketingProfs; Rettberg). In 2008 five new Leads replaced them after an open video application, and terms then rotated every two years "by popular demand" (Social Seeder). There were two Leads in 2012 and one by 2014.
  • Certified Fiskars Demonstrators. The Nifty 50 were trained in 2007, and by 2009 more than 1,000 Fiskateers were "getting paid by craft stores to teach classes" (Chief Marketer). The stores paid, not Fiskars, which made the program partly self-funding.
  • Staff. Fanning acted as "facilitator, coordinator, enabler, nurturer and protector", shielding the community from corporate pressure and adding committees and widgets as members needed them (Simple Marketing). Most local meetings included someone from product development (Gillin).
  • The feedback loop. Members got early polls, named products, and rated ideas on an idea site. One request for new template shapes produced 70 usable ideas in 12 hours. Fiskars ran surveys on retailers' behalf and used the panel to pitch Target (Gillin; Simple Marketing). Jackie Huba cited "13 new product ideas/month" and 85% likely to recommend (TopRank).
  • Rules. "Everyone who joins our community is under no obligation or has any requirements" (Lead May Flaum, Scrapbook.com 2007). No evidence was found of formal moderation problems.

Scores: member ownership 4 (committees, member-led local events, elected Leads, but the platform and the Leads' pay stayed with Fiskars). Staff investment 3 (heavy in 2006–2011, then deliberately run down).

Growth loops

  • Scissors as a conversation starter. The unusual two-tone handles prompted questions at crops (Rettberg).
  • Leads and demonstrators in stores, which brought in new members and sales at the same time. Many independent store owners became Fiskateers themselves and brought their customers (Simple Marketing).
  • Member blogs. Members' personal blogs ended posts with "contact one of the Leads", for example deRosier and Beccy's Place, and members recruited on other forums (Scrapbook.com). Welcome kits included tips for recruiting (Chief Marketer).
  • Marketing-press fame. It was a 2007 Forrester Groundswell finalist for Energizing (SMT), a PRWeek case, the opening of Paul Gillin's Secrets of Social Media Marketing, a chapter of Brains on Fire (2010), and a BlogHer Business case (2009). This helped internal support more than it brought in crafters.

Evidence of business impact

All of the evidence comes from Fiskars or Brains on Fire. None of it is audited, and Fiskars Group's 2007 and 2008 annual reports don't mention the program (checked the 2008 report and the 2007 report).

  • Store sales: "stores visited by a Fiskateer experienced three times the sales growth of other stores in Q1 2007" (PRWeek). This was later loosely restated as "a threefold increase in year-over-year sales" (Gillin), "double their sales" (Agility PR 2018) and "double its profit in key markets" (blurb for Brains on Fire, O'Reilly). The selection effect is obvious: Leads went to engaged stores. In 2007 Fiskars "declines to say by how much" sales rose (MarketingProfs).
  • Conversation: branded mentions rose 400–446% in year one and about 600% by year two, measured by Umbria (PRWeek; SMT).
  • Research savings: the focus groups were dropped (Gillin). Concrete cost savings came from the Heidi Grace paper fix (MarketingProfs).
  • Wider context: Vincent credits the larger turnaround (new products rising from 10% to over 30% of sales) to an "Enthusiast network" that included the Fiskateers (Vincent).

Score, business impact evidence 2: there are plenty of vivid company claims but no outside verification, no measure of member retention, and Fiskars eventually decided the program wasn't worth keeping.

What they did right

  1. Built the community around the customer's passion (memory-keeping and friendship), not the tool. Brains on Fire's research reframed the brief, and the Leads blogged about their lives (Rettberg).
  2. Turned a commodity into a token of status. Numbered, engraved scissors with unique colors cost little and lasted 15+ years in members' hands (2Peas Refugees 2022).
  3. Gave the community paid but transparent human hosts. Pay was disclosed, the Leads were told to say both good and bad, and new members were greeted personally (Rettberg).
  4. Rotated leadership and created ladders, so the most engaged members had somewhere to climb (CFD, Nifty 50, Lead, Legend) (Social Seeder).
  5. Made the product team listen visibly. Fiskaneers, live chats, redesigns, and members naming products (MarketingProfs; Gillin).
  6. Paired online and offline. Crafting happens offline (Church: 92% of scrapbooking word of mouth is offline), so crops, demos and regional events were the core, not an extra (SMT).
  7. Made the program partly self-funding through store-paid demonstrations (Chief Marketer).

What they did wrong

  1. Champion risk. "It all started when the internal champion of the program left Fiskars." After that the structure was dismantled (Social Seeder 2015). The champion isn't named, but Fanning was the publicly identified steward, and she later moved to Spectrum Brands (ZoomInfo); the date of her move isn't confirmed. Church had warned in 2007: "If you empower your customers to become your evangelists, you better be prepared to continue it... It's permanent" (MarketingProfs).
  2. Hollowing out the hosts. The Leads went from 4 or 5 to 2 (2012) to 1, a role that was "more of a community manager instead of a true lead ambassador". The blog turned into crafting and products only and "blend[ed] in to the noise" (Social Seeder). By January 2014 fiskateers.com was a polished single-author product blog (Wayback). Fiskars also closed its separate Fiskars Craft TypePad guest blog in March 2013 (Fiskars Craft).
  3. Killing the two-way spaces. In December 2013 the forums and members' galleries, which held years of members' photos and memories, were shut. What remained was "a blog (with no comments) and a Facebook page", and numbers and scissors stopped going out (Fiskafriends; Social Seeder). The consequence: the core moved to a secret, member-run Facebook group that Fiskars doesn't control or benefit from.
  4. Overstated metrics that were never followed up. A Q1 2007 store comparison got retold as "3x sales" and then "doubled profit", and Fiskars never published retention or activity data. Without hard numbers the program was vulnerable to cuts.
  5. Friction in sign-up and fulfillment. Members waited months for kits: "I signed up months ago and hadn't heard anything" (Scrapbook.com 2007). The "invitation" barrier mostly meant emailing a Lead.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give every member a permanent number and a physical token they can't buy. Sequential IDs make early joiners proud, and something they own and use keeps the membership visible for years.
  2. Put a named, paid, transparent human at the front door. Let new members pick a host and get a personal reply. Pay the hosts openly and let them talk about their lives and competitors' products.
  3. Build a ladder with term limits (featured member, then certified teacher, then elected lead, then alumni "Legend") so the most engaged people keep growing and the voice stays fresh.
  4. Make the product team visibly accountable to the community. Live chats with engineers, members naming products, and public "we changed this because you told us" moments.
  5. Pair a steady online rhythm (weekly challenges, periodic crops, sneak peeks) with member-led local events, and where you can, let partners such as retailers pay for the in-person work.

Avoid these

  1. Single-champion dependence. Write the community into budgets, KPIs and more than one executive's goals before the sponsor leaves.
  2. Turning a two-way community into a one-way content channel. Closing the forums and galleries and turning off comments kills what members came for. If you must move platforms, migrate members' archives and identity with them.
  3. Vanity proof. "3x sales in Q1 2007 stores" carried the story for years but couldn't defend the budget. Measure retention, repeat purchases and costs avoided from the start.
