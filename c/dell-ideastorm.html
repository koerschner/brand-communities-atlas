Dell IdeaStorm

Dell IdeaStorm (ideastorm.com / dellideastorm.com) · Dell

The idea portal that started the genre. Its first year was huge and changed Dell's products, but it wasted away once Dell stopped answering, and its 2012 revival died in Dell's own reorganizations

How it pays off for the company

Dell's community director claimed IdeaStorm members spent 50% more and bought 33% more often than non-members, and that ideas from the site produced 'hundreds of millions' in revenue in 2007–2011 (about $10,000 per submitted idea). TL;DR

  • What it is: a public, Digg-style suggestion site Dell launched in February 2007, about 18 months after Jeff Jarvis's "Dell Hell" posts. Anyone could post an idea, promote or demote ideas and comment, and Dell staff moved ideas through statuses from "Acknowledged" to "Implemented". It started on CrispyNews software, which became Salesforce Ideas, and it was the template for My Starbucks Idea and a whole category of corporate idea portals. Dell credits it with about 500 implemented ideas from 15,000–16,000 submitted.
  • Why people came back: to see their idea climb the vote count and get a status change or a reply from Dell. Over time the reason became the other regulars. One power user said visiting "was more about checking in on my new friends." Few others came back: fewer than 5% of users posted two or more ideas, and they wrote about 40% of all ideas.
  • What Dell fed it: Michael Dell's personal sponsorship, fast early wins announced on the Direct2Dell blog (Ubuntu PCs within about 10 weeks, no-trialware options, Windows XP brought back), a 48-hour acknowledgement promise, later Storm Sessions (time-boxed topics run by a product team), IdeaStorm 2.0 in 2012 with 28 "Idea Partners" and a Rockstar tier, and hiring its loudest critic as community manager.
  • Biggest mistake: Dell stopped talking. By about 2010 Dell had pulled away to the point that one community manager was serving a site of reportedly a million members; ideas "piled up and gathered dust" and regulars "thought Dell had become disrespectful." After a real 2011–12 revival, the social-media team was broken up in 2012 and Dell went private in 2013, and the site lingered unstaffed until it was switched off around 2018–19.
  • The one lesson: a feedback portal is a promise to answer. Its value comes from closing the loop quickly and in public, so staff it as a product pipeline with named owners inside the business units, not as a marketing campaign. If you can't keep answering, narrow it to time-boxed sessions instead of leaving a public suggestion box to rot.

What the community is

IdeaStorm was an ideas portal: members posted an idea, others promoted it (+10) or demoted it (−10), and anyone could comment. Dell staff then gave each idea a status: New → Acknowledged (within 48 hours) → Under Review → Already Offered / Partially Implemented / Implemented / Not Planned → Archived [About IdeaStorm, 2012]. You didn't need to be a Dell customer to join, and there were never material rewards: "At this time, there are no material rewards associated with IdeaStorm" [About IdeaStorm]. Ideas fell into three types: Product, Dell (company and policy) and Topic ideas [Huang, Singh & Srinivasan].

Spaces over time

  • dellideastorm.com (launched 16 Feb 2007) ran on CrispyNews, a Digg clone, "a combination between a message board and Digg.com" in Dell's words [Mashable 2011; Wayback 2007]. Salesforce bought CrispyNews in 2007 and relaunched it as Salesforce Ideas; Dell was its first outside customer [CRN 2007; HN comment by a former Salesforce PM, 2010].
  • ideastorm.com on Salesforce/force.com (from 2008). In October 2008 Dell made "a large number of material changes", and in December 2008 it stopped giving 500 points per posted idea [Huang et al.].
  • Storm Sessions (Dec 2009): Dell posts a topic, and ideas on it are taken only for a fixed window [About IdeaStorm]. Fittingly, the format itself came from a member's idea (user "brokencrystal") [Dell blog 2009, via search snippet].
  • IdeaStorm 2.0 (22 Mar 2012): new profiles, a duplicate checker, "Extensions" (the author promotes a comment into the idea), automatic archiving after six months, Idea Partners and Rockstar user levels [Direct2Dell, "Ideastorm 2.0 Launched Today!"].
  • EmployeeStorm, an internal twin: 2,700 ideas and visits from 22% of employees by Oct 2007 [Social Media Today/Forrester 2007].
  • Unofficial spaces: none of note. Dell fans gathered in Dell's own support forums. The Arctic Shift Reddit archive returned no IdeaStorm threads (the API timed out or came back empty), and Hacker News had only scattered comments.

Status: defunct. Archived home pages show the site still taking ideas in 2016–2018, but with 1–5 votes each, and the "featured" ideas never changed after 2012 (the same "Biodegradable Packing material" idea sat at 200 votes in Jan 2013 and 212 in Jan 2019) [Wayback 2013, 2016, 2018, 2019]. In December 2018 the page said "currently under maintenance", and a Dell moderator said a redesign was being handled by "another organization" with no date [Dell Community, Dec 2018]. From about April 2019 ideastorm.com redirected to Dell, and later to delltechnologies.com [Wayback CDX]. In 2024 a Dell staffer answered a user: "IdeaStorm was ended back in 2013" [Dell Community, Jan 2024]. In practice, Dell stopped staffing it around 2013 and the shell came down in 2018–19.

Origin and strategy

  • Context: Dell Hell. In 2005 Jeff Jarvis's blog campaign about Dell's support became a symbol of corporate deafness. Dell answered by starting a customer-advocate outreach team (which cut the negative share of online comments by 25%), launching the Direct2Dell blog in July 2006, and then IdeaStorm [Forrester Groundswell citation 2007; BuzzMachine 2007].
  • Champion: Michael Dell, who had just returned as CEO. At a roundtable a corporate blogger suggested the idea, and the site "launched a few months later" [Forbes 2012]. He recorded a video introduction [Guardian, Feb 2007]. Day-to-day management sat in corporate communications; Caroline Dietz was named as IdeaStorm manager in 2007 [PR Week], and Vida Killian ran it around 2008–2010 and presented it at Salesforce and PDMA events [Slideshare 2011; Bayus references].
  • Business model fit: Dell sold built-to-order PCs direct, so a site that tells you what configurations customers want feeds straight into the configurator. The About page put it in those terms: IdeaStorm extends "the direct model" by giving "a direct voice to our customers" [About IdeaStorm]. In practice it served three purposes: product feedback, reputation repair, and a public sign of listening. Mashable asked outright whether it was "more a PR exercise than anything else"; Dell's Richard Binhammer said PR goodwill was a "by-product" [Mashable 2011].
  • Later strategy (2010–12): Bill Johnston, Director of Global Online Community and a member of Dell's 13-director Social Media and Community team (SMaC), treated IdeaStorm as one of four sources of value in the hosted community, alongside support deflection, customer knowledge and advocacy. He also started the Dell Rockstars advocacy program [Johnston, Structure3C 2026].

Trajectory: a big launch spike (most votes and most implemented ideas came in the first 2.5 years [Forbes 2012]), a steady drop in new ideas that researchers measured directly [Huang et al.], a staff pullback around 2009–10, a revival in 2011–12, then a collapse after 2013. Wikipedia's "23,000 submitted" figure has no primary source I could find; Dell's own numbers stay at 15,000–16,000.

What keeps people coming back

  1. The vote count and the status change. This was the core loop. In the early CrispyNews days the 500 points per idea and ±10 per vote showed on profiles but "cannot be cashed in" [Huang et al.]. Statuses like "Under Review", and especially "Implemented" with a blog post explaining it, were the reward. Dell published every implementation on Direct2Dell, and economists treat those posts as the signal members learned from [Huang et al.].
  2. Wins that showed up fast. Preinstalled Ubuntu shipped about 10 weeks after launch. Dell said "80 percent came back and said Ubuntu" when it surveyed Linux voters [Fast Wonder 2007, quoting Dell], and Windows XP returned after a separate push [HN 2007]. The Vostro line shipped with no trialware [IdeaStorm status update linking Direct2Dell, 2009]. Early members could see that their votes moved a Fortune 50 company.
  3. Peer debate and friendship. Cy Jervis, the most active member: "I met other people who also were passionate about Dell and the path they should take as a company. We had some great debates … Over time, friendships formed and visiting the site was more about checking in on my new friends" [Forbes 2012]. This lasted even after Dell stepped back, which may be the most transferable finding here.
  4. Status tiers (late). IdeaStorm 2.0 gave top point scorers Rockstar status (the top 80 became "designated Dell Rock Stars … included in special programs and activities") and shown user levels [Forbes 2012; Direct2Dell 2012]. Rockstars get a badge, invitations to online and offline events and early looks at products; nomination is by peers or staff [Dell Community Rockstars page].
  5. Storm Sessions as a cadence. Time-boxed topics run by a named Dell owner (for example, Project Sputnik feedback until 16 Sep 2012, or an EDF sustainability session in Nov 2011) gave members a reason to return on a set date and a team they could see was listening [Dell blogs 2011–12].

What was missing: no streaks, no notifications beyond status emails, no local or in-person layer before the Rockstar era, no member-only content, and no switching cost. Idea authors got nothing material even when Dell shipped their idea. Bayus found that serial ideators who succeeded once rarely did it again; they kept proposing variations of their winning idea [Bayus 2013]. So the people most likely to have good ideas stopped producing them, and Dell had nothing to keep them engaged.

The content engine

  • Direct2Dell "Ideas in Action" posts were the loop-closing content. The 2007 site linked to an "ideas in action" page and later to a Direct2Dell category [Wayback 2007, 2008]. Posts summarized feedback on popular ideas and announced implementations, for example "More feedback from Dell on popular ideas" [CDX], and "Turning ideas into reality with IdeaStorm" (Apr 2013), which marked "At least 8GB of RAM" as Implemented and "Display Resolution >1366px" as Partially Implemented after the XPS 13 Developer Edition [search snippet, Dell blog 2013].
  • Status updates on ideas written by named staff (e.g., "Check out Vida_K's comment below on the New Latitude" on the backlit-keyboard idea) [Wayback 2011].
  • Storm Session briefs written by product teams: Project Sputnik's StormSession, together with a blog, a TechCenter forum and a beta, shaped the Ubuntu developer laptop launched in Nov 2012 [Barton George 2012; The Verge 2012].
  • Social syndication: a Twitter feed (~4,500 followers) and Facebook "promote" buttons [Mashable 2011].
  • Member-made content: the ideas and comment threads were the content. The 2012 "Extensions" feature was the only tool that let members co-write an idea; there was no curation, no featured-member program, and no templates.

Overall the content engine was thin. It was announcement-driven, ran at the pace of product launches, and stalled when staff left.

People, programs and governance

  • Staffing arc: a corporate-comms owner and engaged executives at launch (Michael Dell occasionally commented on ideas [BuzzMachine 2007]); by 2010 "only one community manager … to a constituency that once was over a million members strong" [Forbes 2012]. Johnston: "The process for getting ideas back into the company had become convoluted, and it wasn't clear who was on point to fix it" [Forbes 2012].
  • Critic-turned-community-manager: in early 2011 Cy Jervis, a 911-dispatch supervisor and IdeaStorm's "most active and vocal community member", posted a challenge saying the site would work better if Dell joined the conversation. Michael Dell agreed internally, and Johnston hired Jervis first as a consultant, then as community manager (Feb 2011) [Forbes 2012; Mashable 2011; Prattle & Jaw 2011].
  • Idea Partners (2012): more Dell staff plus 28 partner companies, so ideas reached the right owner in a company of 110,000 people "so that good ideas don't die of old age" [Forbes 2012].
  • Moderation: ideas were not pre-moderated; a profanity filter, takedowns after posting, and archiving after 6 months (2012) or a year (earlier) [About IdeaStorm; Huang et al.].
  • Internal governance (2010–12): SMaC's 13 directors each partnered with a business unit, sent weekly impact newsletters to VPs, ran certification for employees who wanted to post, and kept an escalation path for them. In 2012 the company "exhaled": the directors were sent out into business units, and new marketing executives were "more skeptical … and uncomfortable with the lack of control" [Johnston 2026]. Dell went private in 2013 and merged with EMC in 2016. Johnston's verdict: "I don't think anyone in that building made a decision to abandon social business. I think it stopped being the most important thing in the room, and then it stopped being in the room" [Johnston 2026].

Growth loops

  • Earned media was the main acquisition channel. The Guardian, Forbes, InfoWorld, BusinessWeek and the Forrester award all covered it in 2007. The Linux campaign was organized from outside: Linux fans mobilized ("like teenage girls voting for Sanjaya on American Idol," Forbes sneered [Forbes 2007]), which brought in a large, motivated group of outsiders but skewed the agenda toward a niche.
  • Promote-to-Facebook buttons (2010) and Twitter came late and drove little measurable growth [Mashable 2011].
  • Platform diffusion rather than member growth: Michael Dell shared the experience with Howard Schultz, and Salesforce built My Starbucks Idea on the same product [BuzzMachine 2008]. Best Buy IdeaX, Obama's Citizen's Briefing Book and others followed [Mashable 2011; HN 2010]. IdeaStorm's biggest outside effect was creating a software category, not a self-growing community.
  • No referral, SEO or ambassador loop until the Rockstars, and by then it was too late.

Evidence of business impact

Hard-ish evidence (third party):

  • Product changes are real and documented: Ubuntu PCs (May 2007), Windows XP brought back, no-trialware Vostro, backlit keyboards ("Partially Implemented"), the (RED) partnership, the XPS 13 Developer Edition (Nov 2012; the line ran for more than 10 years) [The Register; HN; Wayback; Mashable; Barton George].
  • The Ubuntu line sold "close to 40,000 systems" in about six months by trade-press sources, against roughly 10M PCs a quarter for Dell; Dell declined to confirm [The Register, Nov 2007]. In other words, 130,000 votes did not turn into anything like 130,000 buyers.
  • Academic studies of the full IdeaStorm data: about 2–3% of ideas implemented [Huang et al.; Hossain & Islam 2015]. Peer voting predicts implementation, contributors with weak ideas drop out over time, and slow or missing replies discourage contributors [Huang et al.]. Serial ideators are more likely to land an implemented idea but rarely repeat [Bayus 2013, Management Science]. Di Gangi, Wasko and Hooker (2010) list four problems from the first 18 months: understanding ideas, picking the best ones, transparency versus secrecy from competitors, and keeping the community going.

Company claims (not audited, never in SEC filings):

  • IdeaStorm members "spent 50% more than non-members" and their "purchase frequency was 33% higher"; ideas "created $100's of millions of dollars in revenue in the period between 2007–2011" [Johnston, Medium 2018 and 2022]. This is a correlation: heavy Dell buyers are the people most likely to join, so it says little about cause.
  • A 2011 study traced five ideas the product teams agreed came "solely" from IdeaStorm, compared their revenue with the program's cost, and spread that revenue over every idea ever submitted to get "approximately ten thousand dollars" per idea [Johnston 2026]. That is an "elevator stat", by his own description.
  • Forrester's Groundswell "Company Transformation" award (Oct 2007) recognized IdeaStorm together with Direct2Dell and the advocate team [Forrester; Social Media Today].

Score justification: business_impact_evidence 3: real shipped products and two peer-reviewed datasets, but the revenue and LTV figures are one executive's correlational claims.

What they did right

  1. Went first and in public, with CEO backing. In 2007 inviting public criticism was bold; Michael Dell's sponsorship gave it reach and attention in the press [Forbes 2012; Guardian 2007].
  2. Acted on the top ideas fast and said so. Shipping Ubuntu and no-trialware options in months, and announcing each on Direct2Dell, made voting feel worthwhile and earned the 2007 award [The Register; Forrester].
  3. Published a clear status lifecycle with a 48-hour acknowledgement promise. Members could see where their idea stood [About IdeaStorm].
  4. Time-boxed Storm Sessions owned by product teams. Project Sputnik shows the model at its best: a named owner, a deadline, a beta, and a product that shipped and lasted a decade [Barton George 2012, 2025].
  5. Turned the loudest critic into staff. Hiring Jervis signaled respect and brought a member's view into the design; customer participation "has risen each month for the last three months" after the relaunch [Forbes 2012].
  6. Tried to measure value in money. Few community teams tied ideas to revenue in 2011, even roughly [Johnston 2026].

What they did wrong

  1. Stopped answering. Within about 2.5 years implementations dried up and staff "drifted off"; members complained Dell had "NOT responded in so MANY areas" and the site was "frankly, stagnant" [Forbes 2012; Huang et al. quoting members]. Consequence: advocates turned into critics, "on the verge of revolt" [Forbes 2012].
  2. A suggestion box with no route into the business. "Convoluted" routing with nobody "on point" meant even top-voted ideas sat for years. "Standardize Power Cables for Laptops" (21,050 points) stayed "Under Review" [Wayback 2008], and later users said Dell's answer was "we aren't changing, this is a business decision" [HN 2013]. Consequence: the most visible ideas became public evidence that Dell didn't listen.
  3. Let it become a complaint board. Many ideas were support grievances (BIOS RAM limits, touchpads, Wi-Fi cards) [Wayback 2013]. Analysts warned about "value co-destruction" when customers flood a co-creation site with complaints [Consumer Value Creation 2016].
  4. Let voting blocs drive the agenda. Linux fans dominated the leaderboard. Dell responded, but sales were small; Forbes called it "Dell's Linux Problem" [Forbes 2007; The Register]. In the Sputnik session, Dell ignored the top request for higher resolution at launch, and HN users noticed [HN 2012].
  5. Wiped out its own status system. Dropping per-idea points in Dec 2008 and auto-archiving unanswered ideas after six months (2012) cleared the backlog but also erased the record members had built [Huang et al.; Direct2Dell 2012].
  6. Tied the revival to an org chart that lasted two years. SMaC was designed to break up after two years. When it did in 2012 and the company went private, no owner was left [Johnston 2026]. Consequence: a zombie site for five years (frozen featured ideas, a counter stuck at "nearly 500"), then a silent shutdown with no announcement or archive. Dell's own staff later gave the end date as 2013 [Dell Community 2024].
  7. No reward for the people whose ideas Dell used. No credit beyond a status change [About IdeaStorm]. Researchers concluded Dell should "reward individuals only when their ideas are implemented" and "identify good idea contributors and respond quickly to their ideas" [Huang et al.].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Close the loop in public, quickly, every time it matters. Statuses, a promised response time, and a named staff reply turn a vote into a relationship. Dell's best period was exactly when it did this.
  2. Run time-boxed, owner-led sessions instead of an endless open box. A product manager asks a specific question, sets a deadline, ships, and reports back (Storm Sessions, Project Sputnik). This is cheaper to staff and produces better ideas.
  3. Give every idea a route to a named owner inside the business. Idea Partners, and SMaC's director-to-business-unit pairings, are the parts worth copying [Forbes 2012; Johnston 2026].
  4. Recruit your sharpest critics. Bring the most vocal power user inside, paid or as an advisor, to rebuild trust.
  5. Invest in the relationships between members, not only member-to-company. The friendships among regulars kept the site alive after Dell left. Design for peer debate (comments, profiles, extensions), not just ballots.

Avoid these

  1. Launching a public suggestion box you can't staff for years. Unanswered ideas are a public record of not listening; it is better to narrow or close with notice than to let the site waste away.
  2. Treating vote totals as demand. Organized minorities can top the leaderboard (130,000 Linux votes ≈ 40,000 sales). Check ideas against sales data before committing.
  3. Tying the program to one executive sponsor or a temporary team. Put ownership in a durable product or support function with a budget line, or it will lose out to the next reorganization.

Related communities worth adding

  • Salesforce IdeaExchange: Salesforce: the in-house original of the Ideas product Dell launched on; still running as part of Trailblazer; a durable comparison for idea portals.
  • Best Buy IdeaX: Best Buy: 2008–2010s idea portal with open-sourced code; a copycat that faded.
  • Yahoo! Suggestion Board: Yahoo: launched the day before IdeaStorm on the same Digg model; short-lived.
  • Dell Community Rockstars and Dell TechCenter: Dell: the advocate/MVP program and enterprise community that outlived IdeaStorm.
  • Dell EmployeeStorm: Dell: internal idea portal (2,700 ideas, 22% of staff by 2007); useful as an internal co-creation case.
  • Autodesk Expert Elite: Autodesk: advocate program modeled on Dell Rockstars (Groundswell award 2015), still running; a durable success.
  • Citizen's Briefing Book (Obama transition, 2009): US government: political copy of IdeaStorm.
  • Lenovo / HP idea portals and Microsoft UserVoice-era feedback boards: the corporate feedback-forum category that IdeaStorm started.
