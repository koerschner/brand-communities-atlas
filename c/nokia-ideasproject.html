TL;DR

  • What it was: ideasproject.com went through two phases. From February 2009 it was a Nokia marketing content site of "big thinkers" (Chris Anderson, Jimmy Wales, Charlene Li) answering questions about the future of communications. At SXSW in March 2011 it relaunched as an open idea-crowdsourcing community: anyone 18+ could post app or product ideas, comment, "like" them and enter themed challenges. It reached about 14,000 members by December 2011 and 20,000+ by September 2012, and went dark between late October 2013 and 30 January 2014.
  • Why people came back: almost entirely the challenge calendar and its prizes: new Nokia phones, trips to Nokia World and Mobile World Congress, and $20,000 cash in the World Bank-backed m2Work challenge. Nokia's own head of crowdsourcing said "small tangible rewards such as Nokia's newest mobile devices have motivated people more than any other reward." Between challenges, the open "Idea Space" was largely silent: in 2013 most new ideas showed 0 likes and 0 comments.
  • What Nokia fed it: a small dedicated team (six named staff in 2012), expert video and article content, big-name partners (UNESCO, the World Bank's infoDev, Earth Hour, the DLD conference, Imagine Cup), Nokia executives as judges, and hackathons that promised to turn winning ideas into apps.
  • The biggest mistake: promising implementation and not delivering it. In October 2011 Nokia told 11 winners their ideas would be "the first ones ever to be implemented from IdeasProject ideas" at a Nokia World hackathon. Sixteen months later one winner wrote that none of the 11 apps had shipped and that Nokia had turned down a developer who offered to build them. On top of that, the terms of service gave Nokia a perpetual, royalty-free licence to everything "without compensation… acknowledgment of source", and the site's purpose drifted from "apps that change the world" to Lumia accessory giveaways.
  • The one lesson: an idea portal is a promise. Only promise what your organization will actually build, show members the status of their ideas, and close the loop on the few ideas you pick. Prizes buy bursts of submissions, not a community.

What the community is

Phase 1: the "big thinkers" site (Feb 2009 to about 2010). Nokia launched ideasproject.com in early February 2009 as "an online information space designed to help people find and understand the connections between thought leaders and their big ideas" (i2fly, Feb 2009). Experts included Wired's Chris Anderson, XPRIZE's Peter Diamandis and Nokia's Maemo VP Ari Jaaksi, and users could "contribute ideas" while IdeasProject editors highlighted the best (Nokia Conversations, 2 Feb 2009). The site was built by an agency (Communication Arts credits Axis41) and won a Communication Arts Webpick in April 2009. A 2009 critic noted that the talent roster came from Monitor Talent and the site was "sponsored by Nokia and powered by Xigi" (Identity Woman, 29 Jul 2009). By mid-2010 its centrepiece was a weekly Q&A: "Each week, a visiting expert will ask a question of the community… with the best answers showcased in a blog post at the beginning of the next week" (Nokia Conversations, 30 Jun 2010).

Phase 2: the idea-crowdsourcing community (Mar 2011 to about Jan 2014). Relaunched at SXSW on 13 March 2011 as "a web-based, innovation community where application enthusiasts (consumers, developers, anyone) can join, share and collaboratively create applications for mobile platforms." Participants could "create or respond to innovation 'challenges'." The first challenge was "Apps That Change the World", and "Based on the best ideas shared, Nokia will produce a free application that will be distributed on the Ovi Store" (Nokia Conversations, 13 Mar 2011). The site had two modes: time-boxed challenges (often co-hosted with partners) and a 24/7 open Idea Space. It ran in English and, from about mid-2011, Chinese, with a mobile site, and members could log in with Ovi or Facebook (Wayback, Jan 2012). The 2011 site ran on Jive. A new site (Liferay-style portlet URLs) opened around September 2012 (Wayback, Sep 2012).

Unofficial spaces: none of note. I found no Reddit threads (an Arctic Shift search for "ideasproject" returned nothing) and no Hacker News discussion. Coverage came from Nokia fan blogs (My Nokia Blog, Install or Not) and innovation-management bloggers.

Status: defunct. The homepage showed live ideas on 26 October 2013. By 30 January 2014 it read "This site is no longer active. You can continue to engage with Nokia by following the links below… © 2014 Nokia" (Wayback 2013-10-26; Wayback 2014-01-30). That timing falls between Microsoft's September 2013 agreement to buy Nokia's phone business and the deal's close in April 2014. I found no public closure announcement and no data export for members.

Origin and strategy

  • Phase 1 was brand marketing. Its champion was Valerie Buckingham, Nokia's "Director of Technology Marketing": "IdeasProject visitors can see new ideas as they surface and be able to track the connection between ideas and the thought leaders that contributed them" (i2fly, 2009). In 2009 she signed a reply as "Forum Nokia Developer Communities" (Identity Woman comments).
  • Phase 2 was catch-up crowdsourcing for an app ecosystem. Nokia was losing to Apple's App Store. Its 2009–2010 Calling All Innovators app contest (whose 2010 winners included Angry Birds) ended, "leaving room for another platform called Ideas Project", which Yannig Roth called "the Finnish equivalent for Dell's Ideastorm" (Roth, Jan 2012). The phase-2 lead, Pia Erkinheimo (Head of Idea Crowdsourcing, later Head of Crowdsourcing, Concepting & Innovation), traced it to January 2008, when "Nokia started utilizing the 'wisdom of the crowds', i.e. internal idea crowdsourcing" (Nokia Conversations interview, 25 Dec 2011).
  • The stated philosophy was "democratized innovation" and open innovation in both directions: "ideas developed within the company that are not being used should be shared freely with the public, e.g. with application developers and start-ups." Ideas "do not expire," so the site would act as "a treasury for ideas" (About page, Jan 2012). Patentable inventions were routed to a separate, paid programme, Invent with Nokia (same page; My Nokia Blog, May 2011).
  • The business model link was indirect. Ideas were supposed to flow to Forum Nokia developers ("Ideas for applications initiated on IdeasProject can be further developed in the Forum Nokia context") and to Nokia business owners who set up challenges, "evaluating the results, and rewarding the best ideators" (FAQ, Dec 2011). In mid-2012 the site moved into Nokia's developer organization "in an effort to bring Ideas closer to the developers," per its new head (Install or Not, 27 Feb 2013). In 2013 some DVLUP developer challenges were seeded from IdeasProject ideas (Nokia Conversations, 25 Mar 2013).
  • On my reading of the evidence (an inference), phase 2 also did CSR and partnership work. Many challenges were built with NGOs and development agencies (UNESCO Education for All, World Bank infoDev m2Work, Earth Hour, Open Innovation Africa Summit), which gave Nokia reputational value in emerging markets where it still sold most of its phones.

Trajectory: membership grew from 0 to 14,000 in nine months, then added only about 6,000 more in the following nine, even with prize promotions. Activity was spiky and challenge-driven: big partner challenges drew hundreds of likes per top idea (m2Work), while the open Idea Space produced unread one-off posts. The cumulative member count stayed small for a brand that sold hundreds of millions of phones a year, and most surveyed members were new and infrequent visitors. I found no data after September 2012. The peak was probably 2012, and the site wound down through 2013.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Challenges as the cadence. By January 2012 the site was running about a dozen parallel challenges, including Designing Privacy, Future Applications for Development, three monthly UNESCO Education for All sub-challenges ("Each month will feature a separate EFA goal") and Chinese-language versions (Wayback Jan 2012; UNESCO archive). Completed challenges by October 2011 included Apps for Change, Create 4 Millions, DLDwomen, Earth Hour, Global Forum, Nokia Power (battery life), Wellness Diary and the N9 UI challenge (Wayback Oct 2011). Each had a deadline, named hosts, a jury and prizes. That gave members a reason to return, but only when a new challenge launched.
  2. Hardware and travel prizes, the explicit lever. Prizes included ten Nokia N9s for UI feedback, judged by design VPs Nikki Barton and Peter Skillman (My Nokia Blog, 12 Oct 2011), a Nokia C7 for the Wellness Diary challenge (mHealth Insight, May 2011), dual-SIM phones plus a paid trip to Nokia World London (Create 4 Millions), trips to MWC 2013 for a Lumia 820 3D-printed case design (3ders, 21 Jan 2013), headphones and speakers for the best Windows Phone Start screen (ceeesquared, Apr 2013), and $20,000 cash in m2Work (World Bank blog). Erkinheimo: "small tangible rewards such as Nokia's newest mobile devices have motivated people more than any other reward" (quoted by Roth, 2012). Even the 20,000-member milestone was celebrated with a "like us on Facebook to win a Lumia 800" draw (Wayback).
  3. Light social feedback. Members could like, comment on, vote on and tag ideas, form a group to "Collaborate on this idea," sort by Most Discussed or Highest Score, and browse an idea visualization (FAQ). In challenges this worked: the top m2Work ideas collected 366–800 likes and 24–61 comments (Wayback 2013). Outside challenges it didn't (see the table).
  4. Status labels, without a loop. Reviewers could move an idea through Take part → Work in progress → Put into action → Thank you ("Thank you" meant "reviewed and did not lead to immediate action") (FAQ). By 2012–13 the visible stages were "Inspiration" and "Appreciated" (Wayback 2012; 2013). I found no badges, points, levels, streaks, leaderboards, member titles or superuser programme.
  5. What members actually valued. In a 2012 survey of 244 Chinese-site members, the significant drivers of knowledge sharing were social benefits (β=.273) and learning benefits (β=.225), plus weaker recognition from Nokia (β=.143, p<.10). Recognition from peers and fun had no significant effect. The authors concluded that "material rewards are not quintessential here, and in fact may even become counterproductive," while "'soft' rewards such as granting time, attention and taking care of a contributor can be productive," and they called for "shorter response times regarding user input" (Kosonen et al., IJIM 2014). Nokia's strongest lever (devices) and its members' stated motives (learning, connection, being heard by Nokia) did not line up.
  6. No switching costs. Members kept ownership of their ideas and were told they were "free to take [their] idea somewhere else" (FAQ). Nothing tied an idea to a Nokia phone, so nothing locked members in.

Score: retention_mechanics 2/5. A real challenge cadence and generous prizes, but no status system, no peer-recognition layer and no visible feedback loop. Most members visited less than monthly.

The content engine

  • Expert content (2009–2012). Phase 1 was mostly content: videos, articles, podcasts and "dynamic maps" connecting thinkers' ideas (Minnov8, Apr 2009), and a weekly expert question (Nokia Conversations, 2010). In December 2010 Nokia ran an "IdeasCamp" and published 30-second "idea" videos from people such as Philip Rosedale (YouTube). Phase 2 kept "Expert Insights" and "Inspiration" sections with articles and transcripts from Microsoft, Google, UNESCO and academic experts, tied to the current challenge (Expert Insights, Dec 2011; Inspiration, Jan 2012).
  • How-to guidance. The FAQ taught members to write a good idea (problem, solution, benefits, challenges, images, tags), to check for duplicates, and to promote ideas on social media, YouTube and Flickr (FAQ). By 2013, after developer feedback, staff were telling ideators to add "screen shots and prototype detail" (Install or Not). That raised the bar on a site built for quick ideas.
  • Thought leadership. Nokia commissioned a whitepaper, The Promise of Idea Crowdsourcing (Aitamurto, Leiponen and Tee, June 2011), "brought to you by www.ideasproject.com" (ResearchGate), and opened the site to academic surveys (Kosonen et al.).
  • Member content. Members contributed ideas (text, sketches, videos) and comments. Curation meant staff blog posts featuring standout ideas (the Chinese team's editor picked favourites, for example) and partner juries.

Score: content_engine 3/5. Better than most idea portals, with real expert content and partner-hosted themes. But the content was mostly marketing-grade inspiration rather than help that made members' ideas buildable, and it thinned out after 2012.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: about six people. The 2012 About page signs off "Pia, Heli, Fatih, Rupesh, Juha, Shamayel" (About). Named roles included Pia Erkinheimo (head and "Co-founder of IdeasProject Challenge," Tekedia), Heli Haapkylä (Manager, Crowdsourcing; My Nokia Blog), community manager Karoliina Harjanne (2011; mHealth Insight) and a Chinese community manager who ran a Weibo-style microblog (Kosonen et al.). Leadership changed in mid-2012, when a new head inside the developer organization took over (Install or Not).
  • Challenge governance: "business owners [are] responsible for setting up challenges, executing them, evaluating the results, and rewarding the best ideators," plus a central review team (FAQ). Juries were high-profile: Mozilla's Mitchell Baker and Abigail Disney for Apps to Empower Women (Tekedia), and the World Bank's CIO for m2Work (World Bank). Erkinheimo: "the best solution is to combine the wisdom of the crowds and the wisdom of the jury" (Roth).
  • Moderation: "moderated by the community and by the core IdeasProject team"; the rules were no spam or flaming, stay relevant, and "Don't steal ideas" (About). The FAQ admitted that "there's no way of preventing" credit theft.
  • IP terms: the FAQ said "You own the ideas," but the terms of service granted Nokia a "non-exclusive, royalty-free… perpetual, worldwide, irrevocable, and fully sublicensable right" to use any submission "without compensation, restrictions on use, acknowledgment of source, accountability, or liability, and with waiver of all moral rights." They also let Nokia change or withdraw prizes "at any time without notice" (quoted by mHealth Insight, 30 May 2011).
  • Superusers / ambassadors: none found. Unlike Beta Labs (Contributor of the Month, badges, member moderators) or Forum Nokia (Champions), IdeasProject never built a recognized-member tier.
  • Feedback into product: the official path ran to Forum Nokia/Nokia Developer, hackathons, AppCampus and DVLUP. Documented outputs were thin (see below).

Score: member_ownership 2/5 (members could create groups and comment, but Nokia set every agenda and kept the IP rights). Score: staff_investment 3/5 (a dedicated team, executive judges, global partners and expensive travel prizes, but too few builders on the far side of the funnel and churn in the leadership).

Growth loops

  • Partner challenges as acquisition. Co-hosts brought their own audiences: the World Bank and UK DFID publicized m2Work (GOV.UK, Feb 2012); UNESCO published the EFA challenges (UNESCO); DLD Women launched Apps to Empower Women (Tekedia); Microsoft's Imagine Cup was pointed at the site as an "idea treasury for students" (Nokia Conversations). Kenyan tech blogs promoted the Open Innovation Africa challenge (KachTech, Apr 2012). The member base skewed toward emerging markets (204–209 countries; m2Work entries 96% from developing economies).
  • Owned media: Nokia Conversations posts, the @IdeasProject Twitter account, a Facebook page, and promotion in Nokia fan blogs.
  • Social sharing of ideas: the FAQ told members to promote their own ideas to raise their odds (FAQ). Like-voting rewarded whoever recruited the most friends.
  • What never formed: an outward loop from shipped products ("this app came from IdeasProject") back into sign-ups. Few ideas shipped, so there was nothing to show.

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence (thin):

  • One documented app from an idea: "Zoom Out – Children's Magic Book," built by developers Tomasz Mucha and Siva Kumar from an idea in the Primary Education challenge (Inspiration page, Jan 2012).
  • m2Work produced a $20,000 winner (Aadhar Bhalinge's Smart Rickshaw Network) and fed a September 2012 hackathon at infoDev mLabs in five countries (Hindu BusinessLine; mLab ECA). The value there went to infoDev's development goals, not to Nokia's revenue.
  • A designer lists himself as "Winner of the Nokia Lumia 820 3D printing challenge hosted by the Ideasproject" (Coroflot), and a student's "Mission for the Week" idea won the UNESCO EFA grand prize (Harkirat Singh). These count as outcomes for members, not for Nokia's products.

Company claims: "So far, the most tangible achievements can be found from application stores" (Erkinheimo, Dec 2011, with no numbers; Nokia Conversations).

Counter-evidence: the 11 Nokia World 2011 hackathon ideas billed as "the first ones ever to be implemented" had not shipped by February 2013, according to one of the winners, who said he had spoken to most of the others (Install or Not). That is one member's account, not independently verified. Nokia's own reply in it concedes that "The developer was incented to publish the app, but has not done so yet." A 2023 innovation-consultancy blog lists IdeasProject as a crowdsourcing failure ("few were actually developed or brought to market"), but it gets the launch year wrong (2008) and cites no sources, so I treat it as weak (Braden Kelley guest post).

No retention, revenue, NPS or cost figures exist in Nokia filings or press for IdeasProject. Score: business_impact_evidence 1/5. Score: durability 1/5: under five years across two different concepts, and the community phase lasted under three years before being switched off with no archive for members.

What they did right

  1. Themed, time-boxed challenges with credible partners. Focused prompts (battery life, privacy, microwork, education) produced far more engagement than the open Idea Space. m2Work drew 939 ideas in about two months, and its top ideas drew hundreds of likes and dozens of comments (Hindu BusinessLine; Wayback).
  2. Visible executive involvement. Design VPs judged the N9 challenge and "The best ideas will be reviewed by Nokia user interface designers" (My Nokia Blog). Members valued recognition from Nokia itself (Kosonen et al.).
  3. Clear contributor guidance and an honest FAQ. A template for a good idea, duplicate detection, plain answers ("Am I free to take my idea somewhere else? Yes you are") and a named stage system (FAQ).
  4. Localization and emerging-market reach. Chinese-language challenges, a mobile site and Ovi/Facebook login met Nokia's actual customers where they were (Wayback Jan 2012).
  5. It studied itself. It commissioned research and allowed independent academic surveys, which is why we know members wanted learning, connection and attention more than devices.

What they did wrong

  1. Overpromised implementation. "Nokia will produce a free application" (March 2011) and "your ideas will be the first ones ever to be implemented" (October 2011) set expectations that the hackathon model, where volunteer developers choose what to build, could not guarantee. Consequence: public, named backlash from a former superfan: "the ideas never materialize" (Install or Not, Feb 2013).
  2. Slow, shifting follow-up. It took seven months to answer "Any progress?" with "Apparently the app is not yet ready," and then the rules changed: ideas would now need screenshots and prototypes to interest hackathon developers. The same account says IdeasProject declined a Nokia Developer Champion's November 2011 offer to build the apps himself. Consequence: the community's best members became its loudest critics.
  3. IP terms that contradicted the ownership message. The FAQ said "You own the ideas," but the terms of service took perpetual rights with no attribution. Consequence: expert outsiders called it "focused too much on the inward transfer of innovations… this obscure side show" (mHealth Insight, May 2011).
  4. Bribing with devices instead of building a community. Nokia's head called devices the top motivator, while its own members' survey pointed to learning and social benefits. Prize-driven challenges produced bursts. The open Idea Space in 2013 showed post after post with 0 likes and 0 comments (Wayback May 2013).
  5. Mission drift. It went from "apps that change the world" (2011) to "show us your Windows Phone Start screen" for headphones (2013) (ceeesquared). As Nokia bet the company on Lumia, the portal turned into a marketing contest page.
  6. Platform and org churn. There were two different concepts on one domain (2009 vs 2011), a re-platforming (Jive to a new site, about September 2012), and a move into the developer organization (mid-2012) inside a company going through the 2011 Windows Phone pivot and mass layoffs. Idea URLs changed between platforms (e.g. /ideas/10999 in 2011 versus /idea/-/ideas/634460 later).
  7. A silent shutdown. I found no announcement, no archive and no export. Thousands of ideas, which the About page had called a permanent "treasury" because "ideas do not expire," were simply switched off by January 2014.
  8. An early diversity misstep (phase 1). Its 2009 "big thinkers" roster was 45 men and 5 women. Nokia replied that it had "just interviewed two female new Ideators" and would "make it a priority" (Identity Woman). Two years later the Apps to Empower Women challenge followed.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Run focused, deadline-bound challenges with a named owner and jury. IdeasProject's activity came almost entirely from themed challenges. Give each one a business owner who has committed to act on the result.
  2. Put real decision-makers in front of members. Executive judges and "reviewed by our designers" promises drove participation, and research on this very community found recognition from the company mattered more than peer recognition.
  3. Publish a "how to write a good idea" template and duplicate detection so submissions arrive buildable. Do it from day one rather than raising the bar later.
  4. Partner with organizations whose audiences you want. The World Bank, UNESCO and DLD brought credibility and reach Nokia couldn't buy with ads.
  5. Invest in "soft rewards": response time, feature posts, and staff attention to individual contributors. These are cheaper than phones and closer to what members said they wanted.

Avoid these

  1. Don't promise implementation you don't control. If shipping depends on volunteer hackathon developers, say "we'll pitch it to developers," not "your idea will be the first implemented." Track and publish every winning idea's status until it ships or is formally closed.
  2. Don't let IP terms contradict your ownership message. "You own it" plus a perpetual, unattributed licence reads as extraction. Promise attribution at minimum.
  3. Don't treat prizes as the community. Device giveaways produce entries, not relationships. Without status, peer ties or visible impact, the open space goes quiet between contests, and the community dies when the budget or the company changes. If you shut it down, announce it and give members their ideas back.

Open questions

  • The exact closure date and whether any announcement went to members. The Wayback Machine has no capture between 26 Oct 2013 and 30 Jan 2014.
  • Peak membership after September 2012 and total idea count at closure.
  • How many IdeasProject ideas became shipped apps in total. Zoom Out is the only one I could verify; the Chinese "穿衣搭配" (outfit matching) winner was promised as an Ovi Store app, and I found no evidence it shipped.
  • The coordinator's note says Nokia Beta Labs referred members to IdeasProject. The Beta Labs report shows Beta Labs routing app ideas to Symbian Ideas (2010). I found no direct Beta Labs → IdeasProject referral.
  • Budget, team size after 2012, and who ordered the shutdown (Nokia before the Microsoft close, or a transition decision).

Related communities worth adding

  • Calling All Innovators — Nokia — 2009–2010 global app-developer contest (Angry Birds among the 2010 winners); IdeasProject's predecessor; ended.
  • Invent with Nokia — Nokia — patent-oriented invention submission programme with revenue share (2011–); now closed to new registrations; a contrast case for paid co-creation.
  • Symbian Ideas — Symbian Foundation — open idea portal Beta Labs pointed members to; defunct with the foundation.
  • AppCampus — Nokia, Microsoft and Aalto University — 2012–2014 app accelerator that IdeasProject leaned on to build ideas; ended.
  • Atizo — independent (Switzerland) — third-party idea community studied side by side with IdeasProject in the research literature; useful as the "hosted by an intermediary" comparison.
  • eYeka — independent co-creation community that ran Nokia N9 video contests; a creator-contest model.
  • AudioDraft Nokia Tune Remake — Nokia/AudioDraft — 2011 crowdsourced ringtone remix contest whose winner shipped on Nokia phones; a rare co-creation output that reached the product.
  • NokiaPartners Open Communities (open-ecosystem.org) — Nokia Networks — current B2B co-creation community; shows the idea outliving the phone business.
  • My Starbucks Idea and Dell IdeaStorm — already in the dataroom; IdeasProject's direct models.
