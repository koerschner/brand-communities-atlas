TL;DR

  • What it is. Meh.com is the one-deal-a-day store that Woot's founder Matt Rutledge and much of Woot's original crew launched in July 2014, after Amazon bought Woot. Every deal page is also a forum page. It carries a daily poll, a video slot, live sales stats and a "Meh" button you click to not buy. The forum, built before the store, is where regulars ("mehtizens") spend their time (TechCrunch 2013; Business Insider 2014).
  • Why people come back. It's a nightly ritual: a new deal at midnight Eastern, the Meh-button streak calendar, the daily poll, and running bits like the monthly Scapegoat, mystery boxes (Fukubukuro, the Instant Regret Kit or "IRK") and all-day Meh-rathons. Rutledge's premise was "a store that you don't need to buy anything from to have fun."
  • What the company feeds it. Funny, honest product write-ups (lately serialized fiction), staff who post in threads, livestreams and videos in the early years, public "OHSHIT REPORT" post-mortems, transparent daily stats, member-run traditions that the company adopts, and since 2024 the Shoddy Goods newsletter by ex-Woot writer Jason Toon.
  • Biggest mistake. The loyal core never brought casual buyers with it. Rutledge admitted that "thousands of core fans had followed us… but hundreds of thousands of casual customers had not heard." The company closed its VMP membership to new members in 2017 and shifted its growth to TV-driven sister stores (MorningSave, SideDeal), which members say "keeps the lights on at meh" (Membership Revisited, 2018). Since then, community investment has thinned: fewer videos and Meh-rathons, volunteer moderators stepped down "with the overall slow decline in participation," and price rises and a corporate rebrand to Stores.com followed.
  • The lesson. Make not buying a rewarded, daily, social act, and you get extraordinary loyalty: 94–96% of visits are people typing meh.com directly. But loyalty is not acquisition. You need a separate engine for new customers, or the community slowly ages in place.

What the community is

Meh sells one item, steeply discounted, each day until midnight Eastern. Visitors can "buy it" or click the "Meh" button for "an even more satisfying shopping experience: abstention" (Meh FAQ). Each deal and each daily poll automatically gets a forum topic, posted by the house bot @mediocrebot. The forum also has free-form topics, video topics and recurring community threads (forum). The home page shows live stats: visitors, the phone share, how many people clicked meh, where they came from, units sold and revenue. On 7 November 2026: 113,496 visitors, 2,200 meh clicks, 94% "just typed meh.com", 374 units sold, $6,828 (meh.com, accessed 2026-11-08).

Official spaces: the meh.com forum (the hub); the deal, poll and video threads; the membership program (VMP → Membership → Stores.com Membership); event formats (Meh-rathons, Fukubukuro/Fukobukuro bags, IRKs, birthday games); the Shoddy Goods newsletter (Substack); YouTube @MediocreMeh (4.7K subscribers). Sister forums sit on the same software: mediocre.com (2013, the original lab forum) and Casemates, the wine site that absorbed Wine.Woot's community in 2018 (Casemates).

Unofficial spaces are tiny, because the forum is the community. r/mehdotcom has 67 subscribers and 66 posts since January 2015 (Arctic Shift). Member-built tools mattered more: MehStalker, a deal tracker with an Android app by member @lichme (2015–Sept 2024, retired: "no longer a reliable source", RIP Mehstalker), a Kotlin Android client (Show HN 2015), and an unofficial Chrome extension.

Status today: active daily, but much smaller and older than at its peak (see the data below). The corporate parent was renamed Mercatalyst around 2019–2020 and Stores.com, Inc. in January 2026. It now runs nine stores, including the relaunched Hammacher Schlemmer, which it bought in March 2026 (Stores.com press release; StockTitan/PRNewswire).

Origin and strategy

  • A community re-founded after an acquisition. Rutledge sold Woot to Amazon in 2010 for a reported $110M and left in 2012. Within Amazon, he said, "the goal-based tactical needs of Amazon were unable to appreciate a relatively small artful business model" (Wikipedia). In September 2013 he regrouped the Woot founding team as "A Mediocre Corporation": brother Dave Rutledge (creative), Shawn Miller (lead developer), Derek Chapin (CFO) and Luke Duff (IT). "The first thing that the company will build is a forum," before any store (TechCrunch).
  • Community before commerce. Before launch, Mediocre sent people $20 Amazon gift cards and invited them to buy something for the new office. About 75% did, spending more than half of the $20,000 outlay (D Magazine 2014, via search snippet). A Kickstarter ("The Classic Daily Deal Site") raised $14,777 from 1,413 backers against a $10,000 goal. Backers got Day Zero access, a day before everyone else, and a permanent forum badge (Bustle 2014; Kickstarter update).
  • Stated strategy. "Everything is centered around our sense of humor." "We're building a store that you don't need to buy anything from to have fun." "The community is really the most important part… if you let that community down, that's your fault" (Rutledge, BI July 2014). At launch Meh deliberately had "no automatic emails or social media presence — it's just the site, the product, a poll, and a video." The team was about 35 people.
  • Measuring loyalty. Rutledge said that at Woot "we lacked metrics that would have helped define and track fan loyalty… In Amazon's culture you're nothing without metrics." So the Very Mediocre Person ($5/month VMP) program "was to be both a customer benefit and a health metric for us" (Membership Revisited, May 2018). The Meh button does the same job for engagement: every day's abstentions are counted and shown publicly.
  • Business model. Closeout and refurb inventory cleared by an audience that shows up on its own. In year one Meh was only 29.71% of Mediocre's revenue; staff noted "lots of stuff we're doing behind the scenes," such as fulfillment and Checkout.org (Year One stats thread, July 2015).

What keeps people coming back

  1. The Meh button and the streak calendar. The core loop. Each day, clicking "meh" adds a face to a monthly calendar and extends your streak. Your current and best streak show on your forum profile, and once you reach seven days a calendar appears on the home page (newcomer explainer). When a 2019 redesign removed the calendar, members protested and staff restored it, but only for users with a current streak of at least 7 days, "so the page will stay simpler and cleaner for those not into the game" (Meh Face calendar & stats, Apr 2019). Streaks show the classic cliff: "If I broke my streak, I'd stop being so nuts about checking it every day" (Year One thread). Members built their own streak tools (MehStalker's calendar).
  2. The midnight ritual and daily poll. One deal, one poll and one discussion every day, and nothing else to browse. The poll is often unrelated to the product ("I clean:", "Worst gift to receive:"), which gives non-buyers a reason to post daily.
  3. Mystery-box lotteries. Woot's Bag o' Crap DNA. Fukubukuro "lucky bags" (from autumn 2014; some buyers got whole pallets delivered) and the Instant Regret Kit, a cheap random box later sold with a refund if you spend enough during a Meh-rathon. Each one comes with a reveal thread where members show what they got (Fukubukuro 3 reveal, 2014; IRK Meh-rathon rules, 2024).
  4. Meh-rathons. Woot-Off-style all-day "gauntlets of nonstop deals… until we run out of stuff (or patience)" (meh.com). These are the spike days, with birthday editions every July.
  5. Scapegoat of the Month. It began on Day Zero (July 2014), when the launch broke and the team wanted "someone to take the blame for all of our mistakes." Each month a member is crowned goat, wears a goat icon and gets blamed for everything; it "has since evolved to be a big part of the community" (Scapegoat history). It still runs every month in 2026 (Sept 2026 thread). Being goat drives activity: "I felt that i was more active in the community than any other month" (Year of the Goat).
  6. Identity and status. Names like "mehtizens", badges next to usernames (Kickstarter Backer, VMP shield, staff flask or Stores.com icon, "Member since"), Irk the mascot puppet (a June 2015 "You asked for it — Ask IRK" Q&A drew 648 comments, per a Wayback capture of the forum), and running jokes (speaker docks, candy corn, PopSockets).
  7. "Ink for IRKs." Members got a Meh-logo tattoo, real or drawn on, to earn a free IRK at every Meh-rathon. The club closed to new entries at 31 members (Ink for IRK 2023). It is extreme commitment, turned into a recurring ritual.
  8. Member-only perks as status. VMPs got a 4 pm reserve of sold-out deals, last-call leftovers, member merch, feature previews and surprise gifts, such as free socks for every VMP in 2015 (State of the VMP, 2017; OHSHIT REPORT, 2015). After new VMP sign-ups closed on 1 March 2017, the grandfathered status became a badge you lose forever if you cancel ("there is no way to rejoin", FAQ). That is a powerful, slightly perverse switching cost: one member stays despite "absolutely no chance I have gotten my money's worth" (Mar 2025).
  9. Peer support and off-topic belonging. Unofficial holiday gift exchanges organized by members (2020), craft threads, life updates, wedding and baby announcements ("I got married, no really" drew 101 comments in 2023), and a coffee club.

The content engine

  • Product write-ups as entertainment. Woot's honest, self-mocking copy came back. Today many write-ups are serialized fiction: "Ghastly Comfort" letters from the proprietor of "The Dread Inn at Death Rock," with an index thread of every installment (meh.com), and "Mehsterpiece Theatre." Readers who never buy still read.
  • Videos. At launch every deal had a video: "Mad Ape Den Karaoke," the 'Possum Head Chronicles series (2014), Irk puppet sketches, and "Hey Everybody, It's Glen" staff reviews (BI; 'Possum Head binge links). Output fell; by 2022 members were asking when Irk would "start making more videos" (Birthday AMA, 2022), and the YouTube channel has 4.7K subscribers.
  • Livestreams (2015). A weekly "Mediocre LabStream" (Cards Against Humanity battles between staff), Periscope streams all day on Meh's birthday, even the community manager opening his wedding gifts live (LabStream; Periscope). The LabStream ended with a "finale" in June 2015.
  • Radical transparency. Live sales stats on every deal, anniversary stat infographics with personalized emails, and published internal "OHSHIT REPORTS" explaining failures down to the Azure Service Bus timeout (2015). Staff also explain business decisions in-thread, such as extending deals because deal sites post them late (Feb 2024) and the price rise to $8.99 (Feb 2026).
  • Shoddy Goods (2024–). A weekly-ish newsletter by Jason Toon "about the stories behind the stuff people make, buy, and sell," on Substack with "hundreds of thousands of subscribers" (that count likely reflects Meh's email list; my inference). Each issue is cross-posted as a forum topic that draws 30–40 comments (Substack; forum).
  • Bot-made content. @mediocrebot posts deal and poll topics and, since October 2022, makes AI images on command: /showme, at first on Stable Diffusion and later DALL·E (mediocrebot takes an art class; /showme v2.0). It became a forum toy that members use in nearly every thread.
  • Member content, adopted by the company. The Scapegoat started in a staff Day Zero game and turned into a member tradition. The 2014 "Mehrican Exchange" was entirely member-run and earned its organizer the goat crown. Birthday write-up contests let members write deal copy (2018). A tenth-birthday scavenger hunt in 2024 was billed as "10 years… of playing along with our stupid experiments" (2024).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff in the threads. Founders post under handles: @snapster (Matt Rutledge), @dave (Dave Rutledge, CMO/CCO, "who steers the community"), @shawn. Early hires included a dedicated community manager, @JonT, who ran topics, bans, the first Fukubukuros and videos, plus customer-support staff (@MEHcus, @hollboll) who were introduced to members by name (Meet Meh: Customer Support, Oct 2014). JonT left around 2015–16 and later called the forum "the best online community I've ever been a part of" (June 2016). A staffer, @ExtraMedium, runs the Scapegoat and Ink-for-IRK threads in 2022–2026.
  • Superuser to staff. @Thumperchick, an early regular, became the first volunteer moderator in September 2015 and later a paid employee in customer service and the community (Meet Thumperchick; Quip thread). Other volunteer moderators followed (moderator family). By late 2024 some had been stepped down because participation was declining.
  • Rules. "No BULLSHIT™," an acronym for Bias, Unmanned accounts, Libel, Luridness, Spam, Hatespeech, Idiocy and Trolling, closing with "cursing will not be fucking tolerated" (rules). Enforcement is light: "I've been able to be extremely lenient"; posts should be "funny, informative, and/or interesting"; criticism of products and of Meh stays up (Feb 2015). Political flame wars are a recurring strain; volunteer moderators edit name-calling, and bans are decided by staff (Meh on Reddit, 2016).
  • Feedback loop. Features change in public threads (the calendar restored after protest; deal extensions explained). VMPs were promised "sneak peeks of new features." Mediocre's lab framing ("experiments") invites members to test ideas.

Growth loops

  • Word of mouth from Woot veterans and tech media. TechCrunch, BI, the Washington Post, Lifehacker and Bustle covered the launch. A Daring Fireball sponsorship headlined "Fucking Amazon" ran in 2014 and again in 2025 (Daring Fireball).
  • Growth was fast and then stalled. 170,000 accounts in six months, but Rutledge says the "Walled Garden internet era had monetized and stalled word spreading of our reboot," so casual buyers never arrived. VMP was pushing Meh "towards being a Members-Only style club whether we wanted it or not," and free shipping for loyalists could not be subsidized (Membership Revisited).
  • The fix was other stores, not the community. MorningSave (daytime-TV deals, 2017), SideDeal, Shop Univision and a 300+ partner affiliate network (Slickdeals, Brad's Deals, Rakuten, Honey) bring in "casual customers [who] can again subsidize freight costs for loyal members" (Stores.com). Stores.com Cash (April 2026) cross-pollinates buyers across stores. Deal sites now feed Meh too: 4–9% of visits come from referrals, and staff extend deals to catch late deal-site traffic.
  • SEO is weak. Most search traffic is people searching for "meh" itself (Half a Year), and 94–96% of visits are typed directly.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard numbers (company-published): year-one revenue share of 29.71% for Meh; 170,000 accounts at six months; daily units and revenue in the stats block, such as roughly $32–36K a day on normal days in 2015–16 vs $6,828 on 7 November 2026 (these are single-day samples, not trends); Stores.com claims 17M+ customers and 16M+ orders across all brands (press release).
  • Company claims: "We've spent a decade building brands that customers genuinely love coming back to" (Rutledge, Jan 2026). The press release singles out Meh for "a devoted community known for its irreverent humor and engaged forums," the community positioned as brand equity.
  • Counter-evidence: the founder's own account that the loyal-member model did not cover shipping costs and had to be closed (2017). Members say MorningSave "keeps the lights on at meh" (2020 thread). No retention, LTV or NPS figures were published, and no academic research on Meh was found. Funding: a roughly $1M CB Insights entry for "Meh Labs" is unverified; Tracxn lists no rounds. Score: business impact evidence 2.

What they did right

  1. Built the forum first and made it the store's home page. The forum went live in 2013, a year before the store, and every product is a discussion (TechCrunch). Result: 94–96% direct traffic for 12 years.
  2. Rewarded a daily visit with no purchase. The Meh button, streaks and calendar make "come back tomorrow" the goal, not "buy now." Members fought to keep the calendar when it was removed (2019).
  3. Turned failure into folklore. The launch crash became the Scapegoat, still running 12 years later; outages became public OHSHIT REPORTS. Honesty became something members enjoy.
  4. Let members own traditions, then amplified them. Gift exchanges, tattoos for IRKs and fan tools were adopted and celebrated, not policed. A volunteer moderator was eventually hired (Thumperchick).
  5. Transparency as content. Live stats, anniversary data and in-thread explanations of pricing and policy keep trust high even through price rises.
  6. Content worth reading without buying. Serialized fiction in deal copy, a newsletter by a veteran writer, and in-thread AI toys give lurkers a daily reason to visit. Content engine score 4: strong, long-running writing, but video and livestreams faded after 2015–16.
  7. Retention design: score 5. Streaks, lotteries, rituals, monthly roles, status badges and spike events, all native to the product.

What they did wrong

  1. Loyalty without acquisition. The community grew quickly, then plateaued at a few thousand daily regulars. The founder conceded casual buyers "had not heard," and the members-only economics broke (2018). Consequence: VMP closed to new members (March 2017), growth moved to TV and affiliate stores, and Meh became the portfolio's "community" brand rather than its engine.
  2. A closed tier that punishes cancelling. Grandfathered VMP "cancelations are final." It keeps a core, but newcomers can never get the status, and departures feel like goodbyes ("It's finally over," a Day Zero member cancelling in Dec 2025).
  3. Community investment thinned as the company diversified. The community manager left (around 2015–16), livestreams ended in June 2015, videos dwindled, and Meh-rathons were cut back in 2022 "to keep things from getting stale" (a member's reply: "not having mehrathons tends to make things more stale", Birthday AMA 2022). Volunteer moderators were stepped down in 2024. Staff investment score 3: heavy early, modest now.
  4. Merchandise drift. Long-timers complain the refurb Roombas and Dysons gave way to TV-shopping brands: "What happened to our nerdy, happy, little corner of the internet?… What happened to all the refurbs?" (May 2026).
  5. Corporate rebrands and platform changes. "A Mediocre Corporation" became Mercatalyst because "business-types took it at face value" (2020), then Stores.com (2026). A 2025 redesign drew "It is fucking ugly, we hate it @dave" (Who moved my cheese, Feb 2025), and Stores.com accounts and Cash brought confusion and complaints about being billed after cancelling (Oct 2026).
  6. Price rises on the loyal core. Shipping went from $5 (2014) to $6 (2022) to $9.99, and membership from $4.99 (2018) to $5.99 to $8.99 (Feb 2026), explained openly but felt (Cost of Membership).
  7. Lost media. Early images and videos hosted off-site died: "most of those media links are now dead… The site that did went under, so we can't get it back" (Forum Stats, July 2025).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Reward the visit, not the purchase. A one-tap daily action with streaks and a visible calendar (the Meh button) makes "come back tomorrow" the habit, and gives you a clean daily metric of your engaged core.
  2. Put the community inside the product surface. Every item, feature or release gets its own discussion thread on the page itself, not in a separate forum users have to find.
  3. Monthly rotating roles and running jokes. A Scapegoat-style title gives one member a month of spotlight and keeps rituals alive at almost no cost.
  4. Publish your numbers and your failures. Live stats and public post-mortems turn trust into content and make price changes survivable.
  5. Spot superusers, give them tools, then hire them. The volunteer-moderator-to-staff path (Thumperchick) and celebrating fan-built tools (MehStalker) build ownership.

Avoid these

  1. Don't mistake a loyal core for a growth engine. Build a separate acquisition loop (referrals, SEO-worthy content, partners) early; Meh learned in 2017 that loyalty alone can't pay the bills.
  2. Don't create status tiers people can never re-enter. Closed, grandfathered tiers freeze your community's demographics and make every cancellation permanent.
  3. Don't let the community become the portfolio's mascot while you starve it. When content, events and moderation are cut, participation decays slowly and quietly, here about 60% over a decade.
