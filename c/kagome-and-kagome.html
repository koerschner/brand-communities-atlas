TL;DR

  • What it is: a free, members-only community site that Kagome (ketchup, tomato juice, the Yasai Seikatsu vegetable drinks) opened on 15 April 2015 for its heaviest buyers. It grew from 10,000 members (Oct 2016) to 49,000 (Sep 2021) and 60,000 (2024). It has never run a campaign to recruit members (eLife 2024).
  • Why people come back: a six-month ritual every year. Kagome sends free tomato seedlings (5,000 winners in 2026), and winners must post their plants' growth to the "Tomato Gallery" in the Tomakomi sub-community, which drew 17,000+ posts in 2026. Around that sit Tomato Points for every login, post, like and reply, member-started word-chain threads with 30,000–44,000 comments, a monthly quiz, polls, and staff who reply.
  • What the company feeds it: two marketers working part-time on it plus the agency eLife running moderation and operations. They post 6–8 themes or articles a month, write staff and employee blog posts, run about 10 roundtables and fan meetings a year, hold small factory and farm tours, live-stream cooking classes and growing clinics, and use the members for co-creation (a fan-voted sound logo that has run in Kagome's TV ads since 2017; a tomato sauce redesigned into a tube in 2019).
  • Biggest mistake: the community is still mostly a marketing-department asset. Kagome's own team admits that few other departments use it and that it is little known inside the company (2024), and it can't show causal sales impact. The prize and points machinery also attracts prize-hunters, which led to rules against multiple accounts (2024) and AI-generated photos (2026).
  • The lesson: pick your KPI (here the share of members who act each month, kept at ~10%, not member count), and build a long seasonal activity around the product. Have the brand ship a physical thing that members then report on together, so the season itself pulls them back.

What the community is

&KAGOME ("Community made by everyone and Kagome", みんなとカゴメでつくるコミュニティ) lives at and.kagome.co.jp. Membership is free, Japan only, and requires registration; reading is mostly public. The launch press release (Kagome, Apr 2015) listed a talk-room bulletin board, "Recipe Notes" (member recipes), "Which do you prefer?" polls, "Kagome Dayori" (staff news "only available here") and an events plaza. Today's navigation adds product reviews (おいしいレビュー), the photo gallery Pasharipo (パシャリポ, opened Jul 2020), product polls (どれにしようかな), Mitty Quiz (ミッティクイズ, Jan 2017), expert articles (実になるおはなし), employee profiles (カゴメの人) and Tomakomi (トマコミ), the tomato-growing sub-community with its yearly Tomato Gallery, a grow-guide corner run by "Dr. Tomato" (あつまれ博士の畑), Q&A and a "#nice failure" theme (Tomakomi; 7th-anniversary timeline).

Kagome owns and runs it from the Advertising Department's publicity group in the Marketing Division. eLife Inc. built it on its "eLife Community Suite" and handles part of the day-to-day operation (PR Times, Aug 2024). There are no unofficial spaces of note: no evidence was found of a fan-run forum or subreddit, and a Japanese food brand's fans are not on Reddit. Related official spaces are Kagome's LINE account (about 4.6M friends in Dec 2019, used for reach, not depth; Commune), the "fan shareholder" program, and VEGECOMI, a separate community site for customers of Kagome's direct-mail business Kenko Chokusobin (VEGECOMI). Status: active and still growing in Nov 2026. New staff posts go up several times a week, and 2026 brought a record gallery season (&KAGOME blog index).

Origin and strategy

  • The trigger was a defection problem, not a growth goal. Kagome analysed panel data in 2014 and found that the top 2.5% of customers accounted for about 30% of sales (30–40% in a 2015 telling). Those heavy users, people buying over ¥80,000 of Kagome products a year (more than ¥220 a day), were slowly leaving as the tomato-juice boom of 2012 faded and more drink choices appeared (Nikkei xTrend, Aug 2015; ITmedia, Mar 2020). A Kagome speaker later added the 2015 low-carb boom, when photos of Yasai Seikatsu juice next to sugar cubes spread online (Hansoku Kaigi, Dec 2020).
  • Proposed in 2013 as part of a "digital marketing strategy framework" while company results were falling (Web Tan, Jul 2018). Kagome's executives agreed from the start not to chase member numbers. The 2015 launch lead, Daiki Shigetomo, said the goal was "not a community that sells itself on millions or hundreds of thousands of members, but a place for top customers who definitely love Kagome products and buy them every day" (Nikkei xTrend 2015).
  • Three stated aims: know the fans, tell the fans, and experience things together with the fans (Sendenkaigi).
  • A planned rollout in phases: 2015–2016 build the space and gather members (target 10,000); 2017–2018 spread its use inside the company and put fan voices into marketing; 2019 onward use it for loyalty and repeat purchase (Sendenkaigi; Nikkei xTrend, Mar 2020).
  • Corporate roots: since 2001 Kagome has courted individual investors as "fan shareholders" (99% of its shareholders by headcount are individuals), with farm visits, factory tours and talks with the president. Kagome says these shareholders buy about 10x as much of its products as the general public. &KAGOME widened that relationship from shareholders to all customers (PR Times 2024; Web Tan). "Shifting to fan-based marketing" became one of the four basic strategies of Kagome's 2022–2025 medium-term plan (Kagome IR).
  • Champions: Shinya Mizuno (publicity group manager; built the customer-data dashboards; joined Kagome in 1991 and spent 21 years in IT before marketing), Yoko Ukigaya, Rumiko Taguchi, and later Kyoko Hisamori, manager of the advertising group (Commune; eLife 2024).

Trajectory: steady, linear growth of about 10,000 members a year from 2016 to 2024, with no visible peak. No official member count after 2024 was found. Activity measures from 2026 (record gallery posts, a bigger seedling giveaway) suggest it is still growing. Member profile: about 70% women, mostly in their 30s to 60s, and 80% have used Kagome products for 10+ years. They are "serious and introverted": they rarely post on social media but evangelise Kagome to family and friends (ITmedia 2020).

What keeps people coming back

  1. The seasonal seedling ritual (the main retention engine). Kagome had given away tomato seedlings for years. In 2016 it tied the giveaway to the site: members enter, winners receive seedlings of Kagome's own varieties (Koama-chan cherry tomatoes, the juice tomato Ririko, high-lycopene tomatoes), and the "mission" for winners is to post growth photos to the Tomato Gallery. Winners who don't post lose their eligibility next year (2026 rules). That turns a one-off prize into six months of repeat visits: planting, flowering, fruit set, harvest, then autumn "keep going" campaigns. The team calls the seedlings the "killer content". Unlike social posts that fade after a day, it works as a stock of content that people keep revisiting (Commune). Taguchi says Tomakomi turned a "point" relationship into a "line" (eLife 2024). Peer help happens on its own: one member posts a problem and others answer, with Kagome's agronomists stepping in. A "#nice failure" (#ナイス失敗) theme makes it safe to post plants that died (Tomato Gallery 2026 recap). Member bios show the habit compounding: "Koama-chan, year 4", "growing tomatoes for 16 years".
  2. Tomato Points on every action. 5 points for the daily login, 10 for a comment (up to 3 a day), 50 for a new thread, recipe or photo, 5 for a vote, 10 for a quiz answer, 10 for clicking a newsletter link, and points when others like or reply to you. Points buy entries into "speed lotteries" (100 points a draw, once a day), prize draws (since Sep 2023) and charity donations (2024, an idea members asked for) (points table; PR Times 2024). Early on, product-tester slots needed 300+ points. That stopped working as a filter because so many members passed 300 (Web Tan).
  3. Member-owned daily rituals. The talk room was designed for Kagome to set topics, but members started their own threads and chat in them (Web Tan). The biggest are endless word-chain games (しりとり) and "what did you eat today" threads with 10,000–44,000 comments each. Interpretation: these behave like a daily check-in. Because points are paid for up to three comments a day and for receiving replies, a word-chain thread is the cheapest way to collect them, so part of this volume is point-driven.
  4. Being heard, with proof. Votes and roundtables visibly change real things: the sound logo, a T-shirt for tomato juice's 90th anniversary, the box design of the "Vegetable stock set", catchphrases on in-store displays, member recipes posted in supermarkets (10th anniversary post; Commune). Members get a sneak preview before the public (the sound logo was played on &KAGOME before the TV launch).
  5. Ties to staff. "&KAGOME Staff" and named "Kagome employee" characters answer posts. Employees from many departments write in Kagome Dayori, and the 10th-anniversary garden picks were drawn by the &KAGOME staffer (10th anniversary). Staff say members treat them as "friends who share our ambitions", and members say the site makes Kagome "feel close" (see Member voice).
  6. Small, special offline events. Roughly 20 people at a time: the first factory tour in Nasu (the most active members, paying their own way on a weekday), yearly factory tours 2015–2018, the Yasai Seikatsu Farm in Fujimi from 2019, an employee mixer in Osaka in 2024, and a farm "Wonderland" tour for 20 members in Sep 2026. They are designed as "experiences only possible because you came today", such as tasting raw materials that aren't normally sold (eLife 2024; farm tour report).
  7. Light-touch seasonal campaigns bring dormant members back. One example is the "kotatsu campaign" (pick the Kagome product you'd enjoy under the heated winter table), which drew more entries than any other campaign and "reached dormant users" (Commune). Anniversary campaigns run every year.

Switching costs are mostly emotional: a growing history of gallery posts, points, followers, and the yearly seedling eligibility, which members keep only by posting.

The content engine

  • Company-made: in 2020, 6–8 operator themes or articles a month, of which 2–3 were also pushed by email newsletter (Hansoku Kaigi). Formats: Kagome Dayori news and campaign posts; employee interviews ("people from various departments… many are energised by users' comments", eLife works); "Useful stories" (実になるおはなし), deep expert health articles. Kagome had worried these would be too specialised, but they became the most-read content. Example: an article on sulforaphane. They also let Kagome explain health effects that Japanese drug-advertising law keeps off its product pages (Web Tan; Nikkei xTrend 2020). Then there are Dr. Tomato's growing guides, the monthly Mitty Quiz, polls, new-product round-ups, and event reports.
  • Live and online (since 2019–2020): Kagome adopted live video in earnest in 2019 (Nikkei xTrend 2020). Examples: an online growing consultation in Jul 2020 (8 members on Zoom with the farm's staff, about 90 watching; Hansoku Kaigi); live-streamed cooking classes with Kagome's corporate chef (from Dec 2020; &KAGOME); online roundtables "several times a year" (eLife works).
  • Member-made: recipes, product reviews, Pasharipo photos, Tomato Gallery growth logs, talk threads. Kagome rewards them with points, prizes in themed photo campaigns, features ("Picks from members' voices", "This month's Recipe Notes"), and reuse in stores. Campaign terms give Kagome free rights to use what members submit (10th anniversary terms).
  • Ambassador cohorts (since 2022): small groups of members chosen for a product (Ketchup 2022, Yasai Seikatsu 100 2023, "Yasai Ichinichi Kore Ippon" 2024 with 20 places). They attend a fan meeting at the Tokyo office (travel at their own cost) and then post recipes and reviews for about three months (ketchup report; 2024 call).

People, programs and governance

  • Team: at launch, a five-person digital-marketing team in Marketing. By 2018 it had become a 10-person publicity group covering both mass and digital media, with 2 people assigned to &KAGOME, both alongside other duties, a set-up that stayed roughly the same for about five years (Web Tan; Commune). eLife handles post checks, email inquiries and operator replies; its operations lead worked remotely from Koriyama (Web Tan).
  • Moderation and risk: quality assurance, legal and the customer centre are part of operations. Posts with inaccurate food or health claims are hidden immediately, after Kagome contacts the poster. Before launch, an escalation process for flare-ups, up to closing or pausing the site, was agreed in advance. Kagome reported "zero major trouble" up to 2020 (ITmedia p2). Members can report posts under eight categories, and the rules ban multiple accounts (2024 notice).
  • Feedback loop: product teams increasingly ask the &KAGOME team "can you ask the fans about this new product?" because answers come back quickly and safely (Commune). The team limits promotional posts from brand managers so the site doesn't turn into advertising (Web Tan). Kagome surveys members' NPS every year and adopts member suggestions, such as the 2024 points donation and emailing people who lost the 2026 seedling draw, which members had requested in a 2024 survey (winner post).

Growth loops

Mostly closed by design. At launch Kagome invited only people who already had a strong tie to it: shareholders and mail-order customers, then factory-tour and cooking-class alumni (Nikkei xTrend 2015). Growth since then has come from: (1) the seedling giveaway and product-tester calls, which pushed it past 10,000 (ITmedia p2); (2) prize-blog and sweepstakes aggregators that list &KAGOME campaigns (e.g. Ameblo winner posts); (3) Kagome's "widen vs deepen" funnel, where mass media and social posts plus the 4.6M-friend LINE account build reach and &KAGOME deepens it (Commune; ITmedia p4); (4) offline word of mouth. Members rarely amplify on social media, which Kagome treats as a feature, not a bug. In Mizuno's words, the hope that "if it succeeds it will spread automatically was an illusion" (Web Tan). Being a famous case study is itself a loop: the community is cited in marketing media and even appears in high-school textbooks (eLife works).

Evidence of business impact

Company figures (not independently audited):

  • Members' spending is 1.4x the general consumer's, across 2x as many product types (2018) (Web Tan). Kagome's "fans" (defined by purchase amount and affection) buy about 10x the general public's yearly amount (2024; this is the fan segment, not only members) (eLife 2024).
  • Member NPS was 53.1 (2017), up three years running, and stays higher than for newsletter or social-media audiences (Web Tan; Commune).
  • A monthly action rate of 10–15%, against "single digits" at typical company communities (eLife) and "a few percent, often under 1%" at large ones in Kagome's 2015 benchmark (Nikkei xTrend 2015).
  • Concrete outputs: the tube-pack "Kagome Rich Tomato Sauce" (Feb/Mar 2019, 1.5x the tomatoes) came from a member roundtable; the sound logo in TV ads since Feb 2017; in-store display copy written by members (ITmedia p3).

Missing: no retention or churn curve for members against matched non-members, no revenue attribution, and nothing about &KAGOME in Kagome's financial filings beyond the general "fan-based marketing" strategy. Hisamori (2024): "we are still in trial and error… to show the effect more precisely" (eLife 2024). Score 3/5: plausible and consistent, but self-reported and correlational.

What they did right

  • Measured depth, not size. Using the share of members who act each month as the KPI from day one kept the team focused on activity, not sign-ups. The rate held at about 10% from 30,000 to 60,000 members (Nikkei xTrend 2021).
  • Seeded the space with the right people. Inviting shareholders and mail-order buyers first gave the site a warm, loyal culture. Even criticism tends to open with "Speaking as a fan…" (Web Tan).
  • Built a ritual around the product's own story. Growing Kagome's tomatoes connects members to the company's farming origins and gives them a reason to post for months. Raising the giveaway to 5,000 winners led to a record 17,000+ gallery posts in 2026.
  • Made co-creation real and visible, then reported the results back to members first (sound logo, sauce, box design, T-shirt).
  • Engineered safety for a health-food brand: QA, legal and customer-service sign-off, quick hiding of health claims, and a crisis plan in place before launch. There has been no major incident in 11 years.
  • Cheap and durable. Two part-time staff and an agency have kept it running continuously for 11+ years through several reorganisations.
  • Listened about the system itself. Kagome added point donations and emails to everyone who loses a draw because members asked, and wrote a clear generative-AI policy in Oct 2026 (AI notice).

What they did wrong

  • It never escaped the marketing department. In 2024 Taguchi said co-creation still relied mainly on marketers who understand &KAGOME, that awareness inside the company was "less than ideal", and that other departments showed a "difference in temperature". Hisamori called fixing this "the most important issue" (eLife 2024). Consequence: it is a strong asset that the rest of the company barely uses.
  • No proof of incremental sales. Ten years in, the evidence is still "members buy more", which could just as well mean heavy buyers are the ones who join. Without a holdout study, the budget rests on the company's belief in its culture.
  • Prize mechanics attract prize-hunters. Members holding several accounts to win seedlings forced a public warning in 2024. AI-generated images in photo campaigns needed a policy in 2026. Bloggers count their "winning streaks" (2024 notice; 2026 rules; member bio). The points rules also encourage low-value posting, such as the word-chain threads (interpretation). The 300-point tester gate stopped filtering anyone because of point inflation.
  • The physical side causes friction. Seedlings arrive wilted or late, and staff now ask members not to post about delivery problems in the talk room or gallery, sending them to a support desk instead (member post, May 2024; winner post).
  • Little amplification. By design, members rarely share on social media, so the community does little for acquisition. Kagome admits it has "honestly not figured out" how to divide roles between social media and the community (Commune).
  • Vendor and platform dependence. A software vulnerability forced the site offline for about 16 hours on 21–22 Oct 2026. No data loss was reported, but it shows the risk of a custom, agency-run stack (maintenance notice).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Start from a customer-value analysis, then choose depth as the KPI. Find your "2.5% who drive 30%" and measure the share of members who act each month, not sign-ups.
  2. Design one long seasonal activity around your product with built-in reasons to post: ship something, require progress logs, and make failures welcome ("#nice failure"). An app could do the same with a cohort challenge whose stages members document together.
  3. Seed with your warmest existing customers and skip acquisition campaigns at launch, so the founding culture is set by people who already care.
  4. Close the co-creation loop in public: let members vote or join roundtables, ship the result, and show it to them first.
  5. Settle safety and the crisis playbook before launch, with legal and support sign-off, especially in health, finance or any other regulated product.

Avoid these

  1. Prize-driven points without quality gates. They inflate activity, invite multiple accounts and fill threads with filler. Reward contributions others find valuable, cap them, and keep the perks experiential.
  2. Letting the community stay a marketing-only asset. Give product, sales and R&D a standing way to use it, or it will stay the "famous case study" the company barely uses.
  3. Skipping impact measurement. Set up a holdout or matched-cohort comparison early. "Members buy more" won't survive a budget review forever.

Score justifications: retention mechanics 4 (strong seasonal ritual plus points, but leans on prizes); content engine 4 (steady staff, expert, live and member content); member ownership 3 (members run their own threads and co-create, but Kagome sets the agenda and there is no member-moderator tier); staff investment 3 (two part-time staff plus an agency, consistent for 11 years); business impact evidence 3 (company-reported spending and NPS figures plus concrete products, no causal data); durability 5 (11+ years of continuous growth and a record 2026 season).
