TL;DR

  • What it was: MOSH ("Mobilize and Share") was Nokia's free, open, user-generated content exchange for phones. Members uploaded apps, games, themes, ringtones, videos, images and documents, and anyone could browse and download them from a PC (mosh.nokia.com) or a phone (mosh.nokia.mobi). Private beta opened 9 August 2007 and the open beta came about two weeks later. It never left beta. Nokia announced the closure on 18 March 2009 and folded it into the Ovi Store, which launched 26 May 2009.
  • Why people came back: Free stuff for their phones. It was one of the few places a feature-phone owner in India, Russia or South Africa could fill a handset without a PC, a credit card or an operator portal. A light status layer sat on top: creator/collector/sharer stats, a dashboard ranking, and prominence for popular uploaders.
  • What Nokia fed it: Very little. A team of about 24 people built it in 94 days. It ran with no advertising, one staff help persona ("Russell"), a content-request feature (SEEK), audio fingerprinting from Audible Magic, and from May 2008 a paid "Spotlight" ad slot.
  • Biggest mistake: It was open with no pre-screening, inside a company whose core partners were record labels and app developers. Porn, pirated music and S60 warez flooded in. Warner Music withheld its catalogue from the Nokia Music Store over MOSH, and Nokia closed the site instead of fixing its governance. Members had nowhere to go: the successor was a screened, partly paid store, not a community.
  • The one lesson: Open UGC with status mechanics grows fast and cheap, especially where nothing else fills the need. Moderation and creator economics have to be designed before launch, though. If your legal and commercial partners can't live with the community, they will kill it.

What the community is

MOSH was a "user defined distribution channel for mobile content" (Wikipedia). Its sign-up page called it "a user-generated content platform designed specifically for use with any mobile device" for photos, videos, music, games, software mash-ups and blogs (Tech Digest, Aug 2007). Mechanically:

  • Open browsing, gated uploading. Anyone could browse and download. Registration, on PC or phone, was needed only to upload, and registered users could build collections, keep a profile page, comment and vote (WAP Review, Aug 2007). Medianama later noted that "registration was not required to download" (Medianama, 2 Apr 2009).
  • Three verbs per item: share, collect, download. Share sent a link by SMS or email, collect bookmarked the item to your account, and every item could be rated "like it"/"love it" and commented on (Mashable, 9 Aug 2007).
  • Mobile-first and device-agnostic. It ran on a .mobi site plus an S60 client that was "nothing more than something that gives you click-able Bookmarks" (Vaibhav Sharma, Mar 2008). Nokia stressed that it was "not Nokia specific" (Nokia PR via WebWire, 23 Oct 2007). About 70% of registered devices were Nokias anyway (TechCrunch, 4 Oct 2007).
  • Uploads up to 100 MB, which made it a personal locker as well as an exchange: "a complete compilation of the stuff on our account ready for download anytime anywhere on any phone" (Sharma).

It was primarily a content exchange with social features, not a conversation community. Mashable noted that "you cannot see another user's friends" and there was no private messaging (Mashable). The "community" was the sum of uploaders, collectors and requesters.

Unofficial spaces: no fan forum dedicated to MOSH turned up. Discussion happened on general Symbian and phone sites (My-Symbian, AllAboutSymbian, GSMArena comment threads, Indian phone blogs like FoneArena). MOSH sat alongside fan download sites such as Dotsis and Symbian Freak and Indian clones like Funmaza, Mobiletoones and Masti4India (Medianama; GSMArena comments, ~Mar 2008). A Reddit search through Brave found no MOSH threads, and the Arctic Shift archive rate-limited the body searches, so Reddit coverage is unverified.

Origin and strategy

  • Founding story. George Linardos, a former Hollywood executive and Macromedia programming director, was told to clear his diary for a six-week ideation retreat. It turned out to be "a pot of porridge every morning at a Spartan hotel hours from Finnish capital Helsinki." Craving social contact, the team designed a site for "informal networking ... especially for people without access to a PC" (Reuters via Mail & Guardian, 24 Oct 2007). Linardos co-founded MOSH and ran its marketing, product and business development, and "a core group of 24 developers and rogue Nokia employees built and released Mosh in 94 days" (TechCrunch).
  • Sponsor. MOSH came out of Forum Nokia, Nokia's developer organisation. Its VP, Lee Epting, fronted the SEEK launch (WebWire) and was later credited with launching MOSH "focused at providing free content to mobile phone users" (Campaign, Aug 2013). Epting called Forum Nokia "a cost centre and not a profit centre" (Economic Times, 2007).
  • Strategic logic. Linardos said: "From content distribution point-of-view, mobile has been a very closed channel. First thing we wanted to do is to open it up." Analyst Neil Mawston framed it as defensive: "In the near-term, such internet services are designed to make Nokia handsets stickier. In the longer-term, the goal is to generate a fresh revenue stream" (M&G/Reuters). MOSH was one of a cluster of 2007 service bets (Nokia Music Store, N-Gage, Twango/Share on Ovi, WidSets, Download!) under Nokia's push to become an internet company.
  • Business model. At launch there was none: "Mosh charges no fees and has no income" (The Register, 14 Aug 2007). Advertising arrived in May 2008 (see Evidence below).
  • Attitude to control. Linardos said: "We opened up the platform. We don't know where it exactly goes and we are not entirely in control." He also described the tension: "We want Mosh to be completely authentic, but at the same time we are part of the global company with global brand." The team planned in 4–6 week cycles, faster than Nokia normally moved (M&G/Reuters).

The trajectory was steep growth with no visible plateau before the decision to close. Reuters called it a "success story" that "attracted a wide audience around the globe, unlike most of Nokia's new fledgling services." The numbers are all download and unique-user counts. Nokia never published active uploaders, retention or cohort data. What keeps people coming back

  1. A free supply of things for your phone (the main driver). Users came for free themes, games, apps and ringtones that fit their exact handset. A Symbian user in 2008 wanted "a central repository where we could find all of them at one place and ... someone uploaded them for us" (Sharma). In India and other PC-poor markets it filled a real gap: "mobile phone is often the fundamental way these people stay in contact with the world" (Linardos via M&G). This is consumption retention, not belonging.
  2. Status for contributors. Each account carried creator, collector and sharer stats (Wikipedia). A "My Dashboard" graphed your ranking among all users (TechCrunch; Mashable). Uploading and commenting raised your popularity, while removed or badly rated content lowered it, and "content uploaded by the most popular users gets greater prominence on the website and phone interface" (The Register; Telecompaper). Uploaders could also see which countries were collecting their work. Gamified badges for milestones like "100k downloads" were promised in Oct 2007 (TechCrunch); no evidence was found that they shipped.
  3. Collections as a light switching cost. A collection was a cloud bookmark list reachable from any phone, so switching handsets meant reloading your apps from MOSH. That made the account useful across devices (Sharma).
  4. Following creators. Users could "subscribe to members who develop the best of Mosh" (TechCrunch).
  5. SEEK (requests). SEEK was announced 23 Oct 2007 and slated to launch 14 Dec 2007. Users could post requests ("a mobile map of Amsterdam", "video of a specific dive in the Maldives") and the community answered with suggestions or custom-made content. Nokia pitched "mobile scavenger hunts, sponsored SEEK competitions" and cross-country developer collaboration (WebWire). This was MOSH's one designed conversation loop. No evidence was found of its usage or of any competitions actually run.
  6. Rituals, events, cohorts: no evidence found of recurring events, challenges, seasons or meetups.

Retention mechanics score 2/5: there was a real status layer for contributors, but nothing pulled casual downloaders back except the free catalog. Durability score 1/5: the community lasted about 20 months and ended with no migration.

The content engine

Nokia produced almost no content itself, by design: "It's all up to the users" (Linardos).

  • "Russell": a staff persona who wrote tips and help and talked with users. TechCrunch called him "the resident lochness monster who interacts with Mosh users" (TechCrunch). Nokia was "looking to hire a new 'Russell'" within weeks of launch (WAP Review). This was the whole staff voice. Cadence unknown.
  • Press releases for feature drops (SEEK in Oct 2007, Spotlight in Jun 2008).
  • Member content was the product: original wallpapers, freeware, concert clips, "the complete works of Shakespeare", and members like "Dr. Dan", who posted heart-attack first aid (TechCrunch). Epting said some uploaders had "become commercial ventures", for example artists making custom wallpapers in minutes (Economic Times). The only reward was ranking and prominence: no revenue share, no curation programme, no featured-creator scheme that could be documented.

Content engine score 1/5: one help persona and a handful of releases, with no education, events or curation.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: a core of about 24 builders (TechCrunch), led by Linardos, inside Forum Nokia under Epting. No community-manager roles were documented beyond "Russell". Staff investment 2/5: a real product team, but no marketing ("no marketing machine pushing Mosh", per Reuters/Firstpost) and almost no community staff.
  • Moderation: reactive only. Audible Magic fingerprinting checked uploads for copyrighted audio (CNET, Dec 2007). Beyond that, takedowns depended on notices: Nokia's spokesman said Beatles and Harry Potter copies stayed up because "we have not been contacted by the copyright owners" (Ars Technica, Nov 2007). Nokia said it removed most flagged content "within a couple of hours" of a takedown notice (SMH/AP, 3 Nov 2007). Down-votes and removals lowered a user's ranking, a weak self-policing incentive. Medianama's verdict: "It was a free-for-all ground; there was nobody in control" (Medianama).
  • Superuser or ambassador programme: none found. (Forum Nokia separately ran a 125-person "Champion" developer programme (Economic Times), but nothing shows it was tied to MOSH.)
  • Feedback into product: Nokia said SEEK came directly from watching users ask each other for content (WebWire). Member ownership 4/5: members supplied all the content and their votes decided prominence, but they had no governance role and no say over the closure.

Growth loops

  • Share by SMS or email on every item (Mashable), plus an option on the PC site to text yourself the mobile link (TechCrunch).
  • Word of mouth only: "no advertisements, just word-of-mouth promotion" (M&G/Reuters). The long nokia.com subdomain may have hurt it ("it affects word of mouth publicity," Sharma).
  • Distribution through Nokia: the MOSH client was offered through Nokia's Download! store, and handset guides pointed new owners there (Lowyat N85 thread, 2009). WAP Review saw Nokia's "ability to put a MOSH bookmark on every phone they sell" as the big lever (WAP Review). No evidence was found that it was preloaded at scale.
  • Search and direct links: MOSH download URLs were pasted into forums and YouTube descriptions (e.g. a GSMArena comment linking a dictionary .sis on MOSH's CDN), which made every upload a landing page.

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence is thin. Business impact evidence 2/5.

  • Reach (company figures): 6M+ downloads in about 10 weeks, 12M unique users by April 2008, 137M downloads by March 2009 (table above). No active-user, retention or revenue figures were published.
  • Advertising: in May–June 2008 MOSH joined the Nokia Media Network and launched Spotlight, paid placements "placed above all organic browse, search and contextual placements." Launch partners claimed download lifts of over 100% (Vringo) and over 95% (BLUE LION's qeep) since early May, and Universal Pictures booked a campaign for Wanted (Mobile Europe, 4 Jun 2008; Adweek). Nokia's ad head called MOSH's audience "the heaviest users of mobile content around the world." These are partner claims in a Nokia release, and no MOSH ad revenue was disclosed.
  • Negative impact on the core business: Warner Music refused to license to the Nokia Music Store at its UK launch (1 Nov 2007) because of MOSH. Warner wanted a promise that Nokia would not promote MOSH alongside paid music, and a second major label threatened to pull out (SMH/AP; Ars Technica).
  • Learning value: Strategy Analytics' David MacQueen: Nokia learned "how to run an open store, how to get developers products quickly to the market and how user recommendations should work ... the new Ovi Store is the child of Mosh rather than the child of Download" (Reuters/Firstpost). That is an analyst's opinion, not a measured result.

What they did right

  1. Fast, cheap launch with a clear job. 94 days, about 24 people, and real traction in weeks: 25k sign-ups and 1M downloads in the first week (TechCrunch; WAP Review).
  2. Built for the users the rest of the industry ignored. Mobile-first .mobi pages that worked on "old, not very advanced cellphones", and 80% of downloads came through mobile browsers. As a result India, Russia and South Africa led usage, not the US (M&G/Reuters; WebWire).
  3. Device-agnostic. Opening it to non-Nokia phones widened supply and demand. About 30% of registered devices weren't Nokias (TechCrunch).
  4. Status tied to contribution. Creator/collector/sharer stats and popularity-weighted prominence rewarded uploading, a pattern later repeated in app-store rankings (The Register).
  5. Product changes came from watching behaviour. SEEK turned observed peer requests into a feature (WebWire).
  6. Fast iteration cadence (4–6 week cycles) inside a slow hardware company (M&G/Reuters).

What they did wrong

  1. No pre-screening in a brand-sensitive company. MOSH "attracted loads of pornographic content". One member couldn't open it from an art gallery's public computers because of its explicit content (Reuters/Firstpost). Consequence: reputational drag and, eventually, closure.
  2. Copyright posture that clashed with Nokia's own partners. Fingerprinting plus notice-and-takedown let Green Day, Rihanna, Beatles and Harry Potter files stay up (SMH/AP; Ars). Consequence: Warner sat out the Nokia Music Store launch, and GigaOm summed up the closure as "Nokia's record label partners didn't like that" (Om Malik via NYT, 18 Mar 2009).
  3. It hosted warez that undercut the developer community Nokia needed. The Register argued that MOSH became "a huge repository of S60 warez - sending a clear message to its community that it needed anonymous freetards more than it needed loyal developers." It also quoted My-Symbian's Michael Jerz on how warez was killing even freeware development (The Register, 20 Mar 2009). (Jerz's quote is about Symbian warez generally. Tying it to MOSH is The Register's inference.)
  4. No creator economics. Uploaders earned rank but no money, and the most committed creators wanted payment: "As a content provider, revenue from the downloads would be welcomed" (Derrick Welsh via Reuters/Firstpost). Monetisation arrived as ads (Spotlight) placed above member content, rather than as a share for members.
  5. Eternal beta and brand ambiguity. It "was still in Beta when it closed" (Medianama). It overlapped confusingly with Share on Ovi (Twango) and Download! ("Why isn't MOSH under the Ovi umbrella?" — Sharma), and a sloppy launch leaked its own beta password (TechCrunch).
  6. Closure with no migration path for the community. Nokia announced it with "no official date", and the date was reported later as 1 May 2009. The replacement was a store where "Nokia says it will screen all content" and some content would be paid (Reuters/Firstpost; TheFoneCast). Collections, rankings, comments and SEEK had no equivalent in Ovi Store, so the community features simply ended. FoneArena told readers to "grab your downloads from Mosh as soon as possible" (FoneArena, 19 Mar 2009). The Ovi Store launch on 26 May 2009 was then widely called a "disaster" (TechCrunch; PocketGamer.biz). PocketGamer listed MOSH among Nokia initiatives that "come and go", next to Preminet, Snap Mobile, Download! and the original N-Gage, which eroded developer trust.
  7. It never decided whether it was a social network or a download site. Mashable found it "slightly less focused on users", with no friend lists and no messaging (Mashable), and an early tester asked "what is this for?" (Tech Digest). Without social ties, members had little reason to stay once the free supply moved elsewhere.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Ship a narrow, useful exchange fast, and let usage tell you where the demand is. MOSH found its real market (India, low-end phones, mobile-only users) within weeks.
  2. Make contribution visible and rewarding. Per-role stats (creator, collector, sharer), rankings, and prominence that follows quality votes.
  3. Give members a personal reason to keep an account, such as collections or a cloud locker that follows them across devices, so the community is also a utility.
  4. Turn observed member behaviour into features. SEEK formalised the peer requests Nokia saw users making.
  5. Design for the constrained user. Low-bandwidth, device-aware, mobile-browser-first access opened markets that PC-centric competitors missed.

Avoid these

  1. Launching open UGC without a moderation and rights model your partners can accept. If your core business depends on rights holders or developers, design pre-screening, trusted-uploader tiers and creator payouts before you scale. Otherwise a partner will force the decision for you.
  2. Monetising around creators instead of with them. Paid placement above member content plus no revenue share teaches creators the platform isn't theirs. Plan creator economics early.
  3. Shutting down without a migration path. Folding a community into a store that keeps none of its social graph, rankings or history wastes the asset, and every abandoned initiative makes the next one harder to recruit for.

Related communities worth adding

  • WidSets — Nokia — widget-sharing community (2006–2009), folded into Ovi Store alongside MOSH; failure/merged.
  • Share on Ovi (formerly Twango) — Nokia — media-sharing community acquired in 2007, rebranded 2008, later shut; overlapping UGC bet.
  • Forum Nokia Champions — Nokia — 125-person recognised-developer programme; possibly covered by the existing nokia-developer entry.
  • Nokia Download! / Ovi Store — Nokia — the curated store that replaced MOSH; useful as the "closed" counterpoint.
  • Yahoo! Mash — Yahoo — 2007 social network (working name "Mosh"), shut 2008; another quick-kill UGC experiment.
  • GetJar — GetJar — independent mobile app exchange that outlived MOSH; developer community and ranking model.
