TL;DR

  • What it is: Sprite Yard ("The Yard") was a branded social network on WAP mobile phones. It launched in China on June 1, 2007 and in the US on June 22, 2007. Teens texted YARD to 59666 ("LYMON"), picked a tag name, and got profiles, a Friends list, photo galleries (Pix), group messages (Shouts), small-group boards (Scribbles), a two-week Planner and a download locker (Nuggets) (Coca-Cola press release, Jun 6 2007; sprite.com/yard via Wayback, Jan 2008).
  • Why people were meant to come back: PIN codes under every 20 oz Sprite cap unlocked a collectible series of animated mobisodes ("consumers will want to buy another Sprite to see the next episode"), and the social tools were meant to keep "friends in their pockets" (SHOOTonline interview with Shelley DeVilliers, 2007; iMedia via leavcom, Jul 2007).
  • What Coke fed it: a "significant investment" in proprietary infrastructure, agency-made mobisodes and visual ringtones from Isobar's Molecular (back end) and Marvellous (mobile content), moderation and a customer-care line. It was promoted mainly through bottle caps and word of mouth rather than paid media (ClickZ, Jun 7 2007).
  • Biggest mistake: it asked teens to rebuild their social graph inside a soda brand's walled garden, on paid data plans and 2007 WAP phones, just as Facebook went mobile. "Nobody wants to go hang out with Sprite" (Organic's Chad Stoller, NYT via Gainesville Sun, Jun 7 2007). No usage numbers were ever released, and by September 2008 its help pages redirected to My Coke Rewards.
  • The one lesson: a brand community has to give members something they can't already get from the networks they use. Cap-code content was the only distinctive value here, and it was a promotion, not a reason to socialize.

What the community is

The Yard was a mobile-web (XHTML-MP/WAP) social network, not an installed app. Anyone with a WAP phone and a data plan could join. In the US you texted the keyword YARD to short code 59666 and received a link, then registered with a tag name, a password, your date of birth and confirmation that you lived in the US. Registration had to happen on your own handset. Coke stored your mobile number "so we can recognise you when you visit" (help/FAQ, Wayback Jan 2008).

The official feature list (sprite.com/yard, Wayback Jan 2008; ClickZ):

Module What it did
Profile and Tag Visible to all members. Tag and profile photo always public; other fields could be made friends-only
Friends Mutual friend requests; refusals were silent; blocking available
Shouts One-to-many messages "somewhere between a SMS and an email"
Pix Gallery of up to 20 photos, uploaded by MMS to pix@spriteyard.mobi; friends could comment
Scribbles Discussion board for a hand-picked group of up to 10 friends
Planner "Where you are going to be… for the next two weeks", with invitations to join
Nuggets Enter a cap PIN to download mobisodes, visitones (visual ringtones), wallpapers and games

Greatrex described it as a "PG-13 experience" (RCR Wireless, Jun 9 2007). Only the downloads needed a Sprite: "For all these functions outside of the downloadable digital content you don't actually need a Sprite" (DeVilliers, SHOOTonline).

Name. Coke's own materials say "Sprite Yard" or "The Yard". "Sprite Yards" (plural, this entry's slug) comes from Carol Kruse's December 2007 NYT quote (Ocala Star-Banner reprint).

Official vs unofficial spaces. Everything happened inside the walled garden. You "have to be a registered member of the Yard to be able to view any part of the site" (FAQ). I found no evidence of fan forums, subreddits or unofficial spaces: Reddit (via Brave), Hacker News and general web searches turned up no member discussion. Laurel Papworth observed at launch that the chatter was "mostly from marketing people, not teens" (laurelpapworth.com, Jun 21 2007).

Status: defunct. No shutdown was ever announced. Evidence for the end date is in the Wayback section below.

Origin and strategy

  • Mandate. "The company charged the Sprite brand with pioneering the mobile space." The team pitched going "beyond promotion for the simple reason of sustainable connections", and the platform was meant to become "a competitive edge for the company for other brands to use" (DeVilliers, Director of Global Brand Management, Sprite, SHOOTonline).
  • Champions. Mark Greatrex, SVP Marketing Communications and Insights, unveiled it in his keynote at the Mobile Marketing Association's Mobile Marketing Forum in New York on June 6, 2007 (press release; MMA). Denis Sison, Sprite Global Brand Director, later listed "led the development of Coca-Cola's first mobile marketing platform the 'Sprite Yard'" in his speaker bio (ANA). On the agency side, Isobar's James Andrews was "Global Account Director and quarterback on 'The Sprite Yard'" for "a little over a year" before he left for Ketchum at the start of 2008 (KeyInfluencer).
  • Why teens and why mobile. "We know that when it comes to reaching teens, mobile is the medium… putting Sprite both in their hand and in their phone" (Sison, press release). Greatrex wanted "an omnipresent, on-the-go, everywhere relationship with teens" (NYT via Gainesville Sun). Sprite is Coke's "second-largest trademark and particularly popular with African-American teens" (Greatrex, RCR). Beverage Digest's John Sicher linked it to Coke "looking for innovative ways to generate loyalty" as consumers drifted to water, tea and energy drinks (NYT).
  • Tie to the business. Purchase frequency came through cap PINs ("a fresh, engaging experience with each purchase"). Coke also gained first-party data: phone numbers, dates of birth, and account linking with "MyCokeRewards, Coke Studios, coke.com, Powerade" (FAQ). Coke told carriers the service was good for them "because it will encourage people to sign up for data plans" (RCR).
  • Scale of ambition. It was described as "not an in and out promotion… a campaign that we will build and enhance over time" (Greatrex, MediaPost, quoted by thinkd2c). Coke promised that "by the end of 2008, the Sprite Yard will be introduced in several additional markets globally" (press release). China and the US together are over half of Sprite's sales (Adweek, Jun 6 2007). Coke said it was "in touch with other social networks such as Facebook about expanding its experience" (Reuters, via thinkd2c).
  • Portfolio context. The Yard was one of several Coke youth-community bets in 2007. Kruse said CC Metro, the There.com district that replaced Coke Studios that December, "is supposed to complement Coke's efforts on Facebook as well as Sprite Yards" (NYT reprint, Dec 7 2007). See the mycoke, cc-metro and my-coke-rewards reports.

Asked about China results at launch, DeVilliers said: "We've had some early qualitative feedback… but we're waiting for official reports. It's too early to say" (SHOOTonline). What keeps people coming back

The design used four loops. I found no evidence that any of them worked.

  1. Collectible content behind cap codes. Mobisodes were "part of a collectible series, so consumers will want to buy another Sprite to see the next episode". The first series followed two Lemon and Lime characters who "escaped from a juice bar in downtown New York" (DeVilliers, SHOOTonline). This was the only mechanic tied to buying the product, and the one most likely to bring people back. It is also a promotion mechanic, and it works without any community at all. Users couldn't even pick their reward: the FAQ has an entry "Can I choose what I get when I use my code to download?" (FAQ).
  2. Social graph and messaging. Friends, Shouts, Pix comments and the Planner copied the MySpace/Facebook loop of profile, friends and notifications. The Planner's two-week "where I'll be" view and invitations were the only feature that pointed to meeting up offline.
  3. Small-group intimacy. Scribbles let members choose "who's in and who's out", up to 10 friends. That is closer to today's group chats than to a public forum, a design choice ahead of its time.
  4. Identity. Tag names, profiles, avatars (Shaping Youth, Jun 2007) and Sprite's "Lymon"/"Sublymonal"/"OBEY" vocabulary (sprite.com/yard footer).

Missing pieces: no streaks, levels, badges, leaderboards, events, hosts, recognition programs or seasons show up in any source. Switching costs were close to zero because the friend graph had to be rebuilt from scratch and stayed locked inside the Yard. Every visit cost the teen (or their parents) carrier data fees: "Your carrier will usually charge you for accessing the internet on your mobile phone" (FAQ).

The content engine

  • What Coke produced: mobisodes (animated shorts for 2007 handset screens and file sizes), visitones (music plus visuals), wallpapers and games (RCR; mobiForge on Marvellous: "they rewarded purchases with downloads of wallpapers, ringtones and games (both branded and unbranded)").
  • Who made it: Isobar was the lead agency. Molecular handled back-end development and Marvellous, a London mobile agency, made the mobisodes (SHOOTonline). Aegis later bought Marvellous and cited the Yard as their joint work (Campaign, 2007).
  • Cadence promise vs reality: the pitch was speed: "unlike a TV ad… it doesn't take us months to make a piece of content." Yet a week before the US launch the first mobisodes were "in the works and should be ready within five to ten days" (DeVilliers). The brand wanted it "not to be overbranded. We don't want to alienate our teen advocates" (MediaPost via thinkd2c). I found no evidence of how often new content shipped after launch.
  • Partner media: Shaping Youth mentions "'partner' content", and ClickZ mentions partnerships with "carriers, technology providers, and brands like the MBA [sic] and McDonalds in China". MediaPost's Mobile Insider argued that without "interesting media figures and assets apart from the Coke brands, then it is just another corporate WAP site giving crap away. But Coke is no MTV" (quoted by thinkd2c). I found no evidence of major partner content.
  • Member-made content: photos, Scribbles posts and Planner entries only. There were no templates, contests, featured members or reward programs. The terms gave Coke a "worldwide, non-exclusive, transferable, royalty-free, perpetual, irrevocable" license to everything users posted, including "your voice or likeness" (Terms).

People, programs and governance

  • Moderation: a "Report It" link on every page; Coke reviewed reports, removed content and warned the poster. An automatic filter turned profanity into the words LEMON or LIME ("just keep it refreshing") (FAQ). Sison told Reuters that child safety "certainly is a priority" and that photos, video and conversations would be screened (thinkd2c quoting Reuters). A "Responsible Networking" page told members never to share their school or phone number and never to plan meetings with strangers (Wayback).
  • Support: a toll-free Customer Care number (1-866-674-2653) in the FAQ.
  • Community team, hosts, ambassadors or superusers: no evidence found. Nothing suggests community managers, member programs or a feedback loop into the product. The people named are brand managers and agency account leads.

Growth loops

  • On-pack activation instead of media spend: "Awareness will be driven not through media buys, but from PIN codes contained in the bottle caps and viral activity", on the basis of 10 billion single-serve Sprite bottles a year worldwide (ClickZ). Brand in Hand CEO John Hadl's doubt: "Who looked under a coke cap before?" (RCR).
  • Friend invites: "Users build their community by inviting and being accepted by other Yard users as their friends" (press release). This was the only peer-to-peer acquisition mechanism.
  • China retail pilot: PIN codes handed out with purchases at McDonald's (press release).
  • What blocked growth: members-only visibility meant no SEO and no public sharing, and you couldn't use the Yard from a computer. The NYT noted it "will function only on cellphones."

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. Coke published no members, MAU, redemptions, sales lift or cost figures, and its SEC filings never mention "Sprite Yard" (EDGAR full-text search, 0 hits).
  • Company claims: at launch only, "the most comprehensive mobile marketing effort on the planet" and "an efficient, sustainable and fresh marketing platform" (Greatrex). There were no follow-up claims, which is itself telling for a program announced in a keynote.
  • Third-party verdicts: Digiday's "That effort fizzled" (Jan 2013). In the same piece Coke's Tom Daly said its working mobile channels were "SMS, mobile Web, apps, video, location and on-package barcode activations", that brands should "simplify", and that "one-off efforts don't work". He also admitted Coke had "well over 100 apps, and only a handful are really good."
  • Academic: the Yard appears as a case of food marketing to children in Linn & Novosat, "Calories for Sale" (Annals AAPSS, 2008), and as an Italian Bocconi working paper, "Socialità e Consumo: il Caso Sprite Yard" (Stefano Pace, 2007; IRIS, no abstract online). Neither reports outcomes that I could see.
  • Scores: business_impact_evidence 1 because there is no outcome data of any kind; durability 1 because it lasted about 15–18 months with no successor.

What they did right

  1. Built for the device teens actually carried. In 2007 this was early thinking, and Forrester's Christine Overby called it "a comprehensive commitment as opposed to the toe-dipping we've seen… from other marketers" (NYT via Gainesville Sun).
  2. Took safety seriously for a teen audience. It had a members-only network, a friends-only default for most pages, silent friend refusals, blocking, Report It on every page, a playful on-brand profanity filter (LEMON/LIME) and a plain-language safety page. These are good patterns for any youth community (FAQ; Responsible Networking).
  3. Linked the purchase to a collectible, made-for-format reward. Episodic content made specifically for small screens, unlocked per bottle, is a sound frequency mechanic. DeVilliers noted teens "appreciate the fact it was made for the small screen."
  4. Kept the core free and lightly branded. Social features didn't require a purchase and the content wasn't meant to be "overbranded", which avoided an obvious pay-to-play trap.
  5. Treated it as a platform to learn from. Adam Broitman argued that judged as an experiment yielding "useable learnings" it couldn't fail (blog, Jul 2007). Coke's later mobile doctrine (simplify, use SMS and on-pack codes, integrate rather than stand alone) reads like those lessons (Digiday).

What they did wrong

  1. Asked people to move their social life into a brand's space. Stoller: "Nobody wants to go hang out with Sprite… You really have to be getting something compelling in return." Mobile Insider: "Aligning a corporate manufacturer with personal communications on the most intimate device we have feels like a very hard sell." Consequence: no evidence of organic member activity, and no member voices survive.
  2. Competed head-on with Facebook and MySpace. The NYT flagged Facebook's mobile site as a competitor at launch. Shaping Youth asked "Am I missing the 'value-add' here?" when Facebook, Helio and Virgin Mobile already served mobile teens (Shaping Youth). Coke said it was talking to Facebook about expanding there but built a walled garden anyway. Papworth: "I doubt that a walled garden is the way to go though. Have they ever worked?"
  3. Put the cost of joining on the member. It required a WAP phone, a data plan and MMS charges, with registration only on your own handset. Arnold Worldwide's Jonathan Sackett warned of "trouble if teenagers run up high data charges", and MediaVest's Yaakov Kimelfeld cited "slow and costly data transfers" and "poorly designed WAP sites" as barriers (iMedia via leavcom). A 20-photo cap and 10-friend Scribbles made it feel small next to MySpace.
  4. Relied on bottle caps for distribution and made content invisible to outsiders. Nothing was shareable or public, so there was no SEO, no shareable links and no web presence beyond a help page.
  5. Overpromised, then went silent. It was billed as "not an in and out promotion" with "several additional markets" by end of 2008, but no expansion, metrics or closure notice ever appeared. The help pages were absorbed into My Coke Rewards by September 2008, and the agency's global account lead had moved on by early 2008. Consequence: no public learning, and Coke's later youth efforts (CC Metro, Facebook) started fresh.
  6. Owned too much and handed members too little. A perpetual, irrevocable license over user content, a promotional data purpose (phone number, date of birth, cross-program account linking), and no member recognition or co-creation gave teens little sense that the space was theirs. Child-advocacy critics framed it as "invasive corporate commando tactics" (Shaping Youth) and as food marketing to children (Linn & Novosat, 2008).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Tie a collectible, episodic reward to real product use. "Buy another to see the next episode" is a good frequency hook, as long as the reward is made for the format and a little surprising.
  2. Default to small, private groups. Scribbles (you pick up to 10 people) and the Planner foreshadowed group chats and event invites, the social features that last.
  3. Design youth safety in from day one, and make it on-brand: friends-only defaults, silent declines, blocking, Report It everywhere, a playful filter, a plain-language safety page.
  4. Keep the social core free of purchase gates; let purchases add extras.
  5. Write down what you learn and publish it. Coke's later "simplify, integrate, don't do one-offs" doctrine is the most valuable thing the Yard left behind. Capture lessons deliberately instead of letting a program fade.

Avoid these

  1. Don't build a parallel social network your users must move their friends into. Go where the graph already is (Facebook then; group chats and Discord now), or offer something those networks can't.
  2. Don't make members pay to show up (data charges, special hardware, account friction), and don't hide everything behind a login. Public, shareable surfaces are your growth loop.
  3. Don't launch a "permanent platform" with keynote fanfare and no plan for content cadence, community staff or published success metrics. Without them it dies silently, and nobody learns why.
