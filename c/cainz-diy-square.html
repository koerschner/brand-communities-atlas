How it pays off for the company

Community IDs are linked to CAINZ loyalty IDs. CAINZ reports that members spend 1.8x the average CAINZ member per year and top-rank (Rank 5) members 5.4x, with a before/after-registration lift. The site also feeds paid in-store workshop bookings, supplier and private-label monitor campaigns, and UGC reused in e-commerce and store media.

TL;DR

  • What it is: CAINZ, a Japanese home-centre chain with about 260 stores, runs CAINZ DIY Square, an online community at diy-square.cainz.com. Members post finished projects, step-by-step "recipes", questions and daily chat. It is tied to paid workshops held in about 70 stores. It soft-launched with 10 hand-picked members in July 2021, opened to the public in November 2021, reached about 43,000 members by September 2024 and passed 100,000 in October 2025 [2][4][8].
  • Why people come back: praise and company. Members say their families shrug at their projects while the community applauds them [7]. On top of that sits a points-and-rank game that resets every October 1. Ranks are earned through daily logins, posts, likes and workshop attendance, and the top rank gets a non-sale "Platinum Apron" handed over in a store [15][16]. Online friendships also turn into meetups at workshops [5][7].
  • What the company feeds it: a staff team of three plus about 15 "DIY Captains" (expert employees) who answer questions online and teach workshops. In the first year the team liked or commented on every single post. It also runs monthly hashtag themes, staff columns, supplier "monitor" campaigns and periodic sign-up point campaigns [4][5][17].
  • Biggest weakness: the evidence and the engagement are both concentrated. The 1.8x and 5.4x spend figures are self-reported and selection-prone. Membership more than doubled in eight months, yet talk-room posting rose only about 2x over four years. In a one-week sample, about 90 people wrote all 300 talk posts and 12 of them wrote about 40%. Members also keep hitting technical friction on the vendor platform, such as logouts in the CAINZ app's built-in browser and slow buttons [24][26].
  • The one lesson: start tiny with your warmest customers, make sure every early post gets a human reply, and make status count somewhere physical (a workshop discount, an apron handed over in person). Then link community IDs to purchase data so you can show the business case without pushing members toward sales.

What the community is

CAINZ DIY Square is two things under one name. The first is a physical "DIY Square" workshop space, first opened in the Makuhari store on July 21, 2021. The second is the online community site, which opened to the public on November 1-2, 2021 [2][13]. The 2021 launch release calls it "a place where stores and online are fused", built around four ideas: ask (きいて), make (つくって), connect (つながって) and enjoy (たのしむ) [2]. Today "CAINZ DIY Square" usually means the online site. It runs on coorum, a white-label community platform from the Tokyo startup Asobica that CAINZ adopted in July 2021 [11]. Members can sign in with their existing CAINZ app or online-shop ID [21].

Spaces on the site today [1][17]:

  • Work Gallery (作品ギャラリー), split since the October 2025 redesign into DIY, Green (gardening), Cooking, Pet, Workshop and Other, plus seasonal menus such as "Autumn/Winter Kitchen Garden 2026".
  • Recipe Notes (レシピノート): how-tos with a fixed template (materials, steps, tips).
  • Ask Everyone (みんなに質問): Q&A with best answers and, since October 2025, "Meister" badges.
  • Talk Plaza (トーク広場): general chat, including a category called "Tell us, Captain!" (教えて！キャプテン) where staff answer.
  • Circles: a Dog Circle organised by breed (2026).
  • DIY Log: a private diary and collection of what you made or bought, which you can share.
  • Company-run areas: announcements, columns, supplier monitor menus (for example Calpis and sodium percarbonate in 2026), "green arrivals" stock news, and a help and inquiries board.

There are no meaningful unofficial spaces. Searches found no subreddit or fan forum; the Arctic Shift Reddit archive has nothing on CAINZ, and academic databases have nothing either. The community is entirely company-owned. Status: active and growing, celebrating its fifth anniversary in October-December 2026 [21][22].

Origin and strategy

  • Parent program: in November 2019 CAINZ launched the "DIYer 1 Million Project" (DIYer100万人プロジェクト). The goal was 1 million people a year touching DIY through CAINZ (workshops, the CAINZ Kobo workshop spaces, certain purchases) by 2024 [3]. CAINZ defines DIY very broadly: "everything you try yourself to make life better", including cooking, gardening, camping and making plum wine [4][14]. The community is one tactic under that program. I found no public evidence on whether the 1M target was reached.
  • Digital transformation: CEO Masayuki Takaie's "IT retailer declaration" (2018-19) put digital touchpoints at the centre of strategy [12][14]. In a February 2022 interview Takaie said the community's KPIs are deliberately not financial. They track how many entry users join, become beginners and then become "LOVERs" [14].
  • The internal reason was customer understanding. In stores, CAINZ could not see what customers did after a purchase or a workshop: how a workshop piece was used at home, or what a "turn my Japanese room into a Western room" project actually looked like [4][10].
  • Why coorum: Keiko Shibuya, who launched the community and is now group manager of the DIY Community Group, said the deciding factor was ID linkage. Members must not need a separate account, and CAINZ wanted to join community behaviour to purchase data for PDCA (plan-do-check-act) cycles [10].
  • Champions: Keiko Shibuya (career CAINZ marketer, formerly in private-label advertising) and Mr. Hinogami of the community team [5][6][10]. The team sits in the 面白事業部 ("Omoshiro Division"), a joint unit CAINZ formed in November 2022 with the creative company Kayac, under the Community Platform Department [6][30].
  • Operating principles (Senden Kaigi, 2023): be polite to every user, never compromise the corporate value of "Kindness", and do no advertising inside the community [12]. Posts about other companies' products are explicitly allowed [5][12].
  • The only formal KPI is member count. Shibuya: "Rushing to monetise a community is fatal" [5]. Spend figures came later, as proof rather than as targets [4].

Trajectory. Membership growth sped up sharply in 2025, from 45k in February to 100k+ in October. That came with sign-up campaigns worth up to 3,000 DIY points and app-ID login [8][21]. Posting grew more slowly and steadily. The mix also shifted. Gardening and cooking now outpace woodworking, and the woodworking (DIY) gallery has slowed slightly since 2024. That fits CAINZ's broad definition of DIY. It also means the hardcore-DIY identity in the name is no longer what drives most activity.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Recognition from peers, at scale. The most-used feature is the Work Gallery. Shibuya says home DIYers "rarely get praised by anyone"; here people across Japan see and praise their work [4]. Members tell staff their families sigh "you're making things again?", while the community responds with likes and questions, and "that is the biggest motivation to continue" [7]. A survey on the About page lists these top attractions: friends with the same hobby, "kind comments and a positive atmosphere", variety, and "fun of climbing the ranks" [23].
  2. A guaranteed reply at launch. For about the first year, staff liked or commented on every post [4][5]. The 10 core members modelled the same behaviour for newcomers. Shibuya calls this the "psychological safety" of knowing someone will always respond [5].
  3. A seasonal points game with real-world prizes [15][16]:
    • Points come from daily logins (20/day), posts (10-30), comments (3), likes (1), best answers (30), referrals (100, up to 10 a month) and, worth the most, attending a paid in-store workshop of ¥1,000+ (500 points). The workshop must be paid for with the CAINZ card or app presented at checkout.
    • Five ranks. Rank 3+ unlocks workshop discount coupons (¥300 / ¥500 / ¥1,000) and logo stickers. At season end, Rank 3-5 members get CAINZ points (500 / 1,000 / 4,000) and CAINZ Select products. Rank 4-5 get community-only goods (a 2023 mobile battery chosen by member vote). Rank 5 gets the non-sale Platinum Apron [20].
    • Everything resets to zero every October 1, which CAINZ named "CAINZ DIY Square Day". That turns status into an annual season and pulls people back each year. Prizes are handed over in person at a workshop venue, so the reward itself causes a store visit [15][20].
    • Members celebrate hitting Rank 5 in public threads, and say the apron kept them going [27].
  4. Expert badges (since October 2025). Being chosen as best answer five times in a category earns a "Meister" badge, with extra stars at 10, 20 and 30. Unlike points, these never reset [19].
  5. Online-to-offline bonds. Members write their community nickname on workshop name tags. Others recognise them ("are you the ○○ from the community?"), then they have tea at the in-store café and plan "CAINZ expeditions" to workshops in other prefectures [5][7]. Staff report members who log in all 365 days, one member who went from zero workshops to 100+ a year, and members who now host workshops at home [4]. A member told staff: "I never imagined I'd make new friends at my age" [4]. Shibuya credits CAINZ's physical stores for making these meetups feel safe [7].
  6. Light monthly rituals. "This month's Megaphone" announces themed hashtags each month [17]. There are seasonal menus (the 2026 kitchen garden, the "Wan Matsuri" dog festival) and anniversary campaigns.
  7. Product hooks. Reply notifications were added at CAINZ's request because members were missing replies [10]. Members can add the site to their phone's home screen, and links lead to CAINZ Reserve for booking workshops [13][17].

The content engine

  • Mostly member-made. About 110k gallery posts, 5.4k recipes and 69k talk posts. The recipe template creates a "chain of posts": a comment asks "how did you make it?", the maker posts a recipe, then someone posts "I made it with ○○'s recipe" [4].
  • Company content is light and steady:
    • 139 staff columns and announcements [24].
    • The "100 STYLES, 100 DIYers" series of employee profiles (hobbies, favourite CAINZ products), which gives the brand a human face (e.g., #47, Feb 2023) [32].
    • Captain answers in "Tell us, Captain!"
    • Monthly themes, and green-plant stock alerts by store.
  • Owned media around it: CAINZ's magazine "Tonari no CAINZ-san" (4M+ monthly page views about a year after launch), YouTube how-tos, and the CAINZ DIY Style site. The 2021 recipe section reused content from these [13][14].
  • Supplier and private-label monitors: manufacturers post usage tips and answer questions in the community, which led to purchases. A CAINZ private-label snack monitor collected member recipes that were reused on the e-commerce site and in store media [9]. Asobica's coorum research tool powers DIY Log and also gathers "zero-party" data (what customers choose to tell the company) [9][34].
  • Rights: the guidelines give CAINZ the copyright in posts and broad reuse rights across flyers, e-commerce, social media and press [18]. Members may not post every step of CAINZ's own workshop designs, which protects the paid workshops [18].

People, programs and governance

  • Team: three community staff, about 15 DIY Captains each covering a region's workshops, plus buyers and other departments as needed (Sept 2024) [4]. CAINZ introduced DIY Captains in May 2021 as "embodiments of CAINZ DIY". The CEO likened them to Starbucks baristas, picked for sociability and love of DIY [14][29].
  • Core members: CAINZ chose the 10 founding members from frequent workshop attendees and Instagram-campaign regulars, with input from store staff. The criteria were that they love CAINZ and are comfortable online. They met monthly to shape campaigns [4]. One of them later joined CAINZ as a part-time employee [4].
  • No formal ambassador or MVP program beyond ranks and Meister badges. Members do co-create in smaller ways: they vote on rank goods [20], and their requests have changed operations, such as a single-store workshop rolled out to every store after members asked for it [10].
  • Moderation: written guidelines (no defamation, no personal information, no luring members to other sites or SNS, no commercial posts), plus member reports. Posts that collect enough reports can be removed automatically [18]. Staff say the site "never flames", which surprises people inside the company; they credit the real-world brand and the everyday-life (not show-off) tone of posts [5].
  • Feedback loop: a public Inquiries board (609 threads) where staff reply on points, workshops and bugs, often within days [26]. Shibuya expects buyers to use the community for product development [10]. Hinogami warns: "judge calmly whether a request is something the company should do; don't let the company's stance waver" [5].

Growth loops

  • Store-staff recruitment: workshop staff invited attendees, starting with a few stores in September-October 2021 and then every store from November [4][7].
  • Workshop stamp card → community: one Rank-5 member asked staff what regulars do after filling the five-stamp workshop card, and was pointed to the Square [27].
  • Sign-up campaigns: 1,000 points plus a toolbox raffle for 100 people (Nov 2021) [2]; up to 3,000 points (Oct 2025) [8]; referral points [15].
  • Digital promotion and the CAINZ app once the community was healthy [4][7].
  • SEO and sharing: the site is public and indexed, with about 100k monthly visitors including non-members [6], and posts have share buttons [17].
  • Peer push into first purchases: non-attendees saw workshop posts, members encouraged them to go, they got hooked and bought tools [5].

Evidence of business impact

  • Company claims, from linked IDs: community members' annual spend is 1.8x the CAINZ-member average, and Rank 5 members' is 5.4x. The gap grows with activity level [4][7]. Comparing spend before and after registration also showed a "large difference", which CAINZ presents as its answer to the internal objection that "they were fans already" [4]. Asobica's release words the same 1.8x as "vs non-members" [9]. The comparison group is described inconsistently.
  • Caveats: all figures are self-reported, with no methodology, time window or control group published. The 5.4x for Rank 5 is circular to a degree, because workshop attendance (paid) is the biggest single source of points. CAINZ is private (Beisia Group), so there are no filings to check.
  • Qualitative claims: buyers and other departments now ask to use the community for monitors and campaigns [4]; supplier Q&A led to purchases; UGC is reused in commerce [9]; one member went from zero to 100+ workshops a year [4].
  • Hard evidence: none independent. No academic study was found.

What they did right

  1. Started closed and small. Ten trusted members for three months, then a few stores, then all stores. Shibuya calls not rushing growth "very important" [4][5].
  2. Answered every post for a year. This set the norm of warm replies, which members now keep up themselves [4][5].
  3. Physical stakes for digital status. Points come mostly from workshops, and rewards (stickers, coupons, Platinum Apron) are collected in person [15][20]. Each loop drives a store visit, and the annual reset brings members back each season.
  4. Broad definition of DIY and no ad pushing. Gardening, cooking and pets are welcome, and so are competitors' products [5][12]. That widened the audience and made posts more trustworthy. Gardening is now the largest gallery [25].
  5. Data wiring from day one. Choosing a platform for single sign-on and loyalty-ID linkage let the team prove value with spend data instead of arguing for it [10].
  6. Celebrating the community itself. A free fan event at headquarters, thank-you campaigns, and member-voted merchandise [20][21][22].

What they did wrong

  1. Member count as the only KPI. That rewards sign-up bribes (up to 3,000 points) over activity. Membership jumped about 2.2x in eight months while posting rose modestly [8][24]. The reported 100k probably includes many app-ID accounts that never post (inference).
  2. A thin, concentrated active core. In a one-week sample, about 90 people wrote all 300 talk posts and 12 wrote about 40% [24]. Three staff for 100k members leaves the culture depending on a few heavy users.
  3. Gamification side effects. Points for logins and likes invite point-farming. One member said she held off buying an apron she wanted while chasing the free Platinum Apron (May 2022) [27]. Rewards depend on paperwork: presenting the app at checkout, surveys to pick the pickup store, and coupon rules that confuse even members [15][20][26].
  4. Platform friction. Recent inquiries describe unexpected logouts and a blank login screen inside the CAINZ app's own built-in browser. Staff advised switching to Chrome, and one five-year member said this was the first they had heard Chrome was the recommended browser. Others report slow category buttons, points not credited, and a coupon that wouldn't apply (Oct-Nov 2026) [26][27]. The whole community depends on one vendor's platform.
  5. Store reality bleeds in. Closures (the founding Isesaki store, 2023) and members' complaints about service at their local store show up in the talk room [27]. The community can only be as warm as the stores behind it.
  6. DIY identity drift. Woodworking posting has flattened while gardening and cooking grew [25]. That is fine for engagement, but core DIY is the category where workshop and tool spend is highest.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Seed with 10 people you already know love you, run it closed for a quarter, and expand one channel at a time [4].
  2. Make sure every post gets a reply in year one, from staff and seeded core members. The norm you set early is the culture you keep [5].
  3. Tie digital status to a real-world or in-product payoff, and make the most valuable action the one that matters most to the business (here, attending a paid workshop). Hand top rewards over in person.
  4. Run status as seasons with an annual reset and a named day, so lapsed members have a fresh start and regulars have a reason to return.
  5. Use one login linked to your customer ID from launch, so you can show spend before and after joining instead of debating it.

Avoid these

  1. Don't make member count the only KPI, and don't buy sign-ups with points. Track active contributors and how concentrated they are.
  2. Don't let gamification stand in for, or cannibalise, the purchase (apron chasing), or turn rewards into admin.
  3. Don't treat the community platform as an afterthought. Embedding it badly (an in-app browser that logs people out) frustrates exactly the daily users you most need.

Score justifications

  • Retention mechanics 4: a rich, physically anchored points season plus peer recognition and meetups; no published cohort retention data.
  • Content engine 3: strong member-made content and templates; company output is modest (139 columns, monthly themes).
  • Member ownership 3: core members shaped the launch and vote on merchandise, but there is no formal ambassador or moderator role for members.
  • Staff investment 3: an intense first year, but only three staff plus about 15 Captains for 100k members.
  • Business impact evidence 3: specific linked-ID spend multiples, but self-reported with no method published.
  • Durability 4: five years of steady growth with executive backing; depends on one vendor and on a small core.
