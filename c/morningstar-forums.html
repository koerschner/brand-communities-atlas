TL;DR

  • What it is. Morningstar opened free "Conversations" forums on Morningstar.com in June 1997. After members lobbied for it, it added a Vanguard Diehards forum in March 1998. By October 2001 that forum had passed 100,000 posts and was the busiest forum on the site. Its members, led by retired volunteers Taylor Larimore and Mel Lindauer, became the Bogleheads. In February 2007 they left to start an independent, ad-free forum, which became Bogleheads.org. Morningstar's forums declined through redesigns in 2007, 2019 and 2021, went login-only in March 2023, and were reported shut on December 20 (2023).
  • Why people came back. Not because of anything Morningstar built. Members returned for a shared creed (low cost, indexing, "stay the course"), for tireless volunteer answerers (Larimore spent "an average of six hours a day" answering questions), for a member-written starter guide, and for the participation of their hero, Jack Bogle, who read the forum "several times a week". From 2000, annual in-person reunions turned an online forum into a friendship network.
  • What Morningstar did to feed it. Not much. It created the forum, but only after "much controversy". It employed a part-time community coordinator and moderator, and managing director Don Phillips posted congratulations. Morningstar hosted the third reunion (Chicago, 2002) and ran one staff article about the group. It did not supply real moderation, modern software, or any ownership for members.
  • The biggest mistake. It let one persistent troll disrupt "thread after thread after thread" for years. Its answer to banned users coming back was a $5 posting fee rather than moderators. Seven months after the walkout, it shipped a redesign that broke every link. Each step pushed the core members toward an exit they could build themselves for almost nothing.
  • The one lesson. If your best members are already building infrastructure around your community (indexes, FAQs, meetups), they are telling you what to give them. Give them moderation powers, a say in changes and a stake, or they will take the community with them, and the brand name with it.

What the community is

Morningstar, the Chicago fund-research firm, ran free threaded discussion boards on Morningstar.com for about 26 years:

Era Platform and URL Notes
Jun 1997–Sep 2007 "Conversations" (socialize.morningstar.com, /NewSocialize/asp/FullConv.asp) "Investment Basics" was the first forum (June 1997); topic and fund-family forums followed (Larimore, 2001)
Sep 2007–2019 "Discuss" (socialize./discuss.morningstar.com/NewSocialize) Rebuilt with blogs, "Experts" blogs, portfolio sharing, tags and thumbs-up/down voting (Wayback, Dec 2008)
May 2019–c. 2021 community.morningstar.com/t5 (Khoros/Lithium) Kudos, ranks, a "Community Manager" (RyanM); old posts kept only for Bogleheads Unite and Vanguard forums
c. Feb 2021–Dec 2023 community.morningstar.com/s (Salesforce) Free accounts gone, "only true Morningstar users can post" (Feb 2021); posts visible only to logged-in users from March 2023

The Vanguard Diehards forum (later subtitled and then renamed "Bogleheads® Unite") was one of its forums, and the reason this one matters. The forum list covered fund families (Fidelity, TIAA-CREF, American Funds, PIMCO, T. Rowe Price), stocks (Stock Picks, Income & Dividend Investing, Warren Buffett Investing), and general topics (Bond Squad, Investing During Retirement, Off Topic). There was also a notorious "Janus Junction", which Morningstar once threatened to close for a "cooling off period" because it had turned so combative (Zweig, Money, Sept 2001).

Unofficial spaces. In July 2001, members Alex Frakt and Larry Auton launched diehards.org, a search and post index for the Morningstar forum. In February 2007 it became the host of the breakaway forum, and in May 2008 it took the name Bogleheads.org (Bogleheads wiki). Later refugees from the wider Morningstar forums went to Big Bang! Investors (ProBoards, tagline "Recreating the spirit of 'Morningstar Discuss' during its 2000-2010 glory days"), Mutual Fund Observer's board, and YBB Personal Finance (Bogleheads, Feb 2024; Big Bang! Investors).

Status today: defunct. Members reported that "the Morningstar Forums shut down on December 20" in a thread opened February 3, 2024 (Bogleheads). The Bogleheads Unite topic and the discussion-categories pages on community.morningstar.com now load only a Salesforce shell or a Morningstar login (checked via r.jina.ai, Nov 7, 2026). I found no Morningstar announcement of the closure, so the year is inferred from the thread date (medium confidence).

Origin and strategy

Members asked for the forum. Morningstar didn't plan it. By Larimore's account, Vanguard shareholders on the general forums "began pestering Jennie Barrie, Community Coordinator, for a separate Vanguard Forum. Morningstar resisted. They doubted that there would be enough response for a single company forum… after much controversy Morningstar finally relented." Members voted for "Bogleheads" in a naming contest. Barrie chose "Vanguard Diehards" instead, because diehards are "persons who vigorously promote a hopeless position" (Larimore, Oct 31, 2001). Lindauer recalls that Morningstar thought "Bogleheads" was derogatory and didn't want to upset Bogle, so the forum got the subtitle "Bogleheads Unite, Talk About Your Favorite Fund Company" (Bogle Center podcast ep. 11, c. 2019, transcript).

Business logic. Morningstar never described the forums' purpose in its SEC filings beyond one sentence. In 2007 it "improved the conversations area of the site to better highlight user-generated content," listed among efforts "to help draw investors deeper into Morningstar.com." The same 10-K says Morningstar.com's revenue came from Premium subscriptions and "Internet advertising space", and that it had 5.8 million free registered users and 180,366 Premium subscribers (Morningstar 10-K for 2007). The forums sat in that funnel: posting required free registration, and the pages carried ads. A member who left in 2007 complained that Morningstar "runs a ton of ads on the forum but does not invest anything back to the community" (The Finance Buff, 2007). No internal champion is visible beyond the coordinators (Jennie Barrie; then Carl Sibilski, "the moderator of the forum," who arranged Diehards III) and Don Phillips' goodwill.

Trajectory. The forums grew fast from 1998 to about 2006. The Vanguard Diehards forum lost its core within weeks of February 2007, and the wider forums faded after the redesigns of 2007, 2019 and 2021. Peak activity was probably 2000–2006. I found no member counts or DAU for Morningstar's forums. The 2015 post totals are cumulative and include the posts migrated in 2007.

What keeps people coming back

Nearly every retention mechanic here came from members. Morningstar's own contributions were thin.

  • A creed and an identity. Members called themselves "Diehards" and "Bogleheads", joked that they held a "hopeless position", and shared one philosophy: "asset-allocation, low-cost, indexing, tax-efficiency, and simplicity" (Larimore 2001). Jason Zweig put it this way: "By singing in harmony from the same page of the same investing hymnal, the Diehards drown out market noise" (quoted in Bogleheads t=7141).
  • Civility as the one rule. "We pride ourselves in being friendly, courteous, and civil. This is the only requirement for being a Diehard. Most of us use friendly real names… We try hard to answer every question" (Larimore 2001). Members enforced this themselves, because Morningstar didn't.
  • Star volunteers who answered everything. Larimore averaged six hours a day answering questions and Lindauer one to five; "No question is considered too stupid" (Zweig 2001). Authors Larry Swedroe (3,000+ posts), Bill Bernstein, Bill Schultheis, Frank Armstrong and Michael LeBoeuf took part (Larimore 2001).
  • A founder figure in the room. Bogle "logs on to their forum several times a week" and recognized screen names in person ("Bonesly! I know you!"). He dedicated a 2001 book to "those 'Bogleheads' of the internet, that dedicated and loyal cadre of Vanguard Diehards" (Zweig 2001; Larimore 2001).
  • Rituals. These included a Thanksgiving thread (1999, the post that led to the first meetup), contests with prizes such as an autographed Bogle book (one "set a Morningstar record for Replies"), milestone celebrations (100,000 posts), and from 2000 the annual Diehards reunions with Bogle (Larimore 2001; Lindauer).
  • Peer support beyond money. Members sent 25 "bon voyage" replies when Larimore left on a cruise. When a poster with terminal cancer asked for help planning for his family, "dozens of posts poured in from the Diehards". Ann Brennan called it "a defining moment for me" (Zweig 2001).
  • Company-built hooks (minor). Users could link a shared Morningstar portfolio to their profile, shown as "the little briefcase next to some the user names that show portfolios," which one member still missed years later (t=73131). The 2007 Discuss rebuild added thumbs voting, "Highest Rated" sorting, tags, member blogs and portfolio sharing (Wayback 2008). The 2019 Khoros version added kudos and ranks. None of these is credited anywhere with bringing people back.

The content engine

  • Morningstar's content sat next to the forums but wasn't wired into them. Morningstar.com published fund and stock analyst reports, articles and (from 2007) video (10-K). Some forum descriptions promised you could "talk to other investors and Morningstar analysts" (Wayback 2015), and the 2007 platform added "Experts" blogs. I found no evidence of a regular staff presence, office hours or editorial features drawn from the forums. The one exception is Sue Stevens' 2001 article "Bogleheads Transcend Cyberspace" (cited in Larimore 2001).
  • Members wrote the canon. Larimore and Lindauer co-wrote "Your First Step", a pinned starter post, because they were "answering the same questions repeatedly" (Lindauer, t=7141). The forum kept its own "Archives" and a reading list (Larimore 2001). Members built diehards.org's index and search (2001). Larimore, Lindauer and LeBoeuf turned the forum's wisdom into The Bogleheads' Guide to Investing (Wiley, 2006). Morningstar curated and rewarded none of this.
  • Earned media. Morningstar told members the Diehards were "the most quoted forum in the financial press" (Larimore 2001). Money magazine ran a five-page feature in 2001 (Zweig), and Forbes and Kiplinger later retold the story (Kiplinger 2016).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. A "Community Coordinator" (Jennie Barrie, later Carl Sibilski, who also moderated Diehards), forum "board monitors" such as MDanielleK (mid-2000s), a product lead, M_Darrin, who announced the 2007 upgrade, and in 2019 a "Community Manager", RyanM (t=741; t=5662; Morningstar Community snippet, Jul 2019). Team size isn't known, but the record suggests one or two people for dozens of forums.
  • No superuser program. The de facto leaders (Larimore, Lindauer, Lynn C., Luahiwa) had no moderator powers, title or perks. Lindauer said later that he joined out of a wish for "volunteer work" (podcast).
  • Moderation was reactive and weak. On the Mutual Fund forum in 2005, Morningstar "refused to step in… until it was completely out of control". It let a banned poster re-register, created a new forum (Income & Dividend) just for him, and then brought in "the despised $5 registration fee" to stop banned users from re-registering. He came back anyway under an old name (t=741, Mar 2007). On Diehards, a poster known as "Hocus" hijacked threads for years. The breakaway forum's founder wrote that the forum "came near the precipice because forum management would not deal with one person who trashed thread after thread after thread" (Phoenix, Feb 26, 2007).
  • Feedback loop. There was a Community Feedback forum, and Morningstar answered shareholder questions in monthly 8-Ks. In 2022 a shareholder cited "your forums" as showing "widespread dissatisfaction with the re-write of the portfolio capability" (8-K, Jul 22, 2022). Members said redesigns ignored them: "Mstar didn't solicit member input BEFORE the redesign" (Morningstar Community, "New Design—BAD NEWS", Apr 2020; search snippet, page offline).

Growth loops

  • Morningstar's traffic fed the forum. Larimore credited Morningstar's audience for the forum's quality: Morningstar "attracts knowledgeable investors… this screening process brings more knowledgeable investors to our site" (2001).
  • The forum fed press and books, which fed the forum. Press coverage (Money, 2001), Bogle's dedication and the 2006 book all pointed readers back. After the split, that loop turned against Morningstar. The 2007 paperback edition "mentions this forum and diehards.org extensively… many of the new members here state that they found this site via our book," Lindauer wrote (Sept 2007).
  • SEO went to the successor. When members worried that leaving would cut off new arrivals, the reply was "Google will send them here" (Harry Sit, Sept 2007, same thread).
  • Events. The reunions grew from 22 to 117 people under the Morningstar banner, then became the Bogleheads conferences, about 200 a year and capped at 500 from 2022 (conference history).

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence: none. Morningstar never published forum traffic, ad revenue or Premium conversion from the forums. EDGAR full-text searches of its filings for "discussion forums", "message boards" and "Vanguard Diehards" return nothing. The only filing mentions are the 2007 "user-generated content" sentence (10-K) and the 2022 shareholder question. The circumstantial evidence: Morningstar kept free forums running for about 26 years alongside a growing Premium business (147,010 → 180,366 subscribers, 2005–2007), and called its forum audience "knowledgeable investors". The value the community created (Bogleheads.org, its wiki, conferences, about 100 local chapters, and a 501(c)(3) holding the trademark) went outside Morningstar. Lindauer claims the Bogleheads "have probably driven… billions to Vanguard" (podcast). That is a member's claim with no data behind it.

What they did right

  1. Saying yes (eventually) to a single-company forum. Morningstar doubted a forum about one company would draw enough response and thought it "unfair". That forum then drew more posts than all its other forums combined (Lindauer, podcast). Members asking for their own space is a strong signal.
  2. Hosting a ritual. Inviting the Diehards to hold their third reunion at the 2002 Morningstar Conference, with logistics handled and a panel of Bogle, Bernstein, Swedroe and Don Phillips, gave the group legitimacy. Bogle called it "the smartest panel he had ever been a part of" (conference history).
  3. Visible executive warmth. Don Phillips posted his congratulations at 100,000 posts: "You've made this board a very special place" (Oct 31, 2001). Morningstar ran a staff feature on the group. It cost little and members remembered it.
  4. Keeping the lights on and the archive (partly). Morningstar ran the forums for 26 years, and in 2019 it carried the Bogleheads Unite and Vanguard archives into the new platform. Lindauer and Larimore kept answering there "out of loyalty for all that M* did for us" (Jul 2015).

What they did wrong

  1. Under-moderation of a known troll problem (2005–07). Morningstar was told repeatedly. It responded by creating a forum for the offender and charging a $5 posting fee instead of enforcing rules (t=741). Consequence: on Feb 19, 2007 a member opened a rival forum promising "no trolls… no advertising… no claims of copyright," and the core moved within days (t=201).
  2. Treating members as a content supply, not owners. The forum ran ads and gave little back; the breakaway's founding promises (no ads, "no claims of copyright" on posts, no data sharing) read as a point-by-point contrast. "I don't want to produce content for Morningstar" (Finance Buff). Consequence: members who felt farmed for content had a moral reason, not just a practical one, to leave.
  3. Ignoring the infrastructure members had already built. Members had built their own index site (diehards.org, 2001) because the Morningstar forum was hard to read and navigate. That showed the software was failing, and Morningstar didn't act. Consequence: when they left, the exit already existed, with its own domain, audience and hosts.
  4. A redesign that broke links, seven months after the walkout (Sept 2007). "There's no excuse for breaking links, ever" (t=5662). It was slow on dial-up and confusing. Consequence: "M*, themselves, pounded in the last nail in the coffin" (t=6383). The Bogleheads wiki lists it as a cause of the decline.
  5. Losing the name. Morningstar refused the name "Bogleheads" in 1998. The independent group took it, and its nonprofit trademarked it. By 2015 Morningstar's own forum was labeled "Bogleheads® Unite", carrying a mark it didn't own (Wayback 2015).
  6. Serial platform churn and gating (2019–2023). Morningstar moved to Khoros in 2019, keeping old links and archives only for two forums ("the old Discuss saved links don't work anymore," Jun 2020 snippet). It moved to Salesforce in 2021, when free accounts lost posting rights. It went login-only in March 2023 and closed the forums quietly in December 2023. "Many posters have departed due to repeated format/platform changes and increasingly user-UNfriendly nature of the M* forums" (Mutual Fund Observer, Aug 2022). Consequence: the remaining regulars scattered to Big Bang! Investors, MFO and YBB.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give a passionate sub-group its own room when it asks. Morningstar's best decision was one it argued against. Self-selected niches with a shared creed outperform generic topic boards.
  2. Turn your top answerers' repeated answers into canon. "Your First Step" and the later wiki show that a pinned, member-written starter guide saves volunteers' time and raises the floor for newcomers.
  3. Host the meetup, then get out of the way. Diehards III at the Morningstar Conference cost little and earned lasting goodwill. In-person rituals turned forum regulars into friends, and friends are what kept them coming back.
  4. Put a figure the members admire in the room. Bogle reading and replying gave members a reason to post well. A founder, product lead or outside hero who takes part regularly is a strong retention driver.
  5. Watch what members build around you. An unofficial index, search tool or FAQ points to a gap you should fill, and to the people you should bring inside as moderators.

Avoid these

  1. Under-moderating while monetizing members (ads, a posting fee). One unchecked bad actor plus a feeling of being farmed for content is enough to start an exodus. Hosting is cheap and phpBB is free.
  2. Redesigns that break links and archives without asking members. Each of Morningstar's four platform eras lost a layer of regulars. Its 2007 rebuild landed just as members had a ready alternative.
  3. Keeping the identity at arm's length. Refusing the name members chose ("Bogleheads") meant the identity, and later the trademark, left with them. Let the community own its name inside your house.

Score notes. Retention mechanics 3: strong rituals and identity, almost all built by members, with weak product hooks. Content engine 2: Morningstar's research was nearby but not connected to the forums, and the canon was member-written. Member ownership 5: members built the FAQ, index, events and finally the successor. Staff investment 2: a coordinator, one hosted event and executive goodwill, but no real moderation. Business impact evidence 1: no numbers disclosed anywhere. Durability 1: lost its core in 2007 and closed in 2023, while the community it incubated thrives independently.

Open questions

  • When the $5 posting fee began and ended (members describe it as around 2005–2007), and when "Vanguard Diehards" was formally renamed "Bogleheads Unite".

Related communities worth adding

  • Bogleheads.org — John C. Bogle Center for Financial Literacy (member-run, not company-sponsored) — the successor that became the internet's best-known investing forum; a useful counterfactual for any company forum.
  • Big Bang! Investors — member-run (ProBoards) — refuge for Morningstar Discuss regulars after the 2019–2023 changes.
  • Mutual Fund Observer discussion board — Mutual Fund Observer (successor to FundAlarm) — absorbed Morningstar refugees; FundAlarm's own closure in 2011 is another forum-loss story.
  • Fidelity Community — Fidelity Investments — a brokerage-run customer forum (Morningstar members mention it in 2020).
  • Morningstar Office / Advisor Workstation "Discussions" — Morningstar — the advisor-product forums, a B2B sibling. Morningstar Office was shut down in 2025.
  • Janus Junction (Morningstar forum) — Morningstar — a 1990s fund-family board so heated that Morningstar threatened a "cooling off" shutdown.
  • Armchair Millionaire — 1990s investing community site mentioned by former Morningstar posters; outcome unknown.
