TL;DR

  • What it is: Deutsche Telekom's German-language customer support community at telekomhilft.telekom.de. It grew out of the @Telekom_hilft Twitter account (May 2010) and became one forum in November 2014, when three older forums were merged on Lithium. Telekom called it "Europe's largest service community", with ~1.7M registered members in 2020. Since December 2024 it has run on Sprinklr.
  • Why people come back: Most visitors are one-off askers. The community itself rests on a tiny core of "Heavy Users" (Mitgestalter and Community Guides) who write roughly half of all posts and two-thirds of all solutions. They return because climbing the ranks gives them real power (marking solutions, escalating to staff, editing and moving posts), private access (a closed Guides group, beta tests) and personal relationships with named staff.
  • What Telekom feeds it: about 120 paid "Telekom hilft" agents who can see account data. Community managers run six online meetups a year with Guides and a fully paid annual weekend (the "HeavyT", held 10+ times since 2013). A seven-person Labor team runs ~20 product tests a year. Product managers join for Q&A, and board members send video greetings.
  • Biggest mistake: the December 2024 move from Khoros to Sprinklr. It was announced alongside a transfer of user data to a US vendor, and members suspected an AI cost play. It shut the forum to posting for about three weeks over Christmas and shipped with 120–150+ open bugs. It brought back deleted accounts, lifted bans, and broke notifications on and off into 2026. Telekom also quietly cut outposts (the Facebook support page, the ComputerBase forum presence in Oct 2025), and long-time fans called that cost-cutting.
  • The one lesson: in a support community, pay back the 0.001% who do the work with permissions, insider access and face time, not points. Then guard their tools: a platform change that breaks their workflow hits exactly the people who keep the place alive.

What the community is

The Telekom hilft Community ("Telekom helps") is an owned Q&A forum for Telekom's consumer and business customers. Topics cover broadband, mobile, MagentaTV, email, smart home, contracts and billing. It has several layers:

  • Public forum. Anyone with a Telekom Login can ask and answer. Members can store a customer number and callback number in a private profile field that only Telekom staff can read. That lets a public thread turn into real account-level service (welcome guide).
  • Telekom hilft Team. Paid service agents step in "where it doesn't work without us". Community Managers act as the moderators (ranks page).
  • Telekom hilft Labor (since 2016). Closed and public test boards for pre-release devices, firmware and apps, plus the MAGENTA Testcommunity for early software updates (Produkte testen).
  • Expert Wiki written by members of rank 2-star Mitgestalter and above, plus a "Vorschläge zur Community" ideas board and a bug board.
  • Social channels under the same brand: @Telekom_hilft on X (created 16 Mar 2010; 63,570 followers and 781,084 posts as of Nov 2026, per the X API), formerly Facebook and an Instagram reels format, and WhatsApp as a fallback channel.

Organisationally it sits in customer service (Telekom Deutschland Service, sites in Kiel, Bielefeld and Westerstede), not in corporate communications (KOM magazine reportage). Status today: active and busy. On the morning of 6 Nov 2026 the homepage feed showed about 20 new threads in roughly three hours, nearly all customer problems.

Origin and strategy

  • Social care first (2010). Oliver Nissen, a Kiel customer-service manager, began answering angry Telekom tweets after work in 2009, without asking permission. "I think it was good that we didn't ask so much back then, but just did it" (KOM). @Telekom_hilft posted its first tweet on 5 May 2010, the first time a German telco offered customer service on social networks, and Facebook followed in September 2010 (Telekom press release, 2020; UPLOAD).
  • Feedback-Community (May 2012). This was a Telekom platform that brought the Twitter and Facebook service answers together in one place (CR report 2012). It sat alongside a decade-old customer "Service-Forum" and a "Business-Community".
  • Consolidation (Nov 2014). Those three were merged into one Telekom hilft Community on Lithium, built with T-Systems Multimedia Solutions and with selected heavy users from the old forums involved: ~360,000 members and >1M legacy posts (Computerwoche, 10 Nov 2014).
  • Strategy in Telekom's own words (Lithys 2016 entry, platform owner Eva Heinrichs): "a pillar of Deutsche Telekom's customer care", focused on support "but also leveraging product feedback and co-creation". Also: "Community Management is the new Service". Telekom linked community answers from its social channels and help portal, and embedded community search in the eShop and help pages. It even ran ratings and reviews in the business eShop. Insights went "to the management board and are part of our early-warning system."
  • Champions: Oliver Nissen (head of Social Media & Services) and his boss Gero Niemeyer, who in 2013 created the outbound "Alert-Team" that seeks out frustrated customers on outside sites. Service CEO Ferri Abolhassan publicly backed the programme in 2018 and 2020. Later, Rodrigo Diehl (board) sent a video greeting to the 2025 HeavyT (HeavyT 25).

Trajectory. Fast growth from 2014 to 2018, with membership roughly tripling in four years. Posting stayed at roughly 35k–50k posts a month through 2020. Annual volume then settled at ~450k–520k posts a year from 2022 to 2025. Monthly visitors look past their peak: about 2M in 2020, 1.4M in 2025. The "members" number counts registrations, not activity, and has not been published since 2020. One oddity: the homepage post counter barely moved between May 2018 (2.95M) and June 2019 (3.02M), although earlier months added ~35k each. That suggests an archive or cleanup that I could not verify. I classify it as plateaued at a high level, not declining sharply.

What keeps people coming back

Two very different groups use the forum. Askers return only when something breaks. What keeps them is the promise that a real Telekom agent with account access will step in, and peers often answer faster. Helpers are the retention problem, and Telekom's design centres on them:

  1. A rank ladder that unlocks real rights. Members climb from Passant (lurker) through Besucher and Mitglied to 1–5-star Mitgestalter ("co-creator"). Rank comes from a secret algorithm of logins, tenure, page views, posts, kudos and accepted solutions. It is kept secret to stop gaming. Each step grants a tool (Ränge, Badges und Rechte):

    • 1★: a "battery" icon showing whether an asker has stored account data.
    • 2–3★: write access to the Expert Wiki.
    • 4★: can mark posts as solutions.
    • 5★: a "buzzer" that escalates urgent threads straight to the Telekom hilft Team.

    Status here is functional power, not just decoration.

  2. Invite-only Community Guides. Community managers hand-pick Guides once a year, judging activity, solution help, and conduct in conflicts. Guides can edit, move and feature posts, and get a closed group with staff. Inactive Guides are quietly removed, never named publicly (Community Guides FAQ).

  3. Rituals. Guides get six online meetings a year with the community management team, and 5★ Mitgestalter join three of them. The HeavyT is a fully paid weekend for ~30 heavy users, held every year since 2013: Kiel 2013, Berlin/IFA 2015, Bonn 2016, Bielefeld 2019, online 2020 with ~70 users, Darmstadt 2022, Berlin T-Labs 2023, Bremen 2024, Frankfurt 2025. Each one has executive Q&A, a tour of a Telekom facility (Cyber Defense SOC, network Command Center, Quantum Lab), workshops and Community Awards (#10JTH Heavy Users; HeavyT20).

  4. Insider access. Labor testers get pre-release hardware: Speed Home WiFi mesh tested by eight members in 2018, a 5G repeater in 2020, MagentaTV One gen 3 betas in 2026 (Mesh test).

  5. Being heard by the top. At HeavyT 2023 the head of consumer service sat in a fishbowl and took users' cases away for investigation. "If the process had run right in the right places, customers wouldn't land desperately in the community" (HeavyT 2023).

  6. Belonging. The posts are full of insider vocabulary ("ThC", "Teamies", "HeavyT"), long-tenured names and memorials: the 2025 event opened with a minute's applause for a heavy user who had died (HeavyT 25). Some heavy users have been active "for almost 20 years", counting the predecessor forums.

  7. Badges and kudos. These are standard Lithium-style tiered badges (1, 3, 5, 10, 25… answers), plus manual badges. Contests show up now and then, such as "missions" with points for a prize draw in 2019. They are secondary to the rights system.

The content engine

  • Telekom hilft News / blog. Feature launches, migration notices, event reports, the "#10JTH" anniversary series (2020), and the "Community Talk" format that features top member posts and new Guides (Top 10 posts, 2017).
  • Expert Wiki (2016). Members write and structure it. Good forum guides get promoted into it: Guide UlrichZ's Wi-Fi setup guide earned the most kudos in 2017 and was moved into the wiki by another member (wiki launch).
  • Product-manager presence. Telekom brought in more than 20 product managers in 2016 alone, through the "PM@ThC" programme (2nd birthday).
  • Labor reports close the loop on each test (findings, fixes).
  • Video and social formats. Live streams at trade fairs and launches (press release 2020). The YouTube help-video channel "Telekom Hilfe-Videos". "Telekom hilft WG" on Instagram: weekly vertical service reels from Jan 2022 for about 2.5 years, with 50+ videos in year one (1 year WG). Values campaigns too: "Und dir soll ich helfen", a 2020 week against online hate (15-year post).

Score 3/5: steady, sincere, mostly community-operations content. There is no large education programme comparable to YNAB's. The best content (wiki, solutions) is member-made.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. About 120 Telekom hilft agents (2018–2020). A community-management team of roughly 6–8 named managers (e.g. Schmidti, Waldemar H., Markus L., Thomas Wi., Henning H.). A seven-person Labor team (2025). Developers, and a platform owner (Eva Heinrichs in 2016, Niko B. in 2020). Agents sign posts with a first name, and on X with initials such as "^an", which builds personal recognition.
  • Superuser tiers. Mitgestalter 1–5★ (algorithmic), Community Guides (by invitation, reviewed yearly), and "Heavy Users" as the informal umbrella. Telekom's own numbers on them:
    • Half of all posts and two-thirds of all solutions (#10JTH).
    • 2025: "10% of all community posts written by only 0.001% of users", and "their activity equals ~200 employees" (HeavyT 25).
    • Perks are non-monetary: rights, events, beta devices, recognition. I found no evidence of payment or free service.
  • Rules. House rules ("Spielregeln"/netiquette), personal-data redaction, and graduated warnings and bans. In 2025 the community managers publicly threatened "harder moderation" because users ignored staff requests to calm down (Miteinander statt Gegeneinander).
  • Feedback loops. The ideas board, the bug board, the closed Guides group, HeavyT workshops on new features (dark mode beta, private messages v3, a redesign for accessibility in 2025), Labor tests (5,780 suggestions logged), and management early-warning reports.

Growth loops

  • SEO from millions of solved threads. The Lithys entry credits ~1.6M monthly uniques and auto-suggest on Telekom portals that feeds the forum (Lithys 2016).
  • Channel funnelling. Community solutions were reposted on Telekom's social channels, and the help-portal search shows community results (2023 redesign).
  • Reputation PR. Viral moments (the 2013 "Griesgrämer" exchange reached 3.8M users on Twitter; a lost plush donkey; Klopp's assistant's phone) and awards: Focus Money "best online service" (c. 2016), a Lithy Regional Spotlight Award Europe (SBE), and 3rd place in Community Engagement at the Digital Communication Awards 2023.
  • Word of mouth among the technically minded, e.g. ComputerBase readers who said the staffed support was why they chose Telekom (see Member voice).

Evidence of business impact

Almost all of it is company or vendor claims. I found none in audited filings.

  • 80% of questions answered by members and 20% by staff. Solution rate up from 25% to 35%. ">350,000 calls can be avoided per month", calculated as 1.6M uniques × 35% solution rate, which is an assumption, not measured deflection (Lithys 2016).
  • Heavy users' output is "the equivalent of ~200 employees" (2025, internal estimate presented to users).
  • Labor: 185 tests since 2016, 5,250 bugs found, 5,780 improvement suggestions.
  • Academic: Bock, Ebner & Rossmann (2013), "Telekom hilft — Kundenservice via Social Media", Marketing Review St. Gallen 30:74–85. It is a survey of Telekom service customers and reports positive marketing effects from social service (Springer). The authors include Telekom staff, so it is not independent.
  • Anecdotal counter-evidence: a ComputerBase user said he would cancel seven contracts after Telekom left that forum. That is one data point, but it shows the community carried retention value for some high-value customers.

Score 3/5: an unusually concrete deflection model and test-programme statistics, but nothing externally verified about churn or revenue.

What they did right

  1. Started in the channels customers already used, then built a home. Twitter (2010), then a Feedback-Community (2012), then one consolidated forum (2014). The forum inherited both an audience and a staffed service promise.
  2. Gave helpers real authority. Solution-marking at 4★, an escalation buzzer at 5★, and editing and moving for Guides. This turns volunteers into a triage layer, and it is why ~30 people can produce 10% of the posts.
  3. Made staff visible and accountable. About 120 named agents with access to account data mean a public thread can actually be fixed. Nissen's personal video apology for "#8Monthsgate" (YouTube) showed a culture of owning failures.
  4. Invested in in-person ritual for 12+ years. The HeavyT survived Covid by going online with a record ~70 participants, and carried on after the platform change. Executives show up, in person or on video, every year.
  5. Turned power users into a product-testing asset. The Labor gives heavy users status and gives product teams thousands of pre-launch testers: ~32,000 since 2016, and ~10,000 in the MAGENTA Testcommunity.
  6. Took a values stance on moderation. The anti-hate campaign (2020) and defending the Pride logo in 2023 comments signalled what the space stands for.

What they did wrong

  1. The Sprinklr migration (Oct 2024–2026).
    • It was announced together with a transfer of profile data to a US company. Mobiflip and Fefe both read it as an AI and cost-cutting move (mobiFlip; Fefe), and members asked whether "AI is replacing the staff" (thread). Telekom later said the data is stored in an EU AWS instance.
    • The forum was read-only from 15 Dec 2024 until 8 Jan 2025, and even the Labor reopening slipped a day (migration notice).
    • Users counted 120 and then 150+ open bugs. Deleted accounts came back and bans were lifted. Profile deletions broke threads. Pagination was replaced by infinite scroll. Email notifications failed repeatedly (Jan 2025, Nov 2025, Dec 2025, Jun 2026), and outages continued: logouts that wiped drafts, a "stream timeout" in Jan 2026, and a spam wave in Oct 2026 (forum.iptv.blog thread).
    • Telekom admitted that "the new house isn't perfectly furnished right away" (15-year post).
    • Consequence: friction for exactly the heavy users whose notifications and workflows drive answers. ComputerBase users connected it with longer response times.
  2. Repeated redesigns that removed power-user tools. The 2023 redesign dropped the beloved "expert view" and cut 20 boards to 8 (redesign post). One year later came a full platform change. That is two disruptions in 14 months.
  3. Quietly cutting outposts. The Telekom-hilft Facebook page is gone, with service moved to the main "Deutsche Telekom" page. The Instagram WG ended. The staffed ComputerBase presence closed on 1 Nov 2025 (ComputerBase). Fans read this as savings: "Auch bei der Telekom muss gespart werden."
  4. Tone and gatekeeping. Telekom's own 2020 release names slow responses as the top criticism and admits the tone "can be rough". Newcomers complain about a few know-it-all regulars (complaint, 2024), and managers had to issue a public warning in 2025. A community that concentrates power in ~0.001% of users risks feeling like a clique.
  5. Stopped publishing growth metrics. No member count has been published since 2020, and visitors fell from ~2M to 1.4M a month. Whether this is a successful shift to self-service or a decline is opaque.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Tie status to permissions. Each rank should unlock a tool that makes helpers more effective: mark solutions, escalate, edit, write docs. Real power keeps volunteers far longer than badges do.
  2. Hand-pick a small, reviewed inner circle. Choose Guides on behaviour as well as volume, review them yearly, and remove people quietly and kindly.
  3. Give the inner circle a standing ritual with face time. Six online calls a year plus one paid weekend with executives and behind-the-scenes tours costs little next to "~200 FTE" of output.
  4. Back the community with staff who can act on accounts. A private profile field for account data turns public Q&A into real resolution, and keeps askers trusting the forum.
  5. Turn your best users into a beta lab. It is a perk for them and a QA asset for you. Publish the results (bugs found, fixes shipped) so testers see their impact.

Avoid these

  1. Platform migrations that break the power users' workflow. Test with Guides long before launch, keep notifications and moderation state intact, and never go dark for weeks.
  2. Leaving the motive for a change unexplained. A vendor or data change with no clear reason gets read as "AI replacing humans / cost-cutting". Say why, early, and in plain terms.
  3. Letting the core turn into a clique. Concentrated expertise brings gatekeeping and a harsh tone toward newcomers. Moderate the regulars too.
