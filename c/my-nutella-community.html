TL;DR

  • What it is. MyNutella (marketed as "my Nutella The Community") was Ferrero's Italian-only fan site. It went live in spring 2003 at mynutella.it. Each registered fan got a free "Personal Site" (a short "Nutella Pensiero", a diary, photos and a guestbook). Fans could also found or join "Fan Club" pages, post to photo galleries ("Nutella Live", "Art & Collection") and read a magazine written by the site's editorial team. It had about 10,000 members by mid-2004. In June 2008 it was folded into Nutellaville, a Flash "virtual city". Ferrero dropped Nutellaville in 2010 for Facebook. (Cova & Pace 2005; Nutella World Book timeline)
  • Why people came back. Mostly they didn't come back to talk to each other. Cova and Pace's netnography, the canonical study, found "personal self-exhibition in front of other members" rather than peer interaction, and most guestbooks were still empty in mid-2005. What did bring people back was the chance to be seen. The site ran a monthly "Nutellaro del mese" (member of the month), whose prize was a caricature poster. It also ran photo and video contests tied to football, travel and seasons. (Emerald abstract; Wayback: Nutellaro)
  • What Ferrero fed it. It provided a platform built by an agency, a "Redazione" that published brand news alongside stories taken from members' pages, a monthly members' newsletter, downloadable gadgets, and prize contests. It also wrote a "Nutiquette" code of conduct. Ferrero's stated stance was that the brand "takes a step back" so the consumer becomes the protagonist. (Wayback: first-visit guide; Giovannoli via Scenari Economici)
  • The biggest mistake. It was a fenced enclosure with no channel back to the company. Ferrero kept the copyright to everything fans posted, offered fans no way to talk to its marketers, and kept sending legal letters to independent fan spaces before, during and after the site's life. Fans had nowhere to influence anything, the site never grew beyond a few tens of thousands of registrations, and Ferrero replaced it with its next platform twice in five years (Nutellaville, then Facebook). (Cova & Pace 2005; LA Times 2013)
  • The one lesson. For an everyday product, a community of "show yourself with the brand" (self-expression plus recognition) can be built cheaply and stays conflict-free, but it produces little peer bonding and no switching costs. The durable energy sat in fan-owned rituals: offline Nutella Parties and, later, the fan-founded World Nutella Day. The company did best when it amplified those rituals rather than enclosing them.

What the community is

MyNutella was a registration-gated Italian portal with five areas. "My Area" was the account. The Magazine was editorial content. Personal Sites were member blogs. Fan Club Sites were group pages. Nutella Art was a user photo gallery. Around them sat e-cards, an instant messenger, a classifieds and merchandise board ("MyBacheca"), downloadable gadgets (screensavers, icons, welcome messages) and the newsletter (Wayback home, May 2006; Giovannoli via Scenari Economici). The site's "why" page called it "the official space where Nutella fans meet, compare and feel emotions". It said "hundreds of existing Fan Clubs" could now publicise their activities and that "NUTELLA is a product that makes community by itself" (Wayback, May 2004).

Who ran it. It was run by Ferrero's Italian internet and multimedia function. Antonio Massimo Castiglia, Ferrero's head of Internet and Multimedia, presented it at Il Sole 24 Ore's "I Nuovi Modelli del Marketing" conference on 29 April 2004. Agencies built it: the footer credits "Powerhouse – The Community Makers®" in 2004 and "Mesa-lab" from 2005 to 2008 (Cova & Pace 2005; Wayback footers above).

Dates (verified). Cova and Pace wrote that Ferrero decided "early 2004" to launch the site at www.mynutella.com. The archive disagrees. mynutella.it already had login-gated personal-site URLs on 13 March 2003 (Wayback CDX). Ferrero's official chronology dates the debut to April 2003 (Nutella World Book). In May 2004 mynutella.com showed only an Apache test page (Wayback). The real site was the .it domain.

Unofficial spaces. The biggest were offline "Nutella Parties": village fêtes, school parties and DJ nights that consumers organised entirely on their own (Cova & Pace 2005). There was also the fan site NutellaFans on Tripod (from 1997–98), and, from 2007, the US-born World Nutella Day. Reddit never mattered: r/Nutella was created in September 2010 and has 971 subscribers and 893 posts in total (Arctic Shift).

Origin and strategy

Ferrero built MyNutella to repair a hostile relationship with online fans. Before 2003 it "pursued all sites that used the name, logo or other signs of Nutella without authorisation". That included NutellaFans, which in late 1999 was the main online meeting place for Italian "nutellamaniaci" and was forced to rename. The Nutell@ Chat Club and nutellamania.com met the same fate (Cova & Pace 2005, citing Padovani 2004). Ferrero's own timeline confirms that from 1997 "the Ferrero legal office is adamant about preventing the illegal use of the brand" (Nutella World Book). By 2003 Ferrero had softened, internally nicknaming the internet "Internut", and it let the file-sharing program Gnutella borrow the brand's typeface without a fight.

Castiglia framed the site as "a complete reversal of the brand–consumer relationship": the brand "takes a step back" and leaves the stage to the consumer. Ferrero listed five objectives (Cova & Pace 2005):

  1. Feed the Nutella myth (the tribe's existence certifies the brand's symbolic values).
  2. Capture weak but spontaneous signals from the most proactive loyalists.
  3. Dialogue with the tribe, giving marketing instant feedback.
  4. Build a base for offline local events.
  5. Develop new forms of business.

In practice only the first two were delivered (see What they did wrong). The business context matters. Ferrero was Italy's second-largest advertiser, spending €250M in Italy and €650M worldwide (Nielsen 2003 via Padovani). Nutella held about 90% of its Italian category (thebrandcommunity, citing Cova & Pace). The community was never a retention engine for a subscription. It was a brand-meaning play bolted onto mass advertising. Cova and Pace called this "two-storey marketing": "Kotlerian" mass marketing sets the brand's meanings, and a "mini-marketing" fallow ground lets fans renegotiate them.

Trajectory. Registrations grew steadily to roughly 15k by 2008 and roughly 25k across the MyNutella and Nutellaville user base by 2011 (estimates from sequential IDs). That is tiny next to Nutella's Italian reach, and two orders of magnitude below what Facebook delivered within months. The publicly visible activity was thin. In December 2007 one fan club's blurb reads "I'm a 12-year-old boy, I'm alone in the fan club, come on…" (Wayback, Jan 2008).

What keeps people coming back

  • Self-exhibition. The core loop was building a page about yourself through Nutella. You wrote a "Nutella Pensiero", kept a diary and posted photos. Most Nutella Live photos showed holidays and parties with no jar in sight. Cova and Pace called the result a "romantic community" in which members use the brand as a "paralanguage" to present identity to strangers, not to discuss the product (Cova & Pace 2005). Their EJM finding: sociality "not based on interaction between peers, but more on personal self-exhibition" (Emerald).
  • Recognition and status. The "Nutellaro del mese" received "fame, honour, glory": an A3 caricature poster, postcards of the caricature, a month on the home page, and a place in a permanent gallery of "the authentic stars of MyNutella". The monthly galleries ran at least from Nov 2004 to Oct 2005 (Wayback). The editorial team also featured personal sites on the home page and wrote news stories drawn from members' pages (first-visit guide). Jar collectors formed an informal top tier (Cova & Pace 2005).
  • Cadenced contests. These included a fitness contest in 2003, photo contests with prizes in 2005, "Che partita sarebbe senza Nutella?" for the 2006 World Cup (fan images, "nutelloso" phone-video spots, web animations, with "champion prizes"), a national-team page in 2007, and Euro 2008 plus a Snack & Drink Inter-Rail photo contest in May–June 2008 (Wayback 2006; Wayback 2008).
  • Groups. Any user could found a Fan Club page to post news, events and Nutella-party photos and recruit members. These were meant to "create small leaders" who would organise parties offline (Giovannoli via Scenari Economici). Most were virtual-only aggregations.
  • Weak spots. There were no streaks, levels or leaderboards, and no peer-support need (nobody needs help eating Nutella). Switching costs were near zero, since your page was the only asset, and Ferrero owned its copyright. Brand rivalry, the classic glue of brand tribes, was absent because Nutella had no real competitor (Cova & Pace 2005).

The content engine

  • Editorial "Redazione". The home page had three news blocks. One carried official brand news, such as design-week tie-ins, Olympic sponsorship ("a sweet part of the Olympic medal won by Arianna…"), Winx/Justice League jars and Easter packs. Two carried stories the editors pulled from members' personal and fan-club sites (Wayback 2006). A 2018 Italian master's thesis calls this the moment "the brand for the first time becomes a publisher" (Giovannoli via Scenari Economici).
  • Monthly newsletter. Five or six items an issue (curiosities, news, questions). Through it the company could "sound out, directly and without the cost of a focus group, the mood of its most loyal customers". Members could set topics and frequency ("Notizie Esclusive") (same source; first-visit guide).
  • Gadgets and tools. E-cards, screensavers, icons, audio, a game and an IM client ("mygadgets" pages, 2004–2007, Wayback CDX).
  • Member content. The site was built to be mostly member content: Pensieri, diaries, photos, recipes, collections. Ferrero imposed no rule that photos show the product (Cova & Pace 2005). Cova and Pace overstate this as "no marketing operation at all on the site". Wayback captures show steady promotional content (jar collections, Snack & Drink contests, licensed cartoons), especially after 2006.
  • What it lacked. There was no how-to or education layer, because the product needs none. The recipe opportunity was left to outsiders. World Nutella Day's recipe index reached 700+ recipes and ranked #1 on Google for "Nutella recipes" for years, and Ferrero did not publish its first cookbook until 2013, by Sara Rosso's account (Rosso 2016).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. There was an in-house internet unit (Castiglia), agencies (Powerhouse, then Mesa-lab) and an editorial team that ran news, the Nutellaro award and contests, answered fan emails about Nutellerie, branded clothing, how to organise a Nutella party and where to buy 3–5 kg jars, and offered surprise prizes. In July 2008, for example, Nutellaville wrote to a recipe blogger: "Nutellaville, the official Nutella community, has decided to reward your creativity and loyalty with a nice surprise" (Un kilo di ricette, comment). No headcount is public.
  • Rules. The "NUTiquette" banned vulgar or aggressive language, sharing others' personal data, commercial promotion, nickname cloning, off-topic posts and third-party IP. It also urged members to keep their own personal data out of public pages, which mattered given how many members were minors (Wayback 2004). There was an abuse-report email. The terms gave Ferrero the copyright to everything members posted (Cova & Pace 2005).
  • Ambassador program. None formal. Fan-club founders and the Nutellaro del mese were the only status roles.
  • Feedback loop. There was effectively none. "Nothing today signals a possibility of interaction and dialogue with the official owners of the brand… There is no place left for fans to interact with Ferrero and thus weigh on product design or brand strategy." Cova and Pace read the site as "more an action to defuse tribal contestation than co-development with the tribe" (Cova & Pace 2005). The newsletter sounded out members one way.

Growth loops

There were weak ones. They included "consiglia" (recommend-to-a-friend) and "invia ad un amico" buttons on articles and pages, fan clubs recruiting members, e-cards, and contests promoted in national advertising and on packs (Wayback CDX). Acquisition was Italy-only by design, the content sat behind a login, and Flash (from 2008, Nutellaville) hurt search and sharing. The loop that actually scaled Nutella's online fandom was outside the site. World Nutella Day was a 2007 blogger round-up. It spread through recipes and SEO, grew to 40,000 Facebook likes and about 7,000 Twitter followers by 2013, and was transferred to Ferrero in 2015 (Rosso 2016; LA Times). Facebook was the other: Ferrero's own page reached 2M likes in six months in 2010.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. No sales, loyalty, NPS or cost figures for MyNutella or Nutellaville were ever published. Ferrero is private, so there are no filings.
  • Company claims. Ferrero claimed "about one million daily visitors" in 2005 (Cova & Pace 2006), and la Repubblica reported 120,000 daily Nutellaville users in 2010. Neither fits the ~10–25k registrations.
  • Academic. Cova and Pace (EJM 2006, 853 citations on OpenAlex) is qualitative and descriptive. Gabrielli and Baghi (J. Marketing Communications, 2014/2016) compared brand concept maps of Nutellaville members and non-members. Members held extra "brand as person" associations ("seductive, popular, reassuring") that, the authors argue, offset the guilt chocolate triggers. That is evidence of richer brand image among members, but it says nothing about causality or sales.
  • Score: 1/5. The community is famous in the literature but unmeasured commercially.

What they did right

  1. Turned a legal posture into a hosted space. After years of shutting down fan sites, Ferrero gave fans an official, free home. Cova and Pace found "no conflict" with the company and saw fans treat Ferrero as the legitimate guardian of a "common good of Italians" (Cova & Pace 2005).
  2. Designed for self-expression, not product talk. No-rules photo galleries and personal Pensieri matched how people relate to an everyday food: through memories of mothers and childhood, comfort, and parties. Members used the site heavily for exactly that, posting 2,318 photos in about two years.
  3. Recognition with a physical artefact. The Nutellaro del mese caricature poster and postcards was cheap, personal and shareable offline. It is a model for low-budget status design.
  4. Light, readable governance. The Nutiquette was plain-language and focused on child safety.
  5. Editorial curation of member content into "news". This gave members a reason to post well and gave lurkers something to read.
  6. The later pivot was right. Ferrero recognised that Facebook had overtaken owned portals (2010). In 2014 it finally made fans the centre of a global campaign (Nutella Stories, 76,400 stories), and in 2015 it adopted World Nutella Day rather than competing with it (Ferrero; Rosso 2016).

What they did wrong

  1. No dialogue, no influence. Ferrero stated "dialogue with the tribe" and "instant feedback" as goals, then never delivered them. Fans could not reach the marketers, comment on products or shape anything. Consequence: no reason for high-value fans to invest. Cova and Pace call it "less implicating than a hybrid forum".
  2. Copyright grab. All member texts and photos became Ferrero property. "Less than a desert island, it is a well-fenced, well-guarded enclosure" (Cova & Pace 2005).
  3. Peer interaction never formed. Most guestbooks were still empty in 2005, fan clubs were often one-person, and there was no ritual cadence beyond the contests. Without social ties, Ferrero could migrate the community twice in five years. The migrations themselves added churn: mynutella.it to Flash Nutellaville in 2008, then to a nutella.it profile page in 2010, then nothing by 2013.
  4. Platform bets on dead ends. Nutellaville was a Flash "virtual city" launched in 2008, as Second Life-style worlds were peaking. A foreign visitor in 2009 found it required "all your personal details" to enter and couldn't get into the Nutella Blog (thebrandcommunity, Apr 2009). It was abandoned within two years.
  5. Legal reflex never fully went away. In May 2013 Ferrero sent World Nutella Day's founder a cease-and-desist. Fans threatened boycotts and some returned jars, and Ferrero reversed within days, calling the letter "a routine brand defense procedure" (LA Times, 22 May 2013). The same pattern had killed NutellaFans in 1999–2000.
  6. Italy-only and gated. Rosso describes Ferrero's 2006–07 English-language online presence as "almost non-existent", with no contact route at all (Rosso 2016). That left the global fan energy, and the recipe SEO, to a blogger.
  7. Inflated metrics. A claimed "one million daily visitors" next to roughly 10k members suggests nobody was measuring community health honestly.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give members a stage for identity, not just a forum. For low-involvement products, "show yourself with the brand" (profile, origin story, photos) is the natural entry ritual. Make the first post a short prompt like the Nutella Pensiero.
  2. Build a monthly spotlight with a tangible reward. A member of the month with a physical, shareable artefact (the caricature poster) creates status cheaply.
  3. Curate member content into the company's own channel. Running member stories as "news" and newsletter items rewards contributors and feeds lurkers.
  4. Tie contests to cultural moments members already care about. World Cup, Euro, Easter and summer travel gave the site a seasonal heartbeat.
  5. Back fan-founded rituals instead of competing with them. The durable wins came from fan-owned events (Nutella Parties, World Nutella Day). Adopt them with credit, as Ferrero eventually did in 2015.

Avoid these

  1. Don't build a one-way enclosure. No route to the product team, plus IP ownership of member content, signals that the community exists for the brand. Expect lurking and eventual abandonment.
  2. Don't send legal letters to your superfans. Shutting down fan spaces before offering an alternative, and doing it again in 2013, cost goodwill each time. Set a fan-use policy before the lawyers act.
  3. Don't chase the platform du jour at the expense of continuity. Moving three times in seven years (a portal, a Flash virtual world, a profile page, then Facebook), each time with fresh registration, broke whatever ties existed. Members' content and identity should survive migrations.
