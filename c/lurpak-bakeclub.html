TL;DR

  • What it was: a UK website (bakeclub.co.uk) built for Lurpak butter by the digital agency Outside Line. Version 1 (Nov 2009–2012) let friends, colleagues or housemates form a private "bake club", take turns baking on a set day (the site emailed whoever was up next), and rate each bake; photos could go into a public gallery. Version 2 (Feb 2013–spring 2014) became a public monthly baking challenge with a guest "mentor", prizes, and a #BakeClub hashtag feed from Twitter and Instagram.
  • Why people came back: in v1, the obligation to your own group (it's your turn to bring cake to the office) plus a reminder email; in v2, prizes and the chance to be picked by a known baker. Neither created ties between members across the site.
  • What Lurpak fed it: a £12m 2010 brand campaign that named the site, in-store booklets, a paid monthly host (MasterChef winner Tim Anderson, Lily Vanilli, Bake Off's Ruth Clemens, food bloggers), prize hampers, and small events for winners.
  • Biggest mistake: it was run as a campaign asset, not a community. Growth stalled after year one (10,096 members in Sep 2010, 12,771 in Nov 2012) even as The Great British Bake Off set off a UK baking boom. The 2013 relaunch tied participation to prizes and a hashtag that wasn't unique, reaching about 5 contributions a day, and the site was gone by May 2014 when Lurpak moved on to its next campaign.
  • The one lesson: a utility for groups who already know each other (the bake rota) is a real retention mechanic, but only if you keep investing in the product. Contests and celebrity hosts bring bursts of entries, not members.

What the community is

Lurpak Bake Club was a branded baking site at bakeclub.co.uk, owned by Lurpak (Arla Foods; the trademark belonged to the Danish Dairy Board) and built and run by its London digital agency Outside Line ("Baked by Outside Line" in the site footer) [1][3]. It went through two distinct incarnations:

Version Dates Core mechanic Where interaction happened
v1 "Bake Club" + "Birthday Bakes" Nov 2009 – early 2013 Register a club of friends/colleagues/family, pick a weekly or fortnightly bake day; the site emails the next baker; members "rate the bake"; public gallery (also Flickr) [3][4] Inside private clubs, mostly offline (the office kitchen); thin public gallery
v2 relaunch Feb 2013 – ~Apr 2014 Monthly themed challenge with a guest "mentor" who posts a recipe and tips and picks winners; badges; social login; uploads or #BakeClub posts on Twitter/Instagram pulled into the challenge gallery [5][6] Public gallery of entries with star votes; Twitter replies to @Lurpak

Status today: defunct. By 16 May 2014 bakeclub.co.uk redirected to lurpak.co.uk/foodadventures (the Cook's Range "Food Adventures" campaign), and from 2016 to the Lurpak homepage [7]. Lurpak today runs only recipe pages and social accounts (Facebook ~1.23M likes; Instagram @lurpakuk ~19K followers) [8][9]. There was no official forum, Facebook group or subreddit, and I found no unofficial fan space for it (Arctic Shift searches of r/UKfood, r/Baking and r/britishbakeoff returned nothing).

Origin and strategy

  • Brand context. In January 2010 Arla launched a £12m Lurpak marketing campaign ("Joy of Creation", led by a TV spot called "Pie") about the pride of cooking from scratch; the press release lists "a new website called www.bakeclub.co.uk" alongside branded lorries, 5,000 poster sites and "digital activity targeting bloggers who are passionate about food" [10]. The site was the digital expression of the "Good Food Deserves Lurpak" positioning, not a business unit.
  • Launch. The site was live by 21 Nov 2009 with 627 bake clubs registered and the tagline "The first rule of Bake Club is that you tell everyone about Bake Club" [3]. A blogger received a "1 yr old" birthday cake from Lurpak on 19 Nov 2010 [11].
  • Business model link. Butter is a low-involvement grocery purchase, so the goal was usage and brand affinity: more home baking means more butter. Brand manager Charlotte Buswell (2010): "a series of digital initiatives that encourage consumers to enjoy good food and to cook from scratch. Bake Club was the first of these" [4]. The in-store booklets of Sept 2010 pushed shoppers to "become a member of the Lurpak Bake Club" and upload creations [12]. There was no subscription, support-deflection or product-feedback purpose.
  • Champions. Agency-side: Outside Line co-founder Ant Cauchi tweeted "Lurpak Bake Club name of the day" at launch [13]; in 2013 the creative strategy director Dan Rutherford fronted the relaunch [5]. Client-side: brand managers Charlotte Buswell (2010) and Samantha Peel (2013) [4][5]. The owner changed with each brief, and Publicis bought Outside Line for about £8m in December 2012 and folded it into Saatchi & Saatchi, two months before the relaunch [14].
  • The 2013 relaunch "comes on the back of Lurpak's current 'Good, Proper Food' campaign created by Wieden & Kennedy London" [5]. Peel: "The new Bake Club site really allows us to tap into the passion around baking – making it easier for good food lovers to share their proud bakes and seek inspiration from like-minded foodies" [5].

Trajectory: one strong year (launch to Sep 2010), then a plateau of ~6% a year through 2012. This happened while The Great British Bake Off (first aired Aug 2010) was creating the biggest UK home-baking boom in decades, so the stall can't be blamed on lack of interest in baking. The relaunch produced bursts around each blogger's month, then the site was retired after roughly 15 months. Peak: members, Nov 2012 (12,771); activity, probably year one. Wayback archived 189 unique URLs in 2011 and 209 in 2013, then 86 in 2014 [7].

What keeps people coming back

v1 (2009–2012): a real but private retention loop.

  • Group obligation plus reminders. The "recipe" on the About page: "Bunch of friends, colleagues or family members; 1 chosen day of the week (can also use fortnightly) ... The chosen baker will be notified in advance. When served, rate the bake" [17]. The rota and the email ("pings an email to alert a baker in the club when it's their turn") are the strongest mechanic Bake Club ever had: accountability to people you see every day [4].
  • Identity through club names. Members named their clubs ("HOT? I'm bloody bakin' ere!" was Outside Line's "name of the day") [13]; Bake Off contestant Rob Billington "signed up to Lurpak's Bake Club ... and started one for me and my housemates" [18].
  • Recognition. Featured bakes on the homepage ("My cake was apparently featured on bakeclub.co.uk") [19]; "Rate the Bake" stars [4].
  • Lower-commitment spin-off. "Birthday Bakes" (Sep 2010) for groups for whom "the commitment of baking every week or every two weeks is too much": take turns baking for each other's birthdays [4].
  • Weakness: the value lived inside each office group. Once a club stopped baking, nothing on the site pulled members back, and there was no reason to talk to strangers.

v2 (2013–2014): extrinsic, monthly, prize-led.

  • Monthly cadence and themes: Cottage Pie, Chocolate Desserts, Bread & Butter Pudding, Tarts, Bread, Muffins, Cheesecakes, Pastry, Mac 'n' Cheese, Winter Puddings, Festive Bakes, Veg Bakes & Cakes, Malt Loaf (Ruth Clemens), Square Bakes [6].
  • Status: badges and bake counts on profiles ("Jacqueline H, 21 bakes") [20]; the homepage promised "take part in monthly challenges, collect badges, connect your social accounts and invite your friends" [20].
  • Prizes: Labour & Wait enamel spoon sets, Sous Chef patissier's hampers, cookbooks, a Lily Vanilli masterclass, cake tins [20][21][22][23]. Winners publicly celebrated ("Wow, for the 1st time ever, I've won a baking challenge") [24].
  • Parasocial pull: each month a known baker or blogger posted a recipe and picked winners [5][21].
  • IRL: winner events, a Lily Vanilli workshop (13 Apr 2013) and a James Ramsden supper club where "the company on my table were Lurpak Bake Club competition winners" [25][26].
  • Switching costs: none. Entries could be just a hashtag on a tweet, so members owned nothing on the site.

The content engine

  • Brand content: W+K TV, outdoor and print ads showing real food made by food bloggers (Made by Many praised that the brand "enlisted the help of some food bloggers to create the food used in the photography") [27]; the Lurpak recipe library and the "Good Food Finder" stockist tool [4][28]; in-store recipe booklets [12].
  • v2 challenge content: one mentor recipe plus "five top tips" each month, posted on the site and on the host's own blog and social channels [21]. Hosts were compensated: Ren Behan disclosed "I have been compensated for producing a recipe and for hosting the Lurpak Bake Club this month. The winner's will be chosen by the Lurpak Bake Club judges" [21]; Urvashi Roe was sent product and "invit[ed] ... to host the January Bake Club" [29]; Amber-Rose Smith was "asked if I would be interested in getting involved" [30].
  • Member content: photos of bakes (uploads or hashtags). Curation was a winner pick per month; there were no member guides, recipes-as-library or comment threads visible in the archived gallery [6].
  • Cadence: one challenge a month from Feb 2013 to about Apr 2014; earlier, ad-hoc @Lurpak tweets like "Test out this recipe for the Lurpak bake club" (Oct 2011) [15].

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: no named community manager on record. The site, emails ("Send us your Bake Club stories" to lurpak@outsideline.co.uk) and judging were handled by the agency [3]. "Lurpak Bake Club judges" picked winners in blogger months [21].
  • Ambassador-like program: the monthly mentor (the Red magazine piece calls Lily Vanilli "this month's ambassador for Bake Club, the online community for keen cooks") [23]. It was a paid rotating spokesperson, not a member superuser program.
  • Moderation: challenge rules existed but were weakly enforced. A participant complained: "can't believe how many people have uploaded pics that have nothing to do with a loaf of bread…cupcakes? Seriously?" [31]. The hashtag feed pulled in unrelated accounts such as @bakeclub_official (a German baking brand) and @bakeclub_australia (an Australian baking school) [6][20].
  • Feedback into product: no evidence found. A butter brand had no product roadmap for members to influence.

Growth loops

  • v1 had a built-in invite loop: you could only start a club by inviting people ("Invite people to join, sprinkle with compliments") [17], and the "first rule ... tell everyone" line made referral the joke [3]. Lurpak claims year-one growth came "through word of mouth ... in the absence of any advertising or PR" [4], although the £12m campaign named the URL [10] and @Lurpak recruited in replies ("join the 12,000 strong talented bakers") [15]. The loop stopped once friend groups were saturated.
  • v2 relied on bought reach: each host's blog and Twitter audience (Ruth Clemens' post brought in at least one new participant who blogged about it [31]), magazine tie-ins (Red Online's Lily Vanilli giveaway [23]), competition aggregator listings ("#comps ... Win Joseph Joseph food preparation set, With lurpak bake club") [32], and the #BakeClub hashtag. These spikes ended when each month did; March 2014's 30 tweets came almost entirely from Ren Behan's network.
  • SEO: none of note; galleries were photo-only.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence on the community: none. No published retention, repeat-entry, sales-lift or reach figures for Bake Club beyond member counts.
  • Adjacent company results (not attributable): Lurpak value sales rose 3.5% in 2013 in a declining category [33], and Lurpak overtook Flora as the UK's top butter/spread brand by volume in 2014 [34]; the W+K "Good, Proper Food" commuter campaign claimed a 9% year-on-year sales rise [35]. Those results came from mass advertising; nobody credited Bake Club with them.
  • Awards: "Lurpak Bake Club" appears among the 2013 Lovie Awards Websites – Food & Beverage winners [36]. That's evidence of craft, not of a community.
  • Revealed preference: Lurpak let the site go to a campaign redirect in 2014 and never rebuilt a community, which suggests it didn't see enough value to keep paying for it.

What they did right

  1. A genuinely social utility in v1. Scheduling a rota and emailing the next baker solved a real coordination problem for office and household cake rotas, and it was a fit with Lurpak's "cook from scratch" message [4][17]. 10,000 members in under a year with mostly organic growth is respectable for a butter brand [4].
  2. Low-commitment variant. Birthday Bakes recognised that a weekly obligation is too much for many groups [4].
  3. Lowering the cost to contribute. v2 accepted entries straight from Twitter and Instagram, so members didn't have to visit the site [5][20].
  4. Credible hosts and real recognition. Monthly mentors from MasterChef, Bake Off and food blogging, physical prizes and winner events made winning meaningful; winners tweeted their delight [24][26].
  5. Brand coherence. The site, the ads (made with bloggers' real food) and the booklets all told the same "pride in homemade" story [10][27].

What they did wrong

  1. Campaign lifecycle, not community lifecycle. Bake Club was born as a line item in the 2010 campaign [10], relaunched to support the 2013 campaign [5], and redirected to the 2014 campaign [7]. FeverBee's diagnosis fits: "Once participation levels drop, interest from the organization wanes and the website disappears a year or so later" [2]. Consequence: members' galleries and clubs vanished with no notice that I could find.
  2. Neglect during the best possible market. Membership grew only ~2,700 from Sep 2010 to Nov 2012 [3] while Bake Off made baking a national hobby. A user said in Oct 2010: "the lurpak bake club is such a bad UX...what happened there? could have been a great community" [37].
  3. Agency-run, with ownership changing hands. All operations sat with Outside Line, which was sold to Publicis/Saatchi two months before the relaunch [14]. FeverBee singled out this pattern: "Don't hire a marketing agency ... They're expensive and take a promotion-driven approach" [2].
  4. Replaced intrinsic group ties with prizes. v2 threw away v1's private-club mechanic in favour of public contests. That drew prize-seekers (and competition aggregators [32]) who didn't stay: "I've just joined the Lurpak Bake Club. I did it to win stuff. I didn't win stuff. Not even butter" [38]. Entries got almost no peer votes (0–1 stars) [6].
  5. A generic hashtag. #BakeClub was already used by other bakers and brands, so the gallery filled with off-topic and other-brand posts [6][20][31], and Lurpak couldn't own the conversation.
  6. No member-to-member layer. No comments, threads, member guides or superusers appear in any archived page [6][20]; the only "community" was a photo wall. Early critics called the premise "an old concept" and "glorified spam" [39].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Build for groups that already exist. The v1 rota (friends/colleagues, a set day, a reminder to whoever is next) is the kind of accountability loop that keeps people returning; it's the same logic as Strava clubs or a shared YNAB budget.
  2. Ship a lighter-commitment mode (Birthday Bakes) for people who drop out of the main cadence instead of losing them.
  3. Let people contribute from where they already post (hashtag or social import), but give them a reason to come to your space afterwards.
  4. Use recognisable hosts to set a monthly ritual, and pair them with tangible, public recognition and occasional IRL meetups for top members.
  5. Keep the community on-message with the product's job: Bake Club made "baking from scratch" the thing to do, which is what sells butter.

Avoid these

  1. Don't tie the community's life to campaign budgets or agency contracts. If the community is only a campaign deliverable, it dies when the campaign ends, and so do members' contributions.
  2. Don't swap intrinsic, social reasons to show up for prizes. Prizes recruit prize-seekers who leave; they don't create ties between members.
  3. Don't ship a gallery and call it a community. Without conversation, peer recognition and member roles (moderators, regulars, superusers), there's nothing to come back to besides the next contest.

Score justifications: retention_mechanics 2 (v1's rota and reminder emails were real but private, and v2 relied on prizes); content_engine 2 (one paid recipe a month plus ads, no member-made library); member_ownership 1 (no member roles, comments or governance); staff_investment 2 (agency build and paid hosts, no community staff, abandoned after each campaign); business_impact_evidence 1 (no data beyond member counts); durability 1 (about 4.5 years over two incarnations, defunct since 2014).
