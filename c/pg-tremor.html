TL;DR

  • What it is. Tremor was a Procter & Gamble business unit, launched in 2001, that recruited a screened panel of US teens aged 13-19 ("the Tremor Crew"). It peaked at 200,000-300,000 members, about 1% of American teenagers. Tremor sent members teasers, samples, coupons, music and chances to vote on real brand decisions, and expected them to talk. Brands, most of them outside P&G, paid for access [1][2][3].
  • Why people came back. Getting it first, inside scoop, and influence. A member who voted on a DreamWorks movie logo later saw it in a theatre trailer: "So they do listen. It does matter." [1]. Mail itself was a hook ("They don't get mail," said Tremor's Steve Knox) [3]. A weekly newsletter asked for 5-10 minutes [4].
  • What the company fed it. Monthly-ish mailed kits built around a "talkable" hook that differed from the ad message, such as blacked-out scripts of a TV finale. It also ran exclusive music streams with ratings, teen lifestyle features, design votes, fashion-week and screening invites, and a moderated members' forum, Local Buzz [5][6][7][8].
  • Biggest mistake. Not requiring teens to disclose the P&G connection. Forbes called it a "stealth sales force", a parent objected on the record, Commercial Alert asked the FTC to subpoena Tremor in October 2005, and the Guardian, Philadelphia Inquirer and others framed it as manipulating kids [1][9][10][11]. P&G's own brands were mostly bought by moms, so the teen panel became a sideshow. Its member site went dark in October 2008 [12][13].
  • The lesson. Exclusive access plus real influence ("you picked that logo") is a strong, cheap hook for advocates. But a panel built on selection and swag, with no peer bonds and no honest disclosure, is a marketing channel, not a community. It disappears the moment the sponsor's business case moves on.

What the community is

Tremor (tremor.com) was P&G's in-house word-of-mouth unit. Its first and, until 2005, only network was a panel of US teenagers called "the Tremor Crew". P&G's own About page (Dec 2007) described it as "a marketing service - powered by The Procter & Gamble Company - that develops teen word-of-mouth marketing programs", whose "over a quarter of a million influential teens ... help develop product ideas and marketing programs that teens want to talk about" [14].

How it worked for a member:

  • Apply and be screened. Teens answered a questionnaire, which included how many people they talk to every day. Only about 10-15% of applicants were admitted. Tremor members averaged about 150-170 names on their instant-messenger buddy lists, against 25-30 for a typical teen [1][5][9].
  • Get programs. A weekly online newsletter and, "from time to time", programs mailed home "which usually contain info about a new product or idea, and may contain coupons or samples". P&G told parents it takes "about 5-10 minutes" per program [4]. Knox said selected members received "a mailing once a month or more" [3].
  • Talk, or don't. Tremor never scripted members and stressed that they could be negative. It didn't require disclosure either [4][10].
  • Report back. Surveys, polls, random phone follow-ups on brand awareness, focus groups (sometimes paid by small checks), and design votes [1][5].

Spaces. The members-only tremor.com had polls, music, news and the Local Buzz forum with posted rules ("Local Buzz is a space for The Tremor Crew – I will treat other members and their opinions with respect"; no links, no personal information) [7]. Members also got mailed kits and invitations to IRL events: an advance screening of Lost for about 70 teens in Manhattan in July 2004, fashion week shows, network TV upfronts and backstage visits [5][8]. A separate P&G teen site, Toejam ("teens openly expressing just about me"), launched alongside Tremor in 2001 to preview products and critique ads. No evidence was found of how long it lasted [15][16].

Unofficial spaces. None found. No fan forum, subreddit or nostalgia thread about being a Tremor member turned up in Brave or Reddit searches. The public conversation about Tremor came from journalists and critics, not members.

Status. The member-facing pages (About, For Parents, news) were captured through 15 September 2008 and return 404 from 21 October 2008 [13]. In February 2009 Tremor's business site still pitched "our panels of Connectors (Vocalpoint Moms - Tremor Teens)" [17]. By December 2009 it described only "a network of more than 500,000 highly connected moms" [18]. The unit then left P&G and survives as the independent Tremor LLC running Vocalpoint (see the separate procter-gamble-vocalpoint report).

Origin and strategy

  • Predecessor failure. In January 2001 P&G wrote off Swizzle.com, a seven-month-old UK teen-girl portal built with Excite to showcase Tampax, Clearasil, Max Factor and Cover Girl, saying it "was no longer part of its strategy" [19]. Two months later Ad Age reported P&G "plunging back into teen community building with two sites in the U.S.-Tremor and Toejam". Those sites carried out CEO A.G. Lafley's stated strategy of "identifying and leveraging influence of 'teen chat leaders'" [15]. The lesson P&G drew: don't build a destination portal; build a recruited panel you can mail.
  • Why teens. Teens were hard to reach with TV and spent about $175 billion a year. "The mass-marketing model is dead," said P&G Global Marketing Officer Jim Stengel [1]. Knox, then VP of business development, said P&G did it "first with teens to learn, and we are more interested in moms next" (Oct 2003) [3].
  • The science pitch. Tremor's first recruiting efforts failed because they targeted trendsetters, who "don't spread the word". Once it switched to "trend spreaders" or "connectors", "the program's measured success rate increased drastically" [6]. Recruiting moved online after tests of mall intercepts, P&G mailing lists and phone calls ("Don't get branded" banners) [6].
  • Business model. A service business, P&G's "first business service offering" [6]. A national campaign cost about $1 million or more. About a third of activity was for P&G brands in 2004, and about 80% of work came from outside P&G by 2005-06 [1][10][20]. Tremor CEO Ted Woehrle expected a profit by June 2004 [1]. Knox said P&G sought outside partners "because it does not have enough material of its own to keep the program fresh for teens" [3]. Retaining members was a cost of keeping the channel valuable, not a goal in itself.
  • Champions. Knox (VP of business development, later CEO of Tremor from 2003 to 2010), Woehrle (CEO in 2004), Erika Brown ("Teen Experience Director", 2003), all under Stengel [1][6][21].

Notes on the numbers. Membership claims swing between 200,000 and 300,000 with no clear trend, so treat them as marketing figures. The home page counter "11354 members online" appears unchanged in captures from 2005 to 2008, so it was almost certainly static and isn't a real activity number [8]. What keeps people coming back

Tremor's own design language names the hooks: "Get it first", "Inside scoop" and "Influence power" [6].

  • Getting it first. Unreleased music, sneak peeks and early product. The 2008 pitch read: "Listen to unreleased music - Name the next big movie - Help design a video game - Be the first to try new products" [26]. "It's cool to know about stuff before other people," a 16-year-old member told Forbes [1].
  • Influence that's visible. Members voted on the Win A Date With Tad Hamilton! logo, a Vans Warped Tour T-shirt, a Crest toothbrush design, Vanilla Coke can slogans, the music for a Pringles ad and the model for a Pantene ad. "Tremor Crew Chooses New Ads" was a 2006 news item [1][5][6][8]. Seeing your pick in the real world was the strongest bond in the record: "Oh, my God, I voted for that logo!" [1].
  • Being heard as a teen. "Teens are one of the most disempowered groups out there. They are filled with great ideas, but they don't think anyone listens to them," Knox said [1]. The parents' page called "freedom of speech ... the driving principal of Tremor" [4]. The panel also published its own results back to members, such as "Top 5 magazines Tremor teens read" and "Tremor's Favorite IM/Text Lingo" [8].
  • Mail as a status object. "While for adults receiving mail usually means bills or junk, to a teen it is something special. 'They don't get mail'" [3]. Kits included a Cover Girl tin, a $2 minidisc plus six $10-off coupons, and shampoo [1][3].
  • Insider status and selection. Being one of the roughly 10% admitted, plus status from "being trendsetters" [1][10]. Selection was continuous: "Why don't all Tremor Members receive the same programs?" was a standard FAQ [14].
  • IRL perks for a few. Screenings, fashion week, celebrity interviews ("Tremor Teens Interview the Stars", 2004) and upfront parties (2006) [5][8].
  • What was missing. No points or levels ("We do not use point systems") [4], no member roles, and no evidence of member-led groups. The Local Buzz forum existed but was rule-bound and, from outside, unmeasurable. Members stayed for the next drop, not for each other. Forbes also captured fatigue: "Sometimes it's a hassle if you get more than one e-mail, and they want you to fill things out" [1]. Mismatched programs hurt too: a Tampax "learner's kit" went to girls too old for it, and a 15-year-old without a learner's permit got Valvoline [1].

The content engine

  • Talkable, not repeated, messages. "For something to spread via word of mouth, the message must be unique -- it can't be the same as what's being plugged in a mass marketed campaign" [6]. Head & Shoulders used Tremor for a point "that won't survive in the mass market" (that it kills the fungus behind dandruff) [1].
  • Mailed program kits. Teasers, samples, coupons for friends, stickers. One TV program got script pages with "the juiciest scenes blacked out" ten days before its finale; the AEF excerpt identifies a WB pilot in which panelists reviewed a Dawson's Creek script [6][16].
  • Weekly e-newsletter with new programs [4].
  • Site editorial, roughly biweekly in 2006-2008. Features included "Tremor Guide to Valentine's Day", "Financial Literacy 101", "Election 2008", "GPS Treasure Hunting", partner pushes ("Tide to Go" talking-stain contest, C-SPAN, Coke Zero e-cards) and community items ("Tremor Yearbook", "Want to Make Tremor Better?", "Thank You Tremor Crew") [8][26].
  • Music. Exclusive tracks streamed with member star ratings (Hot Hot Heat, avg 4.14/5, Jan 2006) [27].
  • Member-made content. Member reviews (Talladega Nights, Dell DJ Ditty), member interviews with designers, skateboard and snowboard designs for Schick's "Build A Board" with a public vote, and a "Big Book of Thanks" to hurricane responders in 2005 [8][27]. It was curated by staff and rewarded with a feature or a prize, not with status.
  • Listening infrastructure. Tremor received a "high volume of email" from teens and bought a routing and text-analysis system to turn it into client insight, per the vendor [28].

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. A dedicated P&G business unit with a CEO (Woehrle in 2004; Knox from 2003 by his own bio, CEO until October 2010), a "Teen Experience Director", a "helpcrew@tremor.com" support team, and a spokesperson. Exact headcount: no evidence found [1][6][21][4].
  • Parents. A dedicated "For Parents" page and a parent letter stressed P&G ownership, voluntary membership, privacy and "never tells teens what to say" [4]. Over-20s were rejected at signup [10].
  • Moderation. Local Buzz had a click-through rulebook and a Language Policy, and members reported problems to the Helpcrew [7]. A columnist who signed up in 2005 found thread titles like "Favorite sexual positions", which suggests moderation was thin [10].
  • Compensation. Officially none. Samples "are not compensation for participation" [4]. One member got "sometimes even a small check for taking surveys and participating in focus groups" [5].
  • Feedback into product. Real but client-directed: design votes, ad choices and surveys flowed to brands. This was the product being sold [1][6].
  • No superuser tier. No evidence found of ambassadors, moderators drawn from members, or any member hierarchy.

Growth loops

  1. Paid acquisition plus screening. Online banners and email invitations, with a sweepstakes hook ("register to win a free product, like a DVD player"). Only the most gregarious ~10% were admitted [1][6].
  2. Friend referrals. Parents were told teens "probably heard about us from a friend or saw one of our online ads" [4].
  3. Coupons as referral tokens. Kits carried extra coupons for friends (the Shamrock Farms milk test got 21% redemption, the dairy's highest ever) [1].
  4. Earned media. A Forbes cover story (Feb 2004), which Tremor celebrated as "Tremor Teens on the Cover of Forbes Magazine" [8]. The coverage also seeded the backlash.

These loops grew the panel, not P&G's customer base directly. The customer-facing loop was teens talking to friends: about nine friends each and five store visits per teen in one retail test [6].

Evidence of business impact

All results are company- or client-reported, mostly from test-versus-control markets:

  • Shamrock Farms chocolate-malt milk (2003): sales 18% higher in Phoenix, where 2,100 Tremor teens got kits, than in Tucson after 23 weeks. Overall milk sales in Phoenix rose 4%, and coupon redemption was 21% [1].
  • Teen TV show: ratings up 171% after the blacked-out-script mailing; Knox put the share at 5.6 rising to 15.2 [3][6].
  • Clothing store opening: sales 30% above control stores; each teen told almost nine friends and brought five [6].
  • Cover Girl: claimed purchases up 10% in three test cities [1]; Knox said the brand saw "a sales spike" [3].
  • Revenue: "might top $12 million" in 2004, against a profit target for June 2004 [1].
  • Strategic outcome: the teen panel was a successful pilot for Vocalpoint, which P&G scaled to 600,000 moms by 2006 [20][24]. That, more than teen revenue, was the return. Knox later boiled it down to "relationship x disruptive experience = advocacy" and said a brand needs a relationship with only about 2% of its audience [29].
  • Independent evidence: none specific to Tremor teens was found. A 2004 NYT Magazine piece noted that a BzzAgent study cast doubt on the "magic people" theory and that several BzzAgents were also Tremor members [5].

What they did right

  1. Recruited spreaders, not cool kids. The switch from trendsetters to connectors lifted measured results "drastically" and gave the model a defensible theory [6][3].
  2. Gave real, visible influence. Logo, T-shirt, can and ad votes that members later saw in the world produced the most enthusiastic member quotes on record [1][5].
  3. Designed for conversation. Every program had a social-currency hook (scripts with blacked-out scenes, store layouts, sneak peeks) rather than ad copy [6].
  4. Measured with control markets. Phoenix vs Tucson and control stores made word of mouth sellable at $1M+ per campaign [1].
  5. Kept the ask small. 5-10 minutes a program, a weekly email, no quotas [4].
  6. Used the teen panel as a lab. It produced Vocalpoint, a far bigger network [3][20].

What they did wrong

  1. No disclosure, with minors. Members weren't required to tell anyone, "even ... their own parents" [10]. Forbes ("stealth sales force"; "some kids aren't even aware that they're participating"), a parent ("they need to make it explicit that this is a selling channel"), Commercial Alert's 2005 FTC letter asking for subpoenas, and the Guardian's 2007 piece made Tremor the poster child for "stealth" marketing [1][9][11]. Consequence: lasting reputational baggage (it's still cited on SourceWatch), and the FTC's 2006 staff letter said material connections "should generally be disclosed" [30].
  2. Strategic misfit. P&G "has only a handful of brands relevant to teens" [20], so the panel depended on outside clients to stay fresh [3]. Once moms proved the bigger prize, the teen panel was wound down about two years later [13][18].
  3. A channel, not a community. No status system, member roles or rituals among peers. The forum had thin moderation and, by 2008, few users online [7][10]. Nothing outlived the mailings, and no member-run space or nostalgia remained.
  4. Mismatched targeting and fatigue. Wrong-age kits, too many survey emails, and members who felt "a little weird" handing out shampoo [1].
  5. A quiet shutdown. No evidence of any announcement to members. The pages just went 404 [13].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Let members change something real, then show them. A vote whose result appears in the shipped product or campaign is the most powerful loyalty moment Tremor produced [1].
  2. Give advocates something to share, not something to say. A teaser, a preview or an extra invite beats talking points [6].
  3. Recruit for reach and willingness to talk, not coolness. Screen on network size and propensity to share [6][3].
  4. Keep each ask tiny and predictable. A weekly digest and 5-10-minute tasks [4].
  5. Prove impact with a control group before scaling, as Tremor did market by market [1].

Avoid these

  1. Don't hide the sponsorship, especially with young users. Make disclosure the norm; the backlash outlived the program [1][9][30].
  2. Don't build an audience you can't feed. If your own product line can't keep members busy, the community depends on other people's campaigns and on a business case that can move on [3][20].
  3. Don't mistake a panel for a community. Without peer ties, roles or rituals, members are subscribers to a goodie feed, and they vanish without a trace when it stops [7][13].
