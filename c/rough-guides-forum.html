TL;DR

  • What it was. Rough Guides ran three owned community spaces in turn, never more than one at full strength. Travel Talk (2001–9 Jun 2009) was a regional Q&A forum on rented Infopop/Eve software. A journals-and-reviews "community" (2001–2009) ran first on travel.roughguides.com and later on community.roughguides.com, white-labelled from the startup IgoUgo. A Q&A forum, later a threads forum, came with the 2013 site relaunch (Jan 2013–Dec 2015). All three are gone.
  • Why people came back. Most didn't. Travel Talk never passed about 9,300 registered members or about 3,500 posts a year, and topics averaged roughly one reply. A thin core came back to answer region-specific questions in the Asia, Europe and South America boards. The IgoUgo side kept writers with points redeemable for gift certificates and a Member of the Year award, but that loyalty belonged to IgoUgo, not to Rough Guides.
  • What the company fed it. Mostly editorial content: free guidebook text from 1995, then daily articles and crowdsourced galleries after 2013. Staff took part in only one period, 2013–15, when editors such as Lottie Gross and Tim Chester were among the most active posters. The community never got a host, a superuser program or moderation at any scale.
  • Biggest mistake. Repeated stop-start with no continuity. Travel Talk closed on 9 June 2009 promising "a new Rough Guides forum later this year". It came 3.5 years later, on new software with none of the old posts, and was left to fill with "Buy Instagram followers" spam before it was closed in Dec 2015. Each relaunch began again from zero.
  • The lesson. A beloved product brand does not turn into a community by itself. Without a reason to return, a home the company owns and keeps, and people tending it, the forum stays a side feature that the next reorganisation deletes.

Origin and strategy

  • Free content as marketing (1995–2001). Rough Guides pioneered putting whole guidebooks online free. Associate publisher Jean Marie Kelly said the web edition of Rough Guide USA tripled sales of the next edition: "up to half of those sales came from having the book on the Web" (PW, Oct 1997). By 2001 about a third of the content was online and syndicated as XML (PW, Jan 2001). The community sat inside this logic: more pageviews and brand awareness, which sell books and ad space.
  • Outsourced community (2001–2009). Rough Guides did not build community software. It licensed IgoUgo's journal/rewards platform for travel.roughguides.com. IgoUgo "started off as a partner for Rough Guides" (Madhulika Liddle, 2012) and listed Rough Guides among the licensees of its community (Travel Daily News). The forum itself ran on Infopop's hosted service. Cree Lawson, who sold Rough Guides' online ads from Sept 2001, said the site had "an audience of international travel planners... We just didn't have enough of them." In Dec 2003 he combined RoughGuides.com with IgoUgo and four other sites into an ad network reaching 1 million travellers (Sramana Mitra interview, Jan 2008). In other words, the community was ad inventory.
  • Corporate neglect (2002–2012). Penguin took full ownership in 2002. Rough Guides author Matthew Teller wrote that under Penguin "every year or two came another promise to revamp the RG website to bring it up to LP's standard; it never happened... The website remains an embarrassment" (Teller, Aug 2012).
  • Engagement relaunch (2013). The new site launched on 22 Jan 2013. One of its stated aims was "a place for Rough Guides to engage with their readers, and for readers to engage with each other, via the community forum, blogs, personal profiles". It carried "virtually no advertising" because "the focus is very much on building brand presence and consumer engagement" (PhocusWire, Jan 2013). Publishing director Clare Currie hoped to "connect with many new Rough Guiders" (Breaking Travel News, Feb 2013). Senior web editor Tim Chester, who came from NME.com in April 2012, championed the relaunch, but the plan centred on editorial: "people just want their interests catered to, and then the buying books... follows on from that" (Journalism.co.uk, Jan 2014).
  • Pivot to trips (2018). Under APA, CEO René Frey said "we want to be seen as a dynamic tech company that provides tailormade trips". The company called its ageing readers "astonishingly loyal", but said the only way out was "a transactional business model" (Guardian, Nov 2018). By then there was no owned community left to carry into the new model.

Trajectory (my derivation from the counts above). Travel Talk added about 3,500 posts a year in mid-2003 to 2004, about 3,000 in 2005, about 2,700 in 2006, about 1,550 in 2007 (the year of the platform rename) and about 2,000 in 2008, much of it spam. Posts per topic stayed at about 2.0 every year, so the typical question got one answer. For scale: the Lonely Planet Thorn Tree report counts about 7,900 new topics a year in one Thorn Tree branch alone in 2008–12, while all of Travel Talk never passed about 1,700 a year (lonely-planet-thorn-tree.md). Travel Talk peaked in 2004–05, flattened after 2006 and was closed in 2009. The 2013 forum never built a member core.

What keeps people coming back

Rough Guides had very few retention mechanics. Score 1/5.

  • Topical utility, not belonging. Travel Talk was organised by region (Europe, Asia, South America were the biggest boards) plus Travel Partners, Women Travellers, Round-the-World and Gay & Lesbian boards. People came with a trip question and left once it was answered. "Registration is optional; you can freely read and post messages without registering", which made posting easy but meant low identity and accountability (Wayback, 2002).
  • Fragmented identity. The forum and the journal community needed separate accounts: "If you're already a member of the Rough Guides and IgoUgo community, you'll still have to register for Travel Talk" (Wayback, Dec 2006).
  • Extrinsic rewards on the IgoUgo side. "Write reviews, earn rewards": Go Points redeemable for gift certificates, an editor's thank-you email with suggestions, and a Member of the Year award. One member: "I submitted a few photographs and reviews, and received both an email thanking me and suggesting improvements, and a few 'Go Points'..." (A Mindful Traveler, Nov 2013). This was the strongest loop in the system, but it built IgoUgo's identity, not Rough Guides'. When the white label ended, the writers stayed with IgoUgo.
  • 2013 forum features. Profiles, votes, a "Top Travellers" leaderboard, "Hot topic of the week", "Best travel tip", and competitions such as "WIN a trip to Turkey for two". That competition thread was the busiest on the front page in July 2014, with 30 replies against 0–2 for real questions (Wayback, Jul 2014). Prizes produced spikes, not habits.
  • Parasocial pull of staff (2013–15 only). Editors answered edition and planning questions within about a day. Example: asked "is there going to be a new edition of the Rough Guide to Mexico this year?", Lottie Gross replied the next day with the edition date and a shop link (Wayback, Jun 2014). That is a real hook, but it was never named, scheduled or turned into rituals such as author AMAs.
  • No switching costs. Every relaunch dropped the old posts and profiles, so members had no history to protect.

The content engine

Score 3/5: strong editorial, weak tie to the community.

  • Free guidebook text online from 1995 (HotWired, then roughguides.com), syndicated as XML (PW, 2001).
  • 2013 relaunch: daily articles with a "news feel", full-screen photo galleries, ThingLink interactive maps, and galleries "based on crowdsourced information" such as "the friendliest countries in the world as chosen by you". It won FutureBook Publisher Website of the Year (Nov 2013), and traffic "quadrupled" (Journalism.co.uk).
  • UGC-for-content contests. The Feb 2013 Round-the-World Contest required entrants to register and post 100 words on "the place that changed me". The winner got £4,000 of travel and a blogging slot (Breaking Travel News).
  • Community-sourced trips (2001). "Rough Guides has put together the world's best trips with the help of our community", built from IgoUgo member guides (Wayback, 2001).
  • What was missing. Member corrections never visibly fed back into guidebook updates. The Travel Talk "Feedback – Suggestions for Rough Guides" board had 143 topics by 2006, most with zero replies, including reader update tips such as "Rough Guide NZ update info" and "Crystal Shopping in Prague -- you missed an important one!" (Wayback, May 2006). The obvious "readers update the book" loop, which earlier guidebook publishers ran by post, was never closed online.

People, programs and governance

Score for staff investment: 2/5.

  • 2001–2009: no evidence of a named community manager. Travel Talk ran on a vendor-hosted platform, and ads (Zedo) were inserted into the boards. Feedback-board titles show members asking about governance and getting nothing back: "TravelTalk Management Problems" (Russell B, May 2006, 7 replies, 246 views), "Ads on Europe board" (Baedekker, Apr 2006, 0 replies) and "Advertisements" (Blocker, 2004) (Wayback, May 2006). The post bodies were not archived.
  • Spam. By Dec 2007 the most recent post in several regional boards was spam for Nike shoes ("www.shoes-new.com) sell nike sh...") (Wayback). By Dec 2015 the 2013 forum's newest threads were "Buy Instagram followers", "Buy YouTube Views" and "Buy SoundCloud Plays" (Wayback). Moderation clearly did not keep up in either era.
  • 2013–15: staff-led. Of the 20 "Top Travellers" in July 2014, at least eight are identifiable from their profiles or other sources as Rough Guides staff: Lottie Gross (editorial assistant), Rebecca Hallett, Tim Chester (senior web editor), Keith Drew (managing editor), Rachel Mills, Alison Roberts and Edward Aves (editors), and Clare Currie (publishing director). Several more names appear to be in-house editors. Only a handful of top posters were outside members: a freelance travel writer (261 replies), "PirateAt50" and a US expat in Thailand (Wayback). Staff carried the forum and never handed it over.
  • No superuser, ambassador or MVP program in any era. Contrast Lonely Planet's Pathfinders and its Thorn Tree volunteer moderators (lonely-planet-thorn-tree.md).

Growth loops

  • SEO from Q&A was the only real loop, and every platform change broke it. Infopop URLs gave way to Eve URLs (2007), then to nothing (2009–2012), then to /questions (2013), then to /community/threads (2014), then to deletion (2015). A Wayback CDX check shows /questions/* URLs answering with 301s and 404s from 2014 on.
  • Press listings. Travel journalists listed Travel Talk next to Thorn Tree as a place to post questions (Chicago Tribune, Jun 2002).
  • Brand halo. Book readers were sent to the site ("Check out www.roughguides.com"). Comparison threads on rival forums recommended the books, not the forum.
  • Contests and social. 2013 competitions required registration, and from 2010 Facebook, Twitter and the newsletter were labelled "Community". The 2016 closure page pointed members to a weekly Twitter chat.
  • No referral, ambassador or event loop found.

Evidence of business impact

Score 1/5. No hard evidence links any Rough Guides community space to revenue, retention or book sales. The only numbers are for the content strategy: the claim that the free USA guide tripled sales of the next edition (PW, 1997), site traffic of 760k monthly uniques in Jan 2014 (PW, 2014), and the 2013 award. Book sales went the other way: the division's 2017 sales fell "back down to 2013 levels, its lowest-selling full year on record post-2002", quoted from The Bookseller at the sale to APA (Travelfish, Oct 2017). Rough Guides was never a public company in its own right; it sat inside Pearson, so SEC filings are not relevant.

What they did right

  1. Opened its content early and generously. The free full-text guides of 1995 to 2001 built real web reach and, by the company's account, sold books (PW, 1997).
  2. Built clear, useful board structure. Regional boards plus niche ones (Women Travellers, Gay & Lesbian, Travel Partners) gave the forum a few genuinely valued corners. Fodor's posters pointed Africa travellers to it: "good questions and answers... about Africa" (Fodor's, Mar 2003).
  3. Put real staff into the 2013 forum. Named editors answered quickly and in a friendly voice, which is rare for a publisher (Wayback, 2014).
  4. Used crowdsourcing in content. Reader-voted galleries and writing contests were among the best-performing 2013 content (Journalism.co.uk).
  5. Closed honestly in 2015. The closure page thanked contributors and named an alternative ("Wheelie sorry!... Join our weekly Twitter chat") (Wayback, Jan 2016). The 2009 notice also gave a date. Still, both closures came abruptly and offered no export of members' posts.

What they did wrong

  1. Rented every layer. The forum ran on Infopop/Eve, the journals on IgoUgo, and the leftovers went to Facebook and Twitter. Rough Guides never owned the code, the identity or, in practice, the members. The IgoUgo writers' loyalty went to IgoUgo, which Sabre bought in 2005 and closed in 2013 (A Mindful Traveler). Consequence: when partnerships ended, the community went with them.
  2. A 3.5-year gap after a broken promise. The June 2009 notice said "We will be launching a new Rough Guides forum later this year" (Wayback). From 2010 to 2012 "Community" meant a Facebook link, and the new forum arrived in Jan 2013. Consequence: the regulars scattered to Thorn Tree, TripAdvisor and Fodor's, and the 2013 forum started with no core.
  3. No continuity of content or URLs. Each migration (2007, 2009, 2013, 2014) dropped or broke the old threads. Consequence: no lasting SEO and no history for members to protect.
  4. Neglect let spam take over twice, with Nike spam in 2007 and follower-selling spam in 2015, while member questions about management and ads went unanswered (feedback board, 2004–06). Consequence: the community looked abandoned just before each closure.
  5. Staff-dependent in 2013–15. Staff were most of the top posters, and there was no plan to grow member answerers, moderators or ambassadors. Consequence: when web-team priorities moved toward shop, flights, hostels and tours links (visible in the 2015–16 navigation), the forum had no one else to keep it going. It closed after under three years.
  6. No business model for the community. It was framed first as ad inventory, then as "engagement". It was never tied to book updates, subscriptions or (after 2018) trip sales, so it was always easy to cut. The 2018 pivot to trips for "time-poor" 40–60-year-olds (Guardian) left no community to sell to. A correction for the master list: one secondary source says the forum closed in 2018 "when they started selling their own tours" (The Longest Way Home). Wayback shows it closed by 1 Jan 2016.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Use your product's content as the front door. Rough Guides' free content drove traffic and, by the company's account, sales. Pair your docs and how-tos with a place to ask follow-up questions on the same page.
  2. Put named staff in the threads, quickly and warmly, as the 2013 editors did. Then make it a ritual (office hours, author Q&As) instead of ad hoc.
  3. Run small, targeted boards where your users have specific, high-stakes needs (Rough Guides' Women Travellers and Africa boards). These are where the "gems" pile up.
  4. Turn member contributions into visible product changes. The guidebook-update loop was Rough Guides' biggest missed opportunity. Credit members when their tip changes the product.
  5. Make rewards point back to your brand. IgoUgo's points-for-reviews and Member of the Year loop worked, but for IgoUgo. If you reward contribution, let the rewards build identity with your product, such as credit toward your own product, badges inside it, or paid contributor roles.

Avoid these

  1. Renting the community and its identity. Hosted forums and white-label partners make launch easy and exit fatal. Own the accounts, the URLs and the data export.
  2. Stop-start relaunches. Promising a forum "later this year" and arriving 3.5 years later on new URLs with no archive means starting from zero each time. Migrate content and keep URLs.
  3. A community with no job in the business model. If it is "engagement" with no metric (retention, support deflection, product feedback, trip sales), it is the first thing cut, as happened here in 2009 and 2015.
