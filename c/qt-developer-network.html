TL;DR

  • What it is: the official forum and wiki for the Qt C++/QML framework. Nokia's Qt unit launched it in 2010 as the "Qt Developer Network" (DevNet). It moved to qt-project.org in 2011 and to forum.qt.io (NodeBB) plus wiki.qt.io (MediaWiki) in 2015. Unlike Forum Nokia, which Microsoft wound down, it survived Nokia's exit through Digia and The Qt Company (AllAboutSymbian, 2010; Qt blog, 2015).
  • Why people came back: Qt is hard and long-lived, so developers came back with real problems. A small core of volunteers came back because they enjoyed answering and being recognised. The forum's first Qt Champion, Samuel Gaist ("SGaist"), had posted 20,000 replies by January 2016 (forum, 2016).
  • What the company fed it: a playful points-and-ranks system ("Lab Rat" up to "Mad Scientist"), weekly leaderboards, badges tied to paid certification, the Qt docs and an FAQ generated nightly from the support database, the Qt Champions awards (from 2014), annual conferences, and since 2023 the free Qt Academy.
  • Biggest mistake: commercial moves that taxed the free community, made in 2015 and again in 2020: required Qt Account logins, LTS releases and the offline installer made commercial-only, and an 18-month-plus gap with no community manager. On top of that, the 2015 migration scrapped the gamification. Volunteer moderators had to tell angry users "this is a user forum, we cannot undo this decision" (forum, 2020).
  • The lesson: a devtools forum can outlive its sponsor if volunteers own the answering and get public, durable recognition. Still, it cannot outrun a shrinking free user base or AI assistants answering the easy questions. Post volume fell about 90% from 2021 to 2026, by our estimate.

What the community is

The Qt Forum (forum.qt.io) is the official Q&A and discussion board for Qt. Most activity sits in the "Qt Development" category (144,736 of 157,519 visible topics). Smaller categories cover General talk, Qt Learning, Special Interest Groups, International (language sub-forums) and "Behind the Scenes" (forum.qt.io categories API, accessed 2026-11-07). Login goes through Qt Account, the same identity used for bug reports, code review and The Qt Company's commercial Support Center (forum login page). Around it sit:

  • wiki.qt.io, a community-editable MediaWiki (migrated from ExpressionEngine in February 2015) (Qt Wiki).
  • The Qt Project mailing lists and IRC/Matrix channels for contributors (Online Communities).
  • Qt Champions, an annual recognition program since 2014 (Qt blog).
  • Qt World Summit (formerly Qt Developer Days) and the Qt Contributors Summit.
  • Qt Academy, free e-learning launched in April 2023 (press release).

The company's own wiki lists the unofficial spaces: the Qt Discord, r/QtFramework, a CppLang Slack channel, Telegram groups, a LinkedIn group and Felgo's forums (Online Communities). The older, independent Qt Centre (qtcentre.org, launched January 2006 "with the support of Trolltech" by ex-admins of an earlier Qt Forum) still exists but is barely maintained (KDE.news, 2006; forum, 2018).

Origin and strategy

Qt's web team drew up a three-year website plan before Christmas 2008. Nokia funded DevNet in autumn 2009. It went live in closed beta on 6 May 2010 and replaced "rather sad" forums on Qt Labs. The goal: "an organized place to collect both internal and external Qt knowledge," with a forum, wiki, blog, an FAQ "generated and auto-updated from our support database every night," and "a nice system of points and rewards" (Qt Labs blog via Wayback, 2010-05-06). It opened to everyone in July 2010. Observers saw it as a "more vendor neutral destination for developers than Forum Nokia," something other handset makers might link to (AllAboutSymbian, 2010-07-14). Web community manager Alexandra Leisse, a KDE veteran, championed it inside Nokia (Open Advice). Members took her 2012 departure as a blow to "such a small team" (forum, 2012).

The strategy shifted with each owner:

  • Nokia (2010–2011): an adoption engine for Symbian/MeeGo. Badges for Nokia-certified developers tied the community to paid certification exams (forum, 2011; forum, 2011).
  • Qt Project open governance (October 2011–): DevNet became qt-project.org. From late 2012, Digia worked with forum moderators to put the website, forums and wiki under open governance as well (Qt blog, 2011; Qt blog, 2013).
  • The Qt Company (2015–): everything was folded into qt.io under Qt Account, described as "the gateway to what we are building as a new Qt developer portal" (Qt blog, 2015). In 2020 the company made the funnel logic explicit: a required account "allows us to initiate a dialogue with commercial companies who mostly work with open-source versions of Qt" (Qt blog, 2020). Its annual report lists "Qt Forum/Community" as the way it engages the open-source community, for "thorough testing of releases" and "bug fixes, new product releases and new product ideas" (Qt Group Annual Report 2025).

Trajectory: steady growth from 2010 to about 2014, then a long plateau at roughly 10–12k topics a year through 2021, with a small pandemic bump in 2020. Decline began in 2022 and steepened sharply in 2025–26. Regulars noticed: "only the past 3 or 4 months where it really quieted down" (December 2025). By March 2026: "I log on every few days and see virtually nothing new" (forum thread "decline of Qtforums"). The unofficial subreddit has held steady at about 70–100 posts a month in the same period, so the drop is mostly a forum effect, not only Qt losing popularity.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Hard, recurring problems. Qt is a large C++ framework with long product lifecycles, so people return with real build, deployment and API questions. Most traffic arrived from search engines: "quite a lot of the viewer traffic to the forums comes from different search engines" (Qt blog, 2015).
  2. Playful status (2010–2015). DevNet's ranks went Lab Rat → Ant Farmer → Hobby Entomologist → Robot Herder → Dinosaur Breeder → Gene Splicer → Area 51 Engineer → Mad Scientist (forum, 2012). The homepage showed a "Hall of Fame" and a "Top 5 this week" (Wayback, 2013). Members asked for ranks beyond Mad Scientist (forum, 2010). Some even scripted ways to game the points; staff worked with them to close the holes (same thread). Points expired after 12 months, which split the regulars: some liked that "the new guys can claim glory," while others found watching "tens of thousands of points evaporate" demotivating (forum, 2013).
  3. Reputation and visible expertise (2015–). NodeBB swapped the ranks for likes-based reputation, badges and groups (Qt blog, 2015). Users read signatures such as "Lifetime Qt Champion" or "Moderators" to judge answers. When a 2024 upgrade hid who had upvoted a post, a regular complained that he relied on knowing whether an upvote came from "a 'Qt champion'... or some random guy" (forum, 2024).
  4. Recognition with real rewards. Each Qt Champion gets a one-year commercial licence and swag. Anyone named three years in a row becomes a Lifetime Champion and helps pick future winners (Qt blog, 2025).
  5. A friendlier culture than Stack Overflow. One regular wrote in 2026 that Stack Overflow's gatekeeping and toxicity are things "Non of this can be said about this Qt forum" (forum). New posters are greeted with "hi and welcome to devnet," a name that has outlived the brand by 15 years (forum, 2025).
  6. IRL ritual. Qt Developer Days ran from 2004, with a support lab where trolls (Qt engineers) solved attendees' problems on site (Qt blog, 2010). Qt World Summit replaced it in 2015, and the Contributors Summit continues (Oslo, October 2026) (forum announcements).

There is no evidence of in-product hooks: Qt Creator does not surface forum content or notifications.

The content engine

  • Reference content as the anchor. In 2011 DevNet absorbed the Qt docs ("Facedoc") and added commenting, rating and bookmarking (Qt blog, 2011). It also had the nightly FAQ generated from support tickets (Qt Labs, 2010).
  • Blog and member guest posts. The DevNet blog ran member posts. The first was by "chetankjain, a Mad Scientist on Qt Developer Network" (April 2011), alongside eLearning videos (Wayback, 2011).
  • Wiki for member-written guides; translations into 20+ languages are visible on its pages (Online Communities).
  • Webinars, conference videos, and since 2023 Qt Academy. The Academy is a free course catalogue partly written by community members such as Alexandra Betouni and Lukas Kosiński, who were later named Qt Champions (Qt blog, 2025).
  • Champion categories reward member content: "Content Creator", "Community Builder", "Ambassador", "Rookie", "Developer" (Qt blog, 2025).

What's missing is any regular editorial cadence on the forum itself: no weekly threads, challenges or seasons. A consultant who took part conceded that Qt Academy "might be a sign that almost noone wants to create Qt-related content out of their genuine interest" (forum, 2025).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: always small. In 2012: Alexandra Leisse plus a few others, "such a small team" (forum). Tero Kojo was community manager from about 2014 to the late 2010s (Qt Wiki Admins). Pedro Bessa arrived in July 2022 and noted "There have been no CM... for quite some time" and a forum-maintenance "roadblock" (forum, 2022). He left on 22 August 2025 (forum). Since then, Qt Company engineer Axel Spoerl has posted the community announcements (Champions, the survey, summits). No successor community manager was found (announcements).
  • Volunteer moderators (jsulm, Christian Ehrlicher, SGaist, aha_1980, mrjj and others) answer most questions. They have run since 2010, when users asked how to become moderators (forum, 2011). In 2013 the web open-governance plan gave them new roles, lazy approval and a conflict-resolution board (Qt blog).
  • Programs: the Qt Ambassador Program (2010, a showcase for projects built with Qt; beta run through Qt Centre) (Qt blog). Qt Champions (2014–, public nominations on the wiki, about 30 nominations in 2024) (Qt blog).
  • Rules: the Qt Code of Conduct (QUIP 12, adopted 2018 from KDE's) applies to the forum (forum; mailing list). New accounts face rate limits and reputation thresholds against spam, which genuine newcomers sometimes run into (forum, 2021; forum, 2024).
  • Feedback loop: a "Behind the Scenes"/Beta category and the QTWEBSITE bug tracker for site issues. Forum regulars become code contributors: SGaist's first patch fixed a QFileDialog bug he found while answering a question (Qt blog, 2014). In 2025, nearly 200 non-employee contributors wrote about 15% of commits (Qt blog).

Growth loops

  • SEO: 15+ years of solved threads rank for Qt error messages. The 2015 migration kept thread-level redirects because search traffic mattered (Qt blog).
  • Account funnel: forum login requires a Qt Account, which by 2020 also gated binary downloads. The company used it to find commercial prospects among open-source users (Qt blog, 2020).
  • Forum → contributor → champion ladder. Champions show up at events and on other platforms; for example, Community Builders active on both the forum and Discord (Qt blog).
  • Events brought in newcomers: at Dev Days 2010 "a large pack of hands went up" when the crowd was asked who was at their first (Qt blog).

Evidence of business impact

No hard numbers link the forum to revenue, retention or support savings; we found none. The evidence is indirect:

  • Qt Group grew into a listed company with roughly €209M in 2024 net sales (implied by EBITA of €71.2M at a 34.1% margin). It describes itself as "trusted by... over 1.5 million developers" (Annual Report 2024; Annual Report 2025). These are company claims, and the forum is not broken out.
  • Qt Account reached "almost one million" registrations by January 2020, and forum login was one of its main early drivers (Qt blog).
  • The annual report lists the Qt Forum/Community as a stakeholder channel for release testing and product ideas (AR 2025).
  • Support deflection is implied: volunteers answer free users, while paying customers use the separate Support Center.

What they did right

  1. Recognition built for long-term regulars, not drive-by activity. Lifetime Champion status, a commercial licence as a prize, and letting veterans pick the next winners kept a core of top answerers (SGaist, jsulm, Christian Ehrlicher, mrjj, aha_1980) active for more than a decade (Qt blog).
  2. Fun, branded gamification at launch. The joke rank names and weekly leaderboards gave DevNet an identity, and members asked for more of it (forum, 2010).
  3. Survived three owners and three platforms by keeping content and redirects through every migration, and the "devnet" name lives on in the culture (Qt blog, 2015). Forum Nokia's boards, by contrast, were dropped by Microsoft (featured board post blog).
  4. Shared governance with moderators (2012–13 web open governance) gave volunteers real ownership (Qt blog).
  5. Fed it with reference content: support-database FAQ, integrated docs, and later free courses that funnel learners into the forum (Qt Labs, 2010; Qt Academy).
  6. Backed down when the community pushed back, at least once. In 2015 the company made Qt Account optional in the installer, admitting "this was not our finest moment" (Qt blog, 2015; quoted on the forum).

What they did wrong

  1. Repeatedly taxing the free community. In 2020 the company required accounts again and made LTS releases and the offline installer commercial-only (Qt blog). It also floated holding back all releases for 12 months, which KDE said "would mean the end of contributions via Open Governance in practice" (LWN; OSnews). The forum absorbed the anger: volunteer moderators had no power to change policy, and users said they would leave Qt (forum, 2020; forum, 2021). In 2025, regulars named licensing and "hiding of Open Source downloads" as reasons students stopped showing up (forum).
  2. Throwing away the gamification in 2015. Ranks and cross-site points were dropped, profile data was not migrated, and the promised "unified-level point program" never appears to have shipped (no evidence found) (Qt blog).
  3. Understaffing and staff gaps. Losing Leisse in 2012 left the future "foggy" (forum). There was a multi-year gap before 2022, a stalled forum upgrade (forum), and no visible replacement after August 2025.
  4. Platform upgrades that broke trust signals, such as hiding who upvoted in 2024 (forum). Spam is a constant ("Currently forum is spammed by Chinese bots and is useless," forum). AI-generated posts have started appearing too (forum, 2026).
  5. No answer to AI. Post volume is down about 90% from its peak (ID-sequence estimate), and we found no company effort to reposition the forum.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Recognise the top 1% heavily and permanently. A product licence, a title shown on every post, and a say in choosing future honourees (Lifetime Champions) kept answerers engaged for more than ten years.
  2. Give status a personality. Joke rank names and a weekly top-5 cost little and gave DevNet an identity. Make points durable, or show people their history, so expiring points don't feel like a penalty.
  3. Hand real governance to volunteer moderators (roles, lazy approval, conflict resolution) so the community outlives any one staff member or owner.
  4. Feed the forum from your support desk and docs (auto-generated FAQ, comments on docs) and keep every URL alive through each migration; search traffic is the growth loop.
  5. Build a ladder from asking to answering to contributing to being honoured. SGaist went from forum answers to Qt patches.

Avoid these

  1. Don't monetise by restricting the free users who fill your forum. Account walls and commercial-only releases turned the forum into a complaints desk and hollowed out the student pipeline.
  2. Don't scrap engagement systems in a platform migration without shipping something just as good, and don't leave the community manager seat empty for years.
  3. Don't assume Q&A volume is permanent. Plan for AI absorbing the easy questions: put your effort into the hard, version-specific, opinion and showcase content an assistant can't provide.

Score rationale: retention mechanics 3: strong recognition, but the gamification was gutted in 2015 and there are no rituals or product hooks. Content engine 3: rich reference content and Academy, but no forum programming. Member ownership 4: volunteers moderate, answer and help pick champions. Staff investment 2: a tiny team with repeated gaps. Business impact evidence 2: company claims only. Durability 4: 16 years across three owners, though now in steep decline.

Related communities worth adding

  • Qt Centre (qtcentre.org) — independent, Trolltech-supported (2006); hosted the Ambassador beta; now neglected
  • Forum Nokia / Nokia Developer — Nokia — the contrast case: dropped after Microsoft acquired Nokia's devices business (2014)
  • Maemo.org / MeeGo forums — Nokia / Linux Foundation — sponsor-funded developer communities left behind by platform changes
  • Symbian Foundation developer community — Nokia-led foundation — shut down in 2010–11
  • KDE community and the KDE Free Qt Foundation — the open-source counterweight that limits what Qt's owner can do
  • Felgo developer forums — Felgo (V-Play) — Qt-based SDK vendor with its own community
  • Qt Discord — volunteer-run with Qt staff moderators — where some of the activity has moved
  • Flutter community (Google) — competitor whose free model forum regulars cite as pulling students away
