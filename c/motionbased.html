TL;DR

  • What it was: MotionBased (Sausalito, CA; public beta June 2004) was a web app that turned GPS tracks into analysis. Every upload also went into the TrailNetwork, a public map of routes that by 2006 claimed to be "the largest online network of GPS activities in the world." Its signature feature was Dot Racing: a virtual race between two recordings of the same route, either yourself against yourself or against another member. Garmin bought it in October 2005 [1][2][14].
  • Why people came back: your training log lived there (switching cost), you could race your own past self (a reason to upload the next ride), and you could find routes near home or while travelling. A self-supporting forum also became the main help desk for Garmin's Forerunner and Edge devices [14][9][24].
  • How the company fed it: a tiny crew of about six people wrote MBlog, sent a monthly newsletter to more than 40,000 people, featured members' activities and athletes, sponsored "Team MotionBased", and put GPS course previews of big races (Sea Otter, Escape from Alcatraz, major marathons) online [14][10][11][7].
  • Biggest mistake: Garmin rebuilt the product as Garmin Connect, missed the migration date by a year (May 2008, then Dec 2008, then mid-2009), froze uploads, made the forum read-only and later deleted it, and shipped the replacement without Dot Racing, the Analyzer or saved reports. The SF team was shut down in 2010. When the site closed in November 2010, up to 1% of activities had still not been migrated [12][13][16][17][19].
  • The lesson: the community's glue was a product feature (Dot Racing plus a public route graph), not the forum. Delete the feature in a migration and the community goes with it. Strava, founded in 2009, later built a business on the same idea (segments) [26].

What the community is

MotionBased was a subscription web service for athletes and outdoor users with GPS devices. Users installed the MB Agent (PC; a Safari web plug-in for Mac from 2006), uploaded tracks from Garmin, Timex or Suunto units, and got dashboards, maps, a "Map Player" replay, weather data and MB Gravity elevation correction [3][11]. Its community layers were:

Unofficial spaces: the Yahoo "GarminF" group and threads on MTBR, Slowtwitch, BikeForums and LetsRun. The MB Forum was officially "self supportive" [14][21][24].

Origin and strategy

Co-founders included Aaron Roller (CTO, "developed most of the core code"). Sebastian Johnck joined in 2003 [2][18]. Testing started in December 2003 with GPS devices given to 20 endurance athletes. The free beta followed in June 2004 and the press launch on 4 Aug 2004 [3]. The pitch blended "elite number crunchers" with fun: "the online mapping and 'dot racing' features… provide a more fun, interactive aspect to training" [3]. Popular Science gave it a 2004 Best of What's New award [4].

Business model. Paid tiers launched on 2 Mar 2005. MB Lite (free) had unlimited uploads, but analysis and mapping covered only your 10 most recent activities. MB Standard cost $11.95/mo or $7.95/mo billed annually ($95.40/yr) [4][5]. Aaron Roller explained the reasoning: no time-limited trial, and "if they like the service… they can always upgrade… and get instant access to their historical data" [4]. Upgrading therefore unlocked your own history, a lock-in the community created. From late 2006 the site and forum also carried ads (AdSense, Chitika, a media kit). Paying subscribers saw fewer ads [11][14][9].

Why Garmin bought it (3 Oct 2005, terms undisclosed). CEO Min Kao: "it complements our strategy to penetrate the fitness market… over 11,000 GPS users have registered… approximately 140,000 individual outdoor activities" [1]. Garmin's 2005 10-K lists the acquisition [6]. The two firms had already shared a booth at Outdoor Retailer, and MotionBased had exclusive communication with the Forerunner 301 [8][4]. The founders wrote on MBlog that bootstrapping was "too much", that they were staying in the Bay Area and that the product would not become free: "The business model has not changed" [20].

The forum grew about 15,000-19,000 posts a year from 2007 to 2008, roughly 40-50 a day. It stopped when Garmin froze it in autumn 2009. Forum members (about 21k) were a small slice of the roughly 100k monthly visitors; most users were lurkers or route browsers. The forum count may not match the customer count because forum registration was split from MB accounts in 2006 "for security reasons" [9].

What keeps people coming back

  1. Racing your past self and your friends (Dot Racing). This was the hook members talked about most. "The dot racing was the reason I ran faster the next day by forcing myself to not slow down in the hill section"; "Dot Racing was one of the main reasons why I paid for a full MB membership" [16]. The media kit called it a "popular feature that enables customers to race multiple instances of a route on a map" [14]. It worked like asynchronous competition years before Strava segments.
  2. A shared route map built from members' uploads. Every upload added to the TrailNetwork. It doubled as route discovery ("I have searched MB extensively for tracks in new areas") and as the reason non-owners visited. Over 30% of traffic went to the content and community properties [14][24].
  3. Status from what you logged. Users could be ranked by activity count, distance and time logged. MBlog described a power user as "ranked 15th on the TrailNetwork based on the number of activities logged… 9th compared to all runners" [23]. A homepage "Featured Activity" and regional "MotionBased Numbers" league tables in the newsletter added light competition between regions [10][11].
  4. Lock-in through your history. The 10-activity limit on the free tier and the promise that "your training data… will never get lost" made MB the place your history lived [3][4]. When the migration lost data, that trust turned into anger (see below).
  5. Peer support. Most forum traffic was device help (Forerunner 205/305, Edge 205/305/705). The forum became a de facto Garmin support board, staffed lightly by moderators jasper_hyde and the staffer "runkk" [9][24].
  6. Partner races. Course previews let racers "dissect and memorize the course" before Sea Otter, which tied the site to real events on members' calendars [7].

No evidence was found of a follow/friend graph, comments on activities, badges or challenges. Socially, it was a map plus a forum, not a feed.

The content engine

  • MBlog (from 4 Mar 2005) was written by "the Crew" (Aaron, Brad, Cathy, Clark, Mike, Sebastian). It mixed releases, tips ("GPS Reception", "MB Gravity"), athlete stories ("Weir Dominates Downieville") and milestones ("TrailNetwork 100 Large") [20][21].
  • The monthly newsletter had a fixed format: feature news, an "MB Tips" item, an "MB Featured Athlete" and "MotionBased Numbers". Examples include swimmer Gary Emich (358+ Alcatraz swims), Mark Weir's "1 Million Foot" year and Team MB at the TransAlp [10][11]. Every issue ended: "Now get off that computer and start trackin'!!!"
  • Event course previews covered Kona 2004, Sea Otter 2005/06, Escape from Alcatraz 2005/06, the 2006/07 marathon series (Boston, Chicago, NYC, Marine Corps and others) and Cougar Mountain. Each had downloadable .crs course files and video fly-throughs [22][7].
  • Team MotionBased (Jul 2006): "we recruited some of MB's finest", elite members who shared stories by RSS and email [11].
  • Member co-design: a product-roadmap survey that rewarded five random respondents with free years [11], a busy Feature Requests board (1,898 posts by May 2008) [9], and gift subscriptions [10].

All of this was staff-made. Member content came from the activities themselves, which was the network's real asset.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: about 6 named crew members in 2005 [21]. After the acquisition the SF office grew into the Garmin Connect team of about 10-15 engineers, QA and PMs [18]. No dedicated community manager role was found. The forum had one or two moderators [9].
  • Power user to employee: the first hire after the acquisition was Brad Culberson, an Ironman triathlete ranked 15th on the TrailNetwork with 108 forum posts. He had built MB Gravity and a Google Maps Dot Racing prototype as a contractor. MBlog: "a good example of how being a power user of MB is so important to making it better" [23].
  • Feedback loop: forum feature requests, the 2006 roadmap survey and staff (runkk) posting migration updates. After the move, users on Garmin's own forums complained of silence: "There never seems to be a response from Garmin… Why have a forum unless you actually want feedback?" [15].

Growth loops

  • Hardware bundling: "most Garmin product users will find out about it in the product literature" [25]. Garmin's device launches (Edge, Aug 2005) brought new users [21].
  • Shareable links: opening the TrailNetwork (Dec 2005) removed link encryption and added a "Link to this Activity" invitation page for newcomers, plus email sharing and "Viewports" for blogs [10]. The walled garden had blocked SEO, and even after opening, "crawlers/bots can't read" the JavaScript [10].
  • Events and press: Sea Otter co-marketing (10,000 athletes, 50,000 fans) [7]; Popular Science award [4]; forum-signature and word-of-mouth posts on Slowtwitch ("you gotta try it, it is SUPER COOL!") and MetaFilter [27][28].
  • Gift subscriptions at the holidays [10].

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard: traffic and engagement figures (media kit, Aug 2006) [14]; the acquisition itself [1][6]; growth from 100 to 11,000 customers in 15 months [20]; 4M+ activities by 2008 [12].
  • No evidence found: paying-subscriber counts, revenue, churn or conversion rates. "Lite is FREE and the choice for most users" suggests that conversion was low [14].
  • Indirect: the codebase, team and data became Garmin Connect, which by 2010 had 20M activities and 290% growth in visits in 2009 [18]. The DC Rainmaker blog called the Connect team "one thing that really differentiated" Garmin from its competitors [19].

What they did right

  1. A social mechanic built into the core action. Each upload added to a shared map and could become a race. Members cite Dot Racing as the reason they paid and returned [16].
  2. Free tier without a time limit. The 10-activity limit meant everyone could keep using it while their history piled up behind the paywall [4].
  3. Opening the walled garden to make sharing and recruitment easier (Dec 2005) [10].
  4. Spotlighting real members in the newsletter and blog (Featured Activity, Featured Athlete, regional league tables) at almost no cost [10][11].
  5. Hiring a power user and doing it publicly [23].
  6. Content tied to events athletes cared about (course previews for partner races) [7][22].

What they did wrong

  1. A migration that ran a year late. It was planned for 21 May 2008 [13], pushed to "December 2008" [12], then opened in stages from 26 May 2009. MB stopped accepting fitness uploads on 30 Jun 2009, and by 16 Jul 2009 only 80% of activities had migrated [17][29]. During the gap, people who had activities on both sites were frozen out (DC Rainmaker: "a bone-headed move") [19].
  2. Shipping without the features people came for. Garmin's own status page admitted: "Garmin Connect does not offer the Analyzer, Saved Reports, Dot Racing, or Viewports features." The "Dot Racing" thread on Garmin's forum was still being bumped a year later at 290,000 views [17][16]. "Bring back dot racing!" threads were still open in 2011 [15]. The public searchable route database also went away for a while ("the only way to share a ride is email a link. Pathetic.") [24].
  3. Deleting the community. The forum went read-only in late 2009, and its final posts were spam. It and MBlog were offline by March 2010 [9][30]. About 75,000 posts of device knowledge were lost.
  4. Losing data, after promising it would never happen. Closure notice, 9 Nov 2010: "All but less than one percent of the activities… have been migrated… some remaining activities… cannot be migrated." One user lost more than 100 activities from 2007 [31].
  5. Paid promises that never shipped. "Garmin Connect Plus" ($40/yr) was promised to MB Standard subscribers in 2008. Later updates said "Garmin Connect does not have a premium service" [13][12][17]. Garmin's first paid Connect+ tier did not arrive until March 2025 [32].
  6. Cutting the founding team. On 8 Feb 2010 Garmin closed the SF office (it shut on 8 May 2010). The team turned down relocation to Kansas and set up "thegcteam.com" to market itself as "for hire" [18][19].
  7. Reliability and ads. Reviewers hit long processing delays and auth outages ("33% uptime ain't good") [33]. Ads arrived on a paid community in late 2006 [11].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make every user action add to a shared asset (an upload grows the route map) so the community grows from normal use, not only from forum posts.
  2. Give users an asynchronous rival, including their past selves. Dot Racing gave each workout a reason to compare and come back.
  3. Use a feature-limited free tier, not a time-limited trial. Let history pile up and charge to unlock depth.
  4. Feature members on a steady schedule (activity of the week, athlete stories, regional numbers) and hire your best power user.
  5. Open public, linkable pages with a welcome page for newcomers so that sharing brings in new users.

Avoid these

  1. Never migrate without carrying over the community's signature feature and its history. Ship the new platform when it can do what the old one did, and run both in parallel.
  2. Don't let the forum rot before you kill it (read-only, filling with spam, then deleted without an archive). Archive it or move it.
  3. Don't make promises to paying members you can't keep. Broken pledges about a premium tier and full migration destroyed trust that took years to build.

Score notes: retention mechanics 3: Dot Racing and the route map were strong, but there was no social graph or recognition system. Content engine 3: a steady blog, newsletter and event content, all from a tiny staff. Member ownership 2: members supplied routes and support but had no programs or governance. Staff investment 2: about 6 people and no community manager; the forum was neglected by 2009. Business impact evidence 2: only traffic figures and the acquisition, no revenue or retention data. Durability 1: shut down within about 5 years of the acquisition.
