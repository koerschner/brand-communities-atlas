TL;DR

  • What it was: a "network within a network" for small businesses. Visa launched it on 24 June 2008 as a Facebook Platform app, the first Facebook app built only for small businesses. AKQA and AllWidgets.com built it, and the launch came with $2 million in Facebook ad credits ($100 each for the first 20,000 US businesses). In 2009 it moved to a standalone site, visabusinessnetwork.com, with Facebook login. It was relaunched in Aug 2010 with goal toolkits, mentors and Q&A, quietly closed in late 2012, and redirected to a Visa Small Business Facebook page (Visa press release, 2008; Visa, 2010; Wayback CDX).
  • Why people came: mostly the free $100 Facebook ad credit. Deal sites (SlickDeals, MyMoneyBlog, BlackHatWorld, Etsy forums) passed the offer around, and early reviewers predicted that "half will probably sign-up to redeem their free coupon and never return" (Small Business Trends comments, 2008). The weaker draw was structured how-to "goals" and a library of syndicated articles.
  • What Visa fed it: media partnerships (Entrepreneur, WSJ, Forbes, Fast Company, AllBusiness, Microsoft, Google tools), print and online ads, Facebook news-feed ads (its "most efficient channel"), outreach to bloggers, and in 2010 a small roster of outside expert mentors. As far as the evidence shows, no staff community managers, rituals or events.
  • Biggest mistake: Visa bought sign-ups instead of building a reason to come back, then let the hook decay into a public grievance. When the credits ran out, people who had installed the app for the coupon called it "bait and switch" and removed it (MyMoneyBlog comments, Dec 2009). Two years after the relaunch, the Q&A held about 140 questions for a claimed 100,000–150,000 members.
  • The one lesson: a paid sign-up incentive and a headline member count are not a community. Member-to-member value, staff who host it, and a recurring reason to return must exist before you pay to fill the room, or the incentive becomes the product.

What the community is

The Visa Business Network (VBN) was Visa Inc.'s free online network for small-business owners in 2008–2012. It ran in three forms:

Period Form Evidence
Jun 2008 – 2009 Facebook Platform app (apps.facebook.com/visabusiness) with business profiles, a "Business Finder" search (industry, size, location, owner gender, home/office/online), "Add Associates", a "Back Office" message centre, and a resource centre with Google Docs/Calendar/Sites/Maps, Entrepreneur and WSJ "Ask the Experts" Small Business Trends, 24 Jun 2008; Finovate, 24 Jun 2008
~Aug 2009 – Jul 2010 Standalone visabusinessnetwork.com ("You've Got Big Dreams. And You're in Good Company") with Facebook login, goal toolkits (online marketing and cash flow), a live "Recent Network Activity" feed, and the $100 ad credit offer back on the page Wayback, 17 Sep 2009
Aug 2010 – late 2012 Relaunch: Business Goal Toolkits, Small Business Mentor Program, Answers Q&A forum, content ranking, Library, Featured Business Visa, 11 Aug 2010; Bob Pullum (AKQA CD) portfolio
Jun 2012 → Successor: Visa Small Business Facebook Page, rebranded "Well Sourced by Visa Business" on 4 Dec 2012. visabusinessnetwork.com redirects there Media Logic, 31 Dec 2012; Wayback redirect 2013-04-05; live redirect checked 2026-11-08

Who ran it: Visa's small-business marketing team: Alex Craddock (Head of Small Business Marketing), Rich Yang (senior business leader), and from 2010 Raghav Lal (Head of Global Small Business) as spokesman. AKQA San Francisco handled creative and build, AllWidgets.com built the Facebook app, OMD West ran media, and Fleishman-Hillard did PR (MediaPost OMD credit list; Ad Age, Nov 2008, via Wayback).

Unofficial spaces: no fan-run space existed. The only off-platform conversation found is on deal-hunting and marketer sites about the ad coupon (SlickDeals, MyMoneyBlog, BlackHatWorld, Etsy forums). Reddit: no evidence found (Reddit was small in 2008–2012, and Brave turned up nothing).

Status today: defunct. The domain still 301-redirects to facebook.com/visasmallbiz, which shows Facebook's "This content isn't available right now" (checked 2026-11-08).

Origin and strategy

  • Why Facebook: Craddock's thesis was to go where small businesses already were rather than pay to build a destination. "We felt this would be a lot more efficient than putting significant dollars into attracting an audience [to a proprietary community]... We wanted to go where small businesses are already going online" (Ad Age, Nov 2008). Facebook's ~80,000 business pages "appear[ed] to be the single biggest online community of small businesses" (SFGate, 24 Jun 2008). Visa also cited Forrester's forecast of 20–33% growth in SMB social-media use (Small Business Trends).
  • Business model link: VBN was brand marketing, not a product. Visa does not issue cards, so a small-business owner's "membership" had no direct tie to any Visa account. You did not need to be a cardholder to join (Small Business Trends). Visa's product pitch came through the goals ("Get Paid With Credit Cards", "Save Money with Business Credit Cards") and the "Visa Resources" tab (Wayback, May 2012). Copywriter Vinit Patil described it as "the first time Visa engaged with their customers as a brand versus advertising to them" (portfolio).
  • Facebook's stake: the $2M in credits was also a demonstration for Facebook's unproven ad product. Facebook's Dan Rose said Visa's program "could turn into a financial catalyst for Facebook if the businesses receiving the ad credits are impressed enough... 'That's what we certainly are hoping for'" (AP via ABC7, 24 Jun 2008).
  • Day-one skepticism: Finovate asked "how much effort the card giant will devote to the service. It doesn't seem to align that closely to its core card-processing business" (Finovate, 2008).

Trajectory: member counts rose almost entirely through paid acquisition (ad credits plus Facebook ads), from ~21k (Nov 2008) to a claimed 150k (Aug 2012). Engagement stayed tiny throughout. Even the most popular goal reached only ~6% of claimed members, and the Q&A averaged a handful of questions a month. The member count was a cumulative sign-up figure, not activity. The 2010 "100,000 active members" wording is a company claim that the site's own visible counters don't support.

What keeps people coming back

Very little did, which is the central finding. The mechanics on offer:

  • The ad-credit email (acquisition, not retention). Joining triggered an email with a Facebook ad coupon. Finovate called it "handled flawlessly" (2008). It gave people a reason to join once, not to return.
  • Goals as progress scaffolding. Each goal ("Promote My Business on Facebook", "Set Up an Accounting System", "Manage Your Cash Flow"…) was a 5-step to-do list, with an "average time to complete" (1.0 hours, "315 members polled"), a public roster of "Businesses working on this goal", a matched mentor, and a filtered library (Wayback /goals/10). AKQA's creative director said the goal was to let members "see and connect with other businesses working on the same goal" (Pullum). This was the most promising mechanic (shared goals as a cohort), but nothing shows cohorts were ever convened, given deadlines or celebrated.
  • Directory and associates. Search by location and business type, then "Add Associates". This duplicated Facebook's own friend graph and added "yet another inbox for small business owners to keep track of" (Andrew Kinnear, Jun 2008).
  • Recognition. A "Featured Business" slot "to encourage SBOs to become thought leaders" (Pullum), and visibility for mentors: "Members who become mentors generate additional visibility for their businesses on the site" (MediaPost, Aug 2010).
  • Rituals, events, streaks, badges, staff hosts: no evidence found. No live events, recurring threads, challenges or named community managers appear in any archived page or press coverage.
  • Switching costs: none. Profiles were thin directory entries, and members' real networks lived on Facebook.

The early 50–60% month-two return rates (Ad Age, MediaPost/OMD) are agency- or company-reported and came from the first months, when coupon redemption and first ad campaigns gave people reasons to come back.

The content engine

  • Syndication, not original work. The library aggregated feeds from Entrepreneur (dominant by 2012), WSJ, Forbes, AllBusiness, Fast Company, Microsoft, and small-business blogs such as PowerHomeBiz, Noobpreneur, Convince and Convert, The Suitcase Entrepreneur, The Mogul Mom and YFS Magazine (Wayback Jul 2010, May 2012). Pullum: "Featuring key bloggers created a symbiotic relationship helping to spread the word." Articles showed share counts of 0–6.
  • In-product how-to. Goal toolkits were Visa's one original content format: step-by-step guides on social media, email newsletters, accounting, cash flow and card acceptance. Visa said the toolkits were built "in response to direct feedback from small business owners" (Visa, 2010). This was the closest VBN came to YNAB-style "how to do it in the product" education. The difference is that VBN had no product of its own behind the lessons.
  • Expert Q&A. Launch-era "Ask the Experts" came from Entrepreneur and WSJ. In 2010 it was replaced by mentors chosen for "expertise in the topic/goal area, previous mentor experience, small business experience, previous active online community involvement, ability to help provide initial goal content, enthusiasm" (Newswire, Mirna Bard, Sep 2010). In May 2009 Visa also called it "a collaboration between Visa and SCORE" (InformationWeek, May 2009). No further detail was found.
  • Member-made content: answers, comments and goal check-ins only. There was no way to publish guides or templates, and nothing rewarded doing so.
  • Successor (2012–14): "Well Sourced by Visa Business" on Facebook ran monthly themes, a 25-day tip series from USA Today columnist Rhonda Abrams, and disclosed paid posts by small-business bloggers (Ellen Rohr, The Mogul Mom, Women on Business, YFS Magazine), several of whom had been in the VBN library (Media Logic; Women on Business, 2013). In other words, Visa kept the influencer content engine and dropped the community.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: agency-run. The credits (MediaPost) list three OMD strategists, three AKQA account staff, and two Visa marketers. No community manager role was found.
  • Mentor program (Aug 2010): invitation-only outside experts. Six were listed in 2012: Adam Toren (YoungEntrepreneur.com), Mark Riffey, Natalie Sisson (The Suitcase Entrepreneur), Holly Neitzel, Mirna Bard and Isabel Isidro (PowerHomeBiz). Each was matched to a goal. Perks were visibility and the Visa association; no payment was disclosed (Wayback mentors page). A "Become a Mentor" form existed, but no member-grown mentors appear.
  • Rules and moderation: Community Guidelines and Terms pages existed, along with "Flag as Abusive" on comments. Registration required declaring "you are a business". Sign-up feeds show many non-US and spam-like accounts joining every few minutes during the 2009 credit push (Wayback Sep 2009).
  • Feedback loop: a "Give Feedback" page, and the claim that the 2010 upgrades came from member feedback. Nothing shows a visible roadmap or that Visa ever closed the loop with members.

Growth loops

  • Paid, by design: print ads in small-business publications, banners on forbes.com and wsj.com, a co-branded Forbes landing page (forbes.com/biznet), Google search ads (gclid-tagged VBN landing URLs in 2010 archives), and above all Facebook news-feed ads. Visa tested "more than a dozen versions" and called Facebook ads "the most efficient channel in terms of acquiring new users" (Ad Age).
  • Incentive virality: the $100 credit spread through deal and blackhat forums. That brought volume but the wrong audience: coupon hunters and non-US sign-ups with no stake in networking.
  • Invites: ~3,000 associate invitations in the early OMD period (MediaPost), a modest organic loop.
  • Blogger and mentor halo: featured bloggers and mentors promoted their own VBN roles (Sisson, Bard).
  • SEO: content was largely syndicated and much of it sat behind "Please Login First", so the site built little search equity.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none tying VBN to Visa card volume, issuer demand, acceptance or revenue. Visa's filings and the 2012 fact sheet mention it only as a free resource (fact sheet). EDGAR wasn't searched in depth because no source suggested a filing mention.
  • Company and agency claims: 21,000 profiles in 4 months, beating the year-end goal; "massively living up to our expectations" (Craddock); >50% repeat visits (Ad Age); 18% conversion and 60% month-two return (OMD award entry, MediaPost).
  • Implied economics: $2M in credits for 20,000 businesses works out to $100 per acquired profile before agency, media and build costs. A commenter at launch put the real cost at "$200+ per user" if half never returned (Small Business Trends). That's an estimate, not data.
  • Academic research: none found (OpenAlex budget exhausted during this session; not checked).

Score rationale. Retention mechanics 2: goal checklists and peer rosters existed, but there were no rituals, hosts or switching costs, and the activity counters stayed tiny. Content engine 3: the goal toolkits were genuinely useful, but most content was syndicated partner and blogger feeds. Member ownership 1: members could only answer and comment, and none became hosts or creators. Staff investment 2: heavy agency and media spend, but six outside mentors and no visible community staff. Business impact evidence 1: only acquisition and return-visit claims from Visa and its agencies, nothing on revenue. Durability 1: about 4.5 years, three platform shifts, and a silent shutdown.

What they did right

  1. Built around a real job, not the brand. The goals were practical (create a Facebook page, set up accounting, accept cards). Even in 2012, 8,513 businesses had enrolled in the Facebook-promotion goal, the one piece of the site that kept attracting organic participation (Wayback May 2012).
  2. Structured, in-product how-to with social proof. Step lists, time-to-complete estimates, and a roster of "businesses working on this goal" are a solid template for onboarding education (goal page).
  3. Fast, measured acquisition. It hit its 20k-profile target within ~4 months, with disciplined ad testing (Ad Age).
  4. Outside experts with explicit selection criteria. The mentor rubric (expertise, prior community involvement, willingness to seed content) is a sensible recruiting standard (Newswire).
  5. Iterated on the product once. The 2010 relaunch added Q&A and mentoring, the two features most likely to create member-to-member value, and said it acted on member feedback.

What they did wrong

  1. The incentive became the product. The $100 credit drew coupon hunters. When it stopped (Mar 2009), restarted (Aug 2009) and expired (Nov 2009), the public conversation about VBN turned into complaints: "bait and switch… they killed me as a new paying customer" (MyMoneyBlog, Dec 2009). Adrants put the campaign on its "Bad" list because the page comments were "almost entirely" from people who never got a coupon (snippet).
  2. A network inside a network. It copied Facebook's friend graph, inbox and pages, and VBN wasn't even searchable from Facebook search (Finovate; Kinnear). Consequence: members had no reason to keep relationships in VBN rather than on Facebook itself.
  3. A platform flip-flop. Visa launched on Facebook to avoid building a destination, then moved to its own domain within ~14 months, then back to a Facebook page in 2012. Each move reset habits and, in the end, threw away the member base.
  4. Vanity metrics. "100,000 active members" (2010) and "150,000 members" (2012) sat beside ~140 total questions and goal pages with 20 comments in 19 months (Wayback). The headline numbers hid that peer exchange never took off.
  5. No hosts, no rituals. Agency teams and six part-time outside mentors can't run a community of 100k+. Nothing shows events, challenges, cohort deadlines or staff presence.
  6. Weak business tie. Visa has no direct small-business customer relationship (issuers own the card), so VBN couldn't be bundled into an account, a benefit or a product flow, unlike Amex's cardmember-gated OPEN Forum. It ended as a line in a fact sheet and was replaced by cheaper sponsored content.
  7. Silent shutdown. No closure announcement was found. The domain simply redirected to a new Facebook page, and members' profiles, Q&A and goal progress disappeared.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Goal-based onboarding with visible peers. Turn your core how-to content into short, checkable step lists, and show "N people are working on this right now". VBN's goal pages were its best idea.
  2. Measure month-two return, not sign-ups. VBN's agency tracked return visits early (50–60%). Make that, not cumulative members, the headline metric.
  3. Recruit expert hosts with a clear rubric, and require them to seed content before launch (VBN's mentor criteria). Then grow hosts from your own members.
  4. Test acquisition creative fast (a dozen-plus ad variants), but only after the retention loop exists.
  5. Tie each lesson to an action in your product. VBN's goals pointed at outside actions. Your app can close the loop: lesson → do it in the app → show it to peers.

Avoid these

  1. Paying people to join. A cash-equivalent sign-up bonus attracts bounty hunters, and when it ends it becomes your loudest public story. If you must use one, make it time-boxed, clearly communicated, and tied to a second action, not to installing.
  2. Rebuilding a host platform's core features inside it, or moving platforms every year or two. Each migration resets habits. Pick a home you can own and keep it.
  3. Reporting cumulative members as "active" while staffing nothing. With no community manager, rituals or events, 150k sign-ups produced ~140 questions.

Open questions

  • The total spend on VBN (build, media, credits beyond $2M) and whether any lift in Visa Business card preference was measured.
  • Whether the Aug 2010 "active member" figure had any activity definition behind it.
