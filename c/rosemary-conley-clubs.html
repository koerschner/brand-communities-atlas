Peak

About 80,000 members in roughly 2,000 weekly classes run by 180-190 franchisees, 2004-2008 (Wikipedia citing Bridge 2004; Growth Business, Mar 2006; Daily Mail citing MD Chris Ash, Aug 2013)

Now

The franchise network was terminated in April 2014 (121 franchisees released; 35 briefly kept the name under an investor). Most classes carried on under their instructors' own names. Rosemary Conley still teaches her own Leicestershire classes (BBC, Jan 2022), and the online club lives on as GetSlim (Digital Wellbeing Ltd).

How it pays off for the company

Franchise fees (£12,000 in 1998, up to £26,100 by about 2012, cut to £19,000 in 2013) plus a weekly per-class management fee (£11 in 1998, £26 by 2013, roughly £13,000 a year per franchisee), plus leaflets and kit sold to franchisees. The classes were also a funnel for the magazine, books, DVDs, Solo Slim ready meals and endorsed products, and NHS GP referral contracts brought in paid members.

TL;DR

  • What it was: a UK franchise of weekly 90-minute classes run from 1993 to 2014. Each class had a discreet weigh-in, a five-minute talk, "Slimmer of the Week" certificates and an optional 45-minute exercise-to-music workout. It was one of the UK's "big three" slimming clubs alongside Slimming World and Weight Watchers, and the only one with a workout in every class. It peaked at about 80,000 members in roughly 2,000 weekly classes [1][2][3][13].
  • Why people came back: a fixed weekly ritual with real physical co-presence: you got weighed, you got recognised, and you sweated alongside people your own size, led by a local instructor who had often lost weight herself. Recognition ran up a ladder from class to region to a national ceremony with £1,000 prizes [13][14][16].
  • How the company fed it: a founder-centred media machine. Books sold about 9 million copies combined with DVDs, she had a weekly ITV This Morning slot from 1993 to 2000, a magazine read by up to half a million people, roadshows, and from about 2009 a web TV channel. Instructors had four months of exercise-to-music training plus a nutrition certificate [1][5][9][17].
  • Biggest mistake: the company kept its margin by charging a fixed fee per class and pushed the risk of falling attendance onto franchisees. When attendance fell after 2008, franchisees went into debt, the network shrank by about 25% in 2013 alone, and the head office (drained by a £1m+ web TV channel and a magazine whose sales halved) went into administration in February 2014 [6][7][8][10][12].
  • The one lesson: members were loyal to their instructor and their class, not to the brand. When the brand died, most classes carried on under the instructors' own names with thousands of members intact [11][12]. Whoever owns the weekly ritual and the local leader owns the community.

What the community is

Rosemary Conley Diet & Fitness Clubs was a national network of franchised local classes, held in "village halls, leisure centres, schools and hired hotel rooms" [13]. Rosemary Conley and her husband Mike Rimmington launched it in April 1993 [13], building on her 1988 bestseller The Hip and Thigh Diet and her TV fame [1]. Each franchisee owned a territory and ran about 10 to 15 classes a week [6][19]. Members paid a one-off joining fee (£6.99 "for a lifetime" in 2003, later £10 with a diet pack) and a weekly class fee (£4.35 in 2003, £5.50 in 2009, £5.95 in 2013) [13][14][16]. The format let members choose: "you can just get weighed or you can just work out if you wish" [13].

Around the classes sat other spaces the company ran itself:

  • Slimmer of the Year: class, regional and national awards with an annual London ceremony. They were featured in the magazine and later on rosemaryconley.tv [15][16][18].
  • Roadshows: day-long events across the UK with Rosemary in person, mixing exercise and talks [20].
  • Rosemary Conley Online: a separate paid online club launched around 2003 [21]. It had a "coffee shop" forum open 24/7, a live chat room where "once a month, Rosemary will host a chat", a private "diet and fitness buddies" system and staff coaches [22].
  • Charity "Aerobathons": nationwide fundraising workouts. Franchisees raised more than £2m for charity over the network's life [10][23].

Status today: the franchise is defunct. Franchise contracts were terminated in April 2014 [7][19]. Rosemary's own site now says "We haven't run local classes since 2014" [24], although she personally still teaches weekly classes in Leicestershire that have run since 1972 [25]. The online club passed to Digital Wellbeing Ltd and was renamed getslim.co.uk after she left its board at the end of 2019 [1].

Origin and strategy

Conley had done this once before. In 1972 she ran a "Slimming and Good Grooming" class in a Thurnby village hall: £8 of posters drew 29 women on the first night [26]. She built it into a chain of about 50 classes and sold it to IPC in 1981 for about £50,000. IPC then disbanded it in 1985 [5][17]. After her 1986 gallstone diet became The Hip and Thigh Diet (more than 2 million copies) and a BBC series, she launched the clubs in 1993 "to give additional help, support and motivation to people following her diet and fitness programmes" [1][13].

The strategy was a brand flywheel with clubs as the retention and monetisation layer. Books and TV created demand. Clubs turned readers into weekly paying members. Members in turn bought the magazine (launched 1996), DVDs, Portion Pots, Magic Measure tape measures and Solo Slim ready meals [1][14][27]. A 2005 magazine review noted it was "very biased towards selling or promoting Rosemary Conley clubs, food products, exercise equipment, videos and roadshows" [28]. Franchising let the network grow without capital: in 1998 each of 170 franchisees had paid £12,000, and Conley received £11 for every class held [5]. She insisted the margin was thin ("about 10 per cent") [5].

Champions: Rosemary Conley (brand, content, and her own weekly class for more than 50 years), Mike Rimmington (business partner), Mary Morris (teacher trainer and choreographer from 1994) [1], and later group MD Chris Ash [6].

The company's own later figures ("160 teachers... 2,000 weekly classes... over 70,000 members" [1]) are lower than the contemporary 190/80,000 figures. Treat 70,000 to 80,000 members as the peak range. Trajectory: fast growth from 1993 to 1998, a plateau from 1998 to 2008, then decline once the credit crunch hit. Franchisee numbers fell by about 37% from 2008 to Aug 2013, then collapsed.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A fixed weekly ritual with three parts: accountability, recognition and exertion. Members registered, were "discreetly weighed-in by your class instructor and given your personal calorie allowance", heard a "five-minute motivational and informative talk", then "it's time for rewards when the most successful slimmers will be presented with their Slimmer of the Week certificates", then the workout [14]. The workout set Conley apart from Weight Watchers and Slimming World, where members mostly sit and talk. It also gave members a reason to come even in weeks when the scales were bad.
  2. A recognition ladder. It ran from class certificates to Regional Slimmers of the Year (16 in 2010, honoured at the HQ) to national Slimmers of the Year. In 2011 there were ten categories (Bride, Yummy Mummy, 50+, Student, Biggest Loser, Super-Fit, Online), each with £1,000 and "a cardboard cut-out of their old selves" [15][16]. Winners featured in the magazine and on the TV channel, so recognition doubled as content [18].
  3. Belonging and safety for beginners. The FAQs answered the core fear directly ("I am frightened that if I join your club I will be the fattest there") and promised that instructors were not "waif-like models"; most "have personally conquered their own weight problem" [13]. The joining page promised "a tremendous camaraderie in class as everyone is there for the same reason" [14].
  4. Ties to the instructor and the founder. The real bond was with the local instructor. When the brand failed, one franchisee said members "increased their loyalty to us rather than walking away" [11]. Above the instructors sat Rosemary: TV, roadshows, a monthly chat, and a weekly class she still teaches, where "most of my class have been coming to me for at least 30 years, if not 40 years" [22][25].
  5. Low switching cost into the club, and an implicit cost to leave. A one-off "lifetime" membership fee [13] lowered the cost of rejoining, and January "rejoins" were a planned annual cycle [10]. There was no maintenance-tier perk equivalent to Weight Watchers' Lifetime/Gold free attendance: no evidence found.
  6. Shared-purpose events. Charity Aerobathons (for example the 40th-anniversary event on 8 Oct 2011, run "all over the UK that day") and local charity dinners with awards [23][32].
  7. Online layer (separate product). Online members had buddies, a forum, a chat room and coaches [22]. There is no evidence that class members got a company-run digital space between classes. Some instructors ran their own Facebook pages [33].

The content engine

  • Founder media (the main acquisition engine): 37 books, 30 fitness videos and DVDs, 400+ cookery programmes, series on BBC1 and ITV, and a weekly slot on ITV's This Morning from 1993 to 2000 [1].
  • Magazine (1996 to Jan 2014, 136 issues): success stories, diet plans, recipes and class promotion. The company claims nearly half a million readers per issue; audited circulation was 108,716 in 2005 and 82,137 in 2011 [10][28][30].
  • rosemaryconley.tv (launched about Jan 2010): a "web-based television video channel" with six channels (recipes, exercise, weight loss, health, shop, "Rosemary's World") and presenters such as Dr Hilary Jones and Dr Susan Jebb [18]. It cost more than £1m and "failed to make a return... draining the company's resources for years" [9][34].
  • Website content: recipes of the week, success stories, a monthly "Rosemary writes" column, product news and franchise case studies (site sections visible in Wayback captures, 2009-2013) [35].
  • Class curriculum refreshes: each new diet book became the new member pack (Gi Hip & Thigh 2009, Amazing Inch Loss Plan, FAB Diet 2013) [14][16][27]. New workout formats arrived late: Zumba (about 2011), Kardy-O-Fun (2012), FitSteps (from Sept 2013) [16][35].
  • Member-generated content: limited to success stories and testimonials, which the company curated and amplified (book testimonials, award entries, roadshow quotes) [5][20]. There were no templates or member recipe programmes: no evidence found.

People, programs and governance

  • Franchisee-instructors: they had to complete "an initial four-month programme" to teach exercise to music, plus a certificate in Applied Nutrition and Weight Management [36]. Later this became the CYQ Level 3 Award in Nutrition for Physical Activity [11]. Contracts required at least ten classes a week and a management fee for each class for 50 weeks a year [6].
  • Recognition for staff: Franchisee of the Year awards (2000, 2013 and others) [19][31]. The company won the British Franchise Association's Franchisor of the Year in 2002 and 2005 [36].
  • Head office: about 26 staff in 2014 [8], including an NHS Referral Scheme Manager [37] and online coaches ("Janice and Amanda") [22].
  • Governance: contracts carried non-compete clauses. One ex-franchisee said Conley "threatened to sue" when classes appeared in her old area [6]. Head office's standard advice to struggling franchisees was "deliver more leaflets", bought from head office [6].
  • Feedback loop to product: no formal member feedback channel found. Changes were made top-down, in step with Rosemary's book releases.

Growth loops

  1. Founder media to class: TV, books and magazine sent readers to the class finder [1][13].
  2. Before-and-after stories: Slimmer of the Year winners got regional newspaper coverage every year (for example Tamworth's Laura Mack in 2013) [18].
  3. Word of mouth and members becoming instructors: many instructors started as members ("I started Rosemary Conley classes... in November 1995 and took my mum along") [38]. A 2011 Super-Fit winner went on to run six exercise classes a week [15].
  4. NHS GP referral schemes: PCT-funded 12-week referrals. The company cited a Sutton and Merton pilot with an average 4.3 kg loss over 12 classes (company claim) [37]. Independent trials (below) made the brand commissionable.
  5. Leafleting and vouchers: the default local acquisition tactic, increasingly ineffective by 2013 [6][12].

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence (health outcomes, not commercial):

  • The BBC Diet Trials RCT (BMJ 2006) included "Rosemary Conley's eat yourself slim diet and fitness plan". All four diets produced an average 5.9 kg loss over six months, with no significant differences between them [39].
  • Lighten Up RCT (BMJ 2011, 740 patients): at 12 weeks, "only participants in the Weight Watchers and Rosemary Conley programmes had significantly greater weight loss than the control group". At one year only Weight Watchers, "which had the highest attendance rate", stayed significantly better than control [40][41].
  • A BJGP 2014 cohort of 3,290 referrals found Rosemary Conley non-inferior to Weight Watchers at 3 and 12 months [42]. NICE guidance (May 2014) named Rosemary Conley, Slimming World and Weight Watchers as effective at 12 to 18 months [43].

Commercial (mostly company claims or press estimates): about 10% profit margin claimed in 1998 [5]; group valued at an estimated £13m in 2006 [6][12]; administration in Feb 2014 with about £1m owed and losses of £850,000 [8][12]; the online club was described as "profitable" at the end [10]. No churn, retention or LTV data found. The company never published audited club membership series.

What they did right

  1. Built a ritual with a physical payoff. The workout made the weekly visit worthwhile even without weight loss, and it set them apart from the big two [3][14]. A 2007 member: "RC is the only one that really worked for me... I think the exercise at RC helped" [44]. ReviewCentre users rated the clubs 9.7/10 on average (as reported by lovemoney) [4].
  2. Turned recognition into content and acquisition. Certificates led to regional and then national awards, which then appeared in the magazine, on TV and in local papers [15][16][18].
  3. Credentialed the community leaders. Formal training and nutrition qualifications produced RCT-grade results and NHS referral contracts, which most rivals' communities could not claim [36][40][42].
  4. Removed beginners' fears up front: flexible participation, a lifetime fee, and instructors who had been overweight themselves [13].
  5. The founder showed up in person. Roadshows, monthly online chats, and her own weekly class since 1972. "My longest-standing member has been coming for 46 years" [20][22][25].
  6. Shared-cause events: Aerobathons and charity dinners gave classes a collective purpose beyond the scales; members raised more than £2m [10][23].

What they did wrong

  1. Franchise economics left franchisees carrying the risk. By 2013 a new franchisee paid £26,100 up front plus £26 a class for at least 10 classes a week for 50 weeks, about £40,000 in the first year before hall hire [6]. Projections promised £27,960 to £40,895 profit. The company later admitted these "needed to be revised", cutting the first-year estimate from £30,000+ to about £18,000 [6]. Franchisees described losing "£40 to £50 a class", debts above £15,000, and lost homes and cars [6]. Consequence: the network fell from 190 franchises (2008) to about 120 (2013), losing 40 in 2013 alone. The administrator noted that "a number of them haven't been able to pay" their fees [6][7][10].
  2. Expensive digital bets that weren't community. More than £1m went into rosemaryconley.tv, a broadcast-style channel, while class members got no shared digital space. The paid online club was sold as a separate product [9][22][34]. Conley later admitted she "misjudged... the power of the internet and voucher schemes... the IT era with internet, texts and apps changed things" [12].
  3. Too dependent on one diet and one face. The low-fat message lost ground to Atkins, Dukan and 5:2, and food and magazine sales halved [8][12]. By 2014 franchisees heard members ask "is Rosemary Conley still alive?" [45].
  4. Fell behind on fitness trends. One ex-franchisee had five people at a Conley class but "could get 84 people through the door" for Zumba on her own [6]. Zumba, Kardy-O-Fun and FitSteps arrived in 2011-13, late [35].
  5. Costly changes and poor communication at the end. In Oct 2013 the business rebranded to "Food and Fitness", costing franchisees about £800 each, months before administration. Communication "got weaker and weaker". One pair of franchisees "lost 100 clients" to the uncertainty and was not picked up by the investor [45]. At the first meeting with franchisees after the collapse, Conley faced "personal attacks and accusations" for three and a half hours [10].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make the core ritual pay off in the moment. The workout gave members something worthwhile every week, not just a number on the scales. Give each session a reward of its own, apart from progress on the outcome metric.
  2. Build a recognition ladder that turns into content: a weekly shout-out, then regional, then a national ceremony with real prizes, each step feeding your marketing.
  3. Train and credential your community leaders. Qualified instructors earned trial evidence and NHS contracts. Leader quality is a moat and an acquisition channel.
  4. Name the beginner's fear out loud ("Will I be the fattest there?") and make participation flexible: lurk, weigh in only, or join the workout.
  5. Founder and staff presence at a steady, low cadence: monthly chats, roadshows, a class the founder actually teaches. Show up regularly rather than through one-off launches.

Avoid these

  1. Don't make your local leaders absorb the risk of falling attendance. Fixed per-session fees and mandated leaflet buying turned the community's most important people into indebted critics, and eventually into competitors.
  2. Don't mistake broadcast media for community. A £1m video channel did nothing for members between classes. Put digital money into member-to-member ties, such as buddies and class groups, for every member, not as a separate upsell.
  3. Don't tie the whole community to one method and one face. When low-fat and the founder's TV fame faded, nothing else held the brand together. The groups survived only because they were loyal to their instructors.

Score justifications

  • Retention mechanics 4: a well-designed weekly ritual (weigh-in, talk, certificates, workout) and a recognition ladder, but no digital layer between classes for class members and no maintenance tier.
  • Content engine 4: a huge founder-led output (books, videos, TV, magazine, roadshows, web TV), but almost no member-made content beyond curated success stories.
  • Member ownership 2: members joined in but never shaped the product. Franchisees owned the classes yet carried the risk without a voice.
  • Staff investment 4: formal four-month instructor training plus nutrition certification, franchise awards and NHS partnerships. HQ staff were small (about 26).
  • Business impact evidence 3: strong independent RCT evidence of health outcomes. Commercial results are thin and mostly company claims.
  • Durability 2: 21 years, then administration. The groups outlived the brand only by leaving it.
