TL;DR

  • What it was. Wesabe (wesabe.com, launched November 2006) was the first of the Web 2.0 personal finance sites. It paired account aggregation with a community: member tips attached to transaction tags and merchants, public goals that other members could join, and from April 2007 an open forum called Wesabe Groups. Members called themselves "Wesabeans."
  • Why people came back. Peer support in debt and savings groups such as "Paying It Off" and "Saving Up," time-boxed challenges like No Spend Month, "pat yourself on the back" victory threads, and a founder who answered posts personally in the Make Wesabe Better group.
  • What the company did to feed it. The founders posted in the forum (CEO Jason Knight even published a phone number with daily office hours). They hired a member advocate (Feb 2008) and a community manager (Jun 2008), shipped features members asked for and credited them by name, linked to the member-built Wesabepedia wiki, and pitched member success stories to USA Today, the Today Show and CNBC.
  • The biggest mistake. They bet that community and "working with your data" would beat convenience. Setup was laborious (manual uploads, no Yodlee) while Mint, launched 10 months later, did the work for users. Growth ran at roughly one-fifth of Mint's, and the money tools shut down on July 31, 2010. By the founder's own measure, the community did not move members' debt or savings beyond national averages.
  • The one lesson. For a utility, community sits on top of time-to-value and can't replace it. Members only reached Wesabe's genuinely good forum after the product had already made them work hard, and most never got there.

What the community is

Wesabe was a San Francisco personal finance site founded in late 2005 by Marc Hedlund (product, later CEO) and Jason Knight (CEO until May 2008). It went live on November 17, 2006, and was backed by O'Reilly AlphaTech Ventures ($700K, Feb 2007) and Union Square Ventures ($4M, Jun 2007) [1][2][3]. Its tagline was "Your Money. Your Community." The community had four layers:

  1. Tips. Members wrote money-saving tips that were attached to tags and merchants and showed up next to matching transactions. For example, a tip about Amazon shipping appeared on your Amazon purchases [4][5]. In April 2008 Tips was rebuilt as a "Value Engine" that compared merchants using pooled member data: spend per visit, repeat-visit rate, and "fan/user/captive" ratings [6].
  2. Goals. Public goals ("Pay Off Credit Card Debt") that anyone could join, with a badge and a list of members working on the same goal. The top goal had 3,838 members in 2008 [7].
  3. Groups. Discussions started inside Goals, then moved to a dedicated Groups tab on April 6, 2007. Any member could create a group and became its moderator. Groups could be read publicly without signing up and had full-text RSS feeds [8].
  4. Make Wesabe Better. The feedback and support group, and the busiest one on the site [7][9].

Unofficial spaces were small. Members built Wesabepedia (a PBwiki) to document the product, and Wesabe adopted it into its help section [10]. Reddit was tiny in that era, and no Wesabe subreddit or fan forum turned up.

Status: defunct. The money tools (accounts, iPhone app, uploaders) shut down on July 31, 2010. Groups stayed up because "one of our customers has agreed to fund its continued service" [11]. Wayback snapshots show Groups live through December 2012, filling with spam (an airport-limo ad thread, spam groups such as "telefon" and "designbyarm"), and returning HTTP 404 from March 2013 [12][13].

Origin and strategy

Hedlund described Wesabe as a Flickr/del.icio.us-style product. It "relied on community and features such as tags" and tried "to learn from the accumulated data our users uploaded, and make recommendations for better financial decisions" [1]. Before launch, his blog argued that self-awareness tools like Quicken are "necessary... but not sufficient" for behavior change. He leaned on Kathy Sierra's "give users a way to kick ass" spiral of rising skill and goals [14]. Kathy Sierra, Clay Shirky and Cory Doctorow all joined the advisory board in November 2006 [15].

The community tied into the business model in four ways:

  • A data network effect. "Wesabe is the only site of its kind where the community gets smarter... with each new member who joins, jumps into Groups, and starts tagging and rating the merchants where they shop" (Hedlund, Aug 2008) [9].
  • Trust instead of ads. Wesabe refused advertising and referral fees on principle. Hedlund argued that a site meant to save people money couldn't push credit cards. One employee told him "If we had to be Mint in order to win, I'm glad we lost" [16]. A planned $4.99/month Pro tier was dropped in February 2007 so that data collection wouldn't be limited [17].
  • Support and feedback. Make Wesabe Better worked as public support and as a roadmap.
  • B2B packaging. From March 2009 the Springboard white-label product sold Wesabe tools to banks and credit unions. The community was pitched as an asset: "FIs can leverage the vibrant Wesabe community to instantly provide interesting content" [18]. Addison Avenue Federal Credit Union (more than 150,000 members) launched "Addison Avenue Groups," a customized version that shared discussions with Wesabe.com members [19][20].

The internal champion was Hedlund himself: he moderated Groups personally until the community manager arrived in June 2008 [21].

Trajectory. The community grew fast from April 2007 through 2008, helped by the financial crisis and press coverage. It plateaued through 2009 and declined after 2010. Its whole lifetime produced roughly 5,000–6,000 threads, which is small next to forums like FatWallet. One 2008 reviewer called the Entrepreneurship group "a very slow forum" [7].

What keeps people coming back

  • Peer support around a shared struggle. The strongest groups were about pain points: "Paying It Off," "Saving Up," "Student Loans," "Poor Credit," "Financial Repair After Divorce," "Another Great Depression" [12][13]. Knight's pitch was anonymity: "people can have conversations about money that they do not feel comfortable having with their friends and family" [30]. Members could take part anonymously [8].
  • Rituals and challenges. No Spend Month (November 2008) ran as a group thread. Participants reported "plumper bank accounts," and the company turned their advice into a blog post [31]. A standing "Pat yourself on the back" thread collected wins [32].
  • Recognition. Wesabe pitched members to the media. CymbidiumKelly's No Spend Month appeared in USA Today and on the Today Show, and a Groups thread about credit-limit cuts became a Washington Post story [33]. The December 2008 Wesabe/CNBC "On The Money" Success Club put members on air, with DVDs and Wesabe T-shirts as thank-yous [32]. The T-shirts themselves came from a member-requested thread [34].
  • Identity. "Wesabeans," plus niche and identity groups: Canadian Wesabe, Kiwi-sabe, UK Wesabe Users, Homeschooling Wesabeans, Military Money, All WAHMs, Christians, Linux Love [12][13]. Every page had an "I'm Freaking Out" link to a kitten slideshow, a calming in-joke that members were still mentioning in 2012 [5][12].
  • Ties to staff. Hedlund was "very active in the Group, responding with very thorough posts" [7]. Knight took calls from a homepage "Talk to Jason" link during posted office hours, noon to 4 pm Pacific, seven days a week [35][36].
  • In-product hooks. Tips appeared next to your own transactions. Goals showed a badge and fellow members. Following a thread or joining a group subscribed you to email updates automatically, which one reviewer criticized for being opt-out [7].
  • Switching costs. These were weak. Wesabe's Data Bill of Rights promised that members could export or delete their data at any time [37], and the community lived on a separate, public tab. When the tools shut down, members simply left.

Score: retention mechanics 3/5. The rituals, recognition and peer support were real. But there was no progress system beyond a goal badge, and the in-product social hooks (Tips and Goals) were cut in 2009.

The content engine

  • Company content. The blog ran from 2006 to 2010, mixing feature launches, privacy explainers ("Super Ninja Privacy Techniques"), member roundups ("Some great Groups discussions") and financial-advice posts [23][38]. There were also screencasts (tagging basics, tag splits; Jan–Feb 2007) and a full user manual in 2009 [10][38]. Hedlund appeared regularly on CNBC's On the Money and talked about the community on air [33].
  • Borrowed authority. In January 2009 the top personal finance bloggers of the day, J.D. Roth (Get Rich Slowly), Ramit Sethi (I Will Teach You To Be Rich) and Trent Hamm (The Simple Dollar), joined the advisory board to write guest posts and "participate in community discussions" [39].
  • Member-made content. This was the core: tips, discussions, and Wesabepedia. Wesabe said Wesabepedia "wasn't even our idea," and that it covered uses "far better than what we had planned" [10]. The company curated member posts into blog roundups and press pitches.
  • Gaps. Wesabe had no regular education program: no webinars, courses or newsletter cadence comparable to YNAB's. Mint, by contrast, built a widely read tips blog (MintLife), which HN commenters credited for its reach [40].

Score: content engine 2/5. The company blog was steady and the blogger board was smart, but most of the content came from members and the company did little to scale it.

People, programs and governance

  • Team. 14–18 staff in total [24][25]. Community work was done by Hedlund, member advocate Nate Hatcher (hired Feb 2008 because support was overwhelmed by "a huge influx of new members") [41], and community manager Allese Thomson. She joined as a summer intern in 2007, started posting in Groups on her own initiative, and was hired full time in June 2008 so that "we can answer every question in Make Wesabe Better" [21]. Paul (documentation) and the engineers also answered in Groups [41]. Director of communications Debbie Pfeifer ran the press and success-story programs [26][32].
  • Moderation. Whoever created a group became its moderator and could appoint others [8]. No formal superuser or ambassador program turned up.
  • Feedback loop. Engineers discussed a member complaint every day [42]. Requests were shipped and credited publicly. User kmccormi asked in Make Wesabe Better for a Mac Dashboard widget, and an engineer built one [43]. A member survey led to new graphs in July 2008 [44]. Wesabe Labs (January 2009) let members opt into beta features [39].
  • After shutdown. Volunteer-run groups ("Open Wesabe Installation," "Open Source Wesabe") supported the Apache-licensed code release [12][45]. Once paid staff were gone, nobody moderated, and spam took over by 2012.

Scores: member ownership 4/5 (user-created and user-moderated groups, the member wiki, open-sourced code, data portability). Staff investment 3/5 (unusually personal founder involvement, but one community manager at most).

Growth loops

  • Public, indexable forum. Making Groups public in April 2007 was an explicit acquisition move: "let people sign up once they've gotten interested" [8]. Old threads were still drawing searchers in 2012 [12].
  • Asking members to invite others. Hedlund's "Tell a friend in debt" post (August 2008) asked members to recruit, arguing that each new member made the community smarter [9].
  • Press built on member stories. Wesabe spent "almost nothing on marketing," and growth followed "press wins, economic conditions, and sometimes drafting on Mint's coverage" [1].
  • Distribution partners. The Telegraph's co-branded Personal Finance Planner (UK, November 2008) and credit union white-labels [46][19].
  • What didn't loop. Hedlund: "Wesabe wasn't viral and Mint was — half-true: neither product was" [1]. Mint even bought Google ads on the keyword "Wesabe" when it shut down [29].

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence is thin, and what exists points against the community driving outcomes.

  • No retention or churn data was ever published. User counts appear only as Finovate ranges [24][25][26].
  • No measurable behavior change (founder's own analysis). "Both products helped some people — ours mostly through a supportive community... But... the debt reduction and savings increase numbers directly matched the national averages" [1].
  • Some B2B value. Addison Avenue FCU said "after discovering the richness of personal finance wisdom in Wesabe's online community, we knew it was essential to provide this experience to our membership" [19]. After the shutdown, a customer paid to keep Groups running [11][27]. Bank sales still lagged: "It hasn't helped as much as I had hoped," Hedlund said in May 2010, and analysts blamed the lack of a sales force [16][47].
  • Revenue. Wesabe earned revenue from late 2008, ran out of invested money around November 2009, and lived on revenue alone until July 2010 [1].
  • Company claims. "Largest, most active community in the space" [26]; Hedlund said the community "is so strong" that it drew bank interest during the 2008 crisis [18].

Score: business impact evidence 1/5. No retention numbers, and the founder's own data says outcomes didn't beat the baseline. Durability 1/5. The tools lasted under four years and the forum about six and a half.

What they did right

  1. Community built around the hardest emotional problem. Debt and money stress drove the most active groups. Coverage praised the tone: "supportive, constructive, and unique" (Hedlund) [11]; "discussion groups are packed with cash-saving ideas" (Web User magazine) [39].
  2. A founder who showed up. CEO posts in the feedback group, a public CEO phone line, and credited feature requests were remarked on in reviews [7][35]. Signal v. Noise cited Wesabe's CEO office hours as the inspiration for its own [36].
  3. Members turned into heroes. Press placements (Today Show, USA Today, Washington Post, CNBC) gave members status and gave Wesabe free coverage [32][33].
  4. Member ownership embraced. Open group creation, creator-moderators, and Wesabepedia adopted into the official help pages [8][10].
  5. A graceful, honest shutdown. A month's notice, full export, open-sourced code that imported member data, links to alternatives, the forum kept online, and a candid postmortem that 350,000 people read [11][16][45]. Fred Wilson called it the right way to fail [48].

What they did wrong

  1. Community put ahead of time-to-value. Wesabe required downloads and manual uploads and didn't accept bank passwords until 2008. Hedlund: "Mint focused on making the user do almost no work at all... we completely sucked at all of that" [1]. Users bounced before they ever reached the community. A 2008 reviewer spent minutes on a spinning bank login and hours tagging two months of history [7]. Consequence: growth at about a fifth of Mint's rate, then shutdown.
  2. Community features that overlapped and confused. Discussions began inside Goals, which confused members and had to be moved out [8]. Goals with the same name split into separate communities (a 19-member "Pay Off Credit Card Debt" next to the 3,838-member one), and the goal-member page took five minutes to load [7]. Tips were judged no better than "any money saving forum or... a quick Google search" [7]. Consequence: in the April 2009 redesign Wesabe removed Goals and Tips, "two whole tabs out of four," and "haven't gotten a single complaint" [49]. The in-product social layer had not been driving retention.
  3. A business model that couldn't pay for the community. No ads, no referral fees, a cancelled Pro tier, and slow self-serve bank sales [16][17][47]. Members on the shutdown thread offered to pay, too late [28][47].
  4. Not scaling support. Hedlund's shutdown letter said members "hit a problem, often after investing many hours, and aren't able to get help" [11]. The forum carried support load that a 15-person company couldn't keep up with.
  5. An orphaned community. Once paid staff left, Groups had no moderation and filled with spam, then disappeared in early 2013. No public archive of the discussions turned up [12].
  6. Trust branding. Critics blamed the name and the rough look. Hedlund disputed that this was decisive, but conceded that rivals reduced security "to a couple of icons" while Wesabe made it "the elephant in the room" [16][50].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Organize groups around members' struggles, not product features. The debt and saving groups carried the community. Let members create niche and identity groups and moderate them.
  2. Run time-boxed challenges and keep a standing wins thread. No Spend Month and "Pat yourself on the back" generated content, accountability and press with almost no tooling.
  3. Make members famous. Pitch their stories to media, feature them in your blog, and send them swag. It costs little and rewards the behavior you want.
  4. Put the founder and engineers in the feedback forum, and credit requests publicly when they ship. Reviewers noticed and praised it.
  5. Adopt member-made documentation and make the forum public. Link to member-made docs officially, and let people read the forum without signing up so it brings in search traffic.

Avoid these

  1. Don't use community to make up for onboarding friction in a utility. If first value takes hours, most people never reach the forum. Fix time-to-value first.
  2. Don't scatter social features across overlapping surfaces (Goals vs Groups vs Tips) without measuring which ones retain users. Wesabe cut two of its four tabs and nobody missed them.
  3. Don't build a community you can't fund or steward through decline. Tie it to a revenue line, and plan for archiving and moderation if the product dies.
