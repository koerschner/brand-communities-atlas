MyFamily.com

MyFamily.com (private family websites) · Ancestry (MyFamily.com Inc. 1999–2006, The Generations Network 2006–2009, Ancestry.com Inc. from 2009)

A pre-Facebook family social network that kept people through love and an accumulating archive, then lost them through a failed rewrite, neglect and a shutdown that discarded their words

Peak

'More than 3 million users' when Ancestry's parent opened a Bellevue office to rebuild it (Mar 2006, as quoted by The Ancestry Insider, Apr 2008). Earlier traction: 1 million registered users in its first 140 days (1999), and 'the highest number of unique online visitors' among password-protected family sites (Media Metrix, Sep 1999, per Deseret News).

Now

Shut down 30 Sep 2014 (originally 5 Sep), with prorated refunds. The export returned photos and videos but not the text discussions (Ancestry FAQ; Slate, Apr 2015). Ancestry would not tell Slate how many users it had at closure.

How it pays off for the company

First the master brand and growth engine of a VC-funded dot-com (free sites paid for by ads, sponsorships and a gift store; more than $90M raised). After the crash it became a small paid add-on: $19.95–$99.95 a year in 2002 and $29.95 a year per group by 2013, bundled free with Ancestry subscriptions, with a cross-sell into Ancestry family trees. By Feb 2002 it had 35,000 paid subscriptions against Ancestry's 500,000+.

TL;DR

  • What it was. Ancestry's parent launched MyFamily.com in December 1998: free, password-protected websites where an extended family (or a club, class or team) shared news threads, photos, a calendar, chat, recipes and a family tree. It grew fast enough (1M registered users in 140 days) that in November 1999 the whole company renamed itself MyFamily.com Inc. The site was retired on 30 September 2014 (Deseret News, Nov 1999; Ancestry, 2014).
  • Why people came back. The other members were their own relatives, and the site pushed news to them. Email notifications, a "What's New" page, birthday and anniversary reminders, and over the years an irreplaceable archive of family conversation all brought them back. One member called it "our archive for family stories, for family history, for family photos. It. Was. Wonderful." (Looking Glass Letters, Sep 2014).
  • What the company did to feed it. It supplied the tools, and not much else. It ran a product blog, surveys and advisory groups during the 2.0 rebuild, and sponsored the member-run "Admins Family Site", where volunteer site admins taught each other tricks. It produced very little content of its own: the families produced it all.
  • Biggest mistake. A 2006–2010 "2.0" rewrite tried to force-migrate loyal 1.0 families. Ancestry reversed the migration in April 2008, froze both versions in 2010 and closed the Bellevue team. Then the 2014 shutdown's export returned only photos and dropped every news thread, "like taking two boxes of old letters in your grandmother's upstairs bedroom and tossing them in the trash" (Slate, Apr 2015).
  • The one lesson. When the community is the users' own relationships and memories, the switching cost is huge, and so is your duty of care. You have to keep investing in it, evolve it in small steps, and give members a full, readable export, or the archive that kept them becomes the reason they turn on your core brand.

What the community is

MyFamily.com was not one community. It was hundreds of thousands to millions of tiny private ones, each with a "site admin" who invited relatives in ("family is a relative thing"). The 1999 homepage listed "threaded discussions, calendar, chat, instant messaging, multimedia album, a shared file cabinet, e-mail, member wish lists and a gift store" (Wayback, May 1999). Later versions added a family tree linked to Ancestry, polls, recipes and history threads. Version 2.0 (2006–2010) added voice comments left by phone, narrated photo slideshows ("SnapGenie"), video and blogs (Ancestry press release, Oct 2007; CNET, 2007). Many groups used it for "sports teams, schools, hobbies", not just families.

The official spaces around the product were the 2.0 blog (blog.myfamily.com), a help and migration center, a beta Flickr group, and the Admins Family Site (adminfamily.com). That last one was itself a MyFamily site, "sponsor[ed]… each year" by the company, where admins "shar[ed] tips and tricks… out of the goodness of their hearts" (MyFamily blog, Mar 2007). There was no meaningful public fan space. After the shutdown, the unofficial successor was Spokt, a small family-hub startup whose paid "Mayflower" scraper rescued "tens of thousands" of MyFamily users (Spokt).

Status: defunct since 30 September 2014.

Origin and strategy

The founders' idea. Paul Allen and Dan Taggart, BYU dropouts behind the CD-ROM publisher Infobases, bought Ancestry, a small genealogy publisher, and turned it into a dot-com (Deseret News, Jun 2000). Allen "conceived of and helped launch the MyFamily.com service to provide private web sites for families" in 1998 (BYU-Idaho bio). The pitch on the 1999 homepage is the product thesis: "much of the communication we would like to share with family members is too personal for public viewing. If only we could have a private Web site like at the office."

Strategy, 1999–2000: the community as the company. In November 1999 Ancestry.com Inc. renamed itself MyFamily.com Inc. to pursue "the largest and most active online community in the world for families". MyFamily became "the company's master brand" and Ancestry a sub-brand (Deseret News, Nov 1999). Free sites were paid for by "sponsorship and ad revenues… a gift center". A trend-watcher noted in 1999 that "connections to e-commerce site… is their economic model, but the links are mild" (Masie Learning Trends, Mar 1999). Investors included CMGI @Ventures, Kodak, AOL, Compaq, Intel Capital and Groupe Arnault (CNET, Jun 2000), for a total of $90.5M (CNET, Jul 2001). The audience was also the reason for buying RootsWeb in June 2000 (see the Ancestry Message Boards report).

Strategy after the crash: a paid add-on. By February 2002 "online data subscriptions" (Ancestry records) were "over 80% of total revenue". MyFamily had launched paid premium sites about five months earlier and had 35,000 paid subscriptions, while Ancestry had more than 500,000 (PR Newswire via Ancestry, Feb 2002). The free tier shrank to 5MB with banner ads, and premium sites were bundled into Ancestry subscriptions (Family Tree Magazine, undated c. 2001). The 2004 homepage pitched it as "Perfect for the Family Historian!" (Wayback, Oct 2004). In 2006 the company renamed itself The Generations Network, and in 2009 Ancestry.com Inc. The 2009 S-1 mentions MyFamily only as a trademark and as "private family websites" inside subscription revenue (S-1, Aug 2009).

Champions. The champions were Paul Allen at launch, then Michael Graff, a 14-year Microsoft veteran hired in September 2005 to run the MyFamily business unit. He opened a Bellevue, WA office in March 2006 and left in March 2008 (Ancestry Insider, Apr 2008). CEO Tim Sullivan talked up "terrific synergies" with Ancestry in 2007. No champion was found after 2010.

Trajectory. Explosive free growth from 1999 to 2000. A paywall from 2001 to 2003 cut it down to a paying core. Claimed users peaked at about 3M in 2006, then fell to 1.8M by 2008 during the rewrite. It drifted from 2010 to 2014 as families moved to Facebook. (Slate's "1.5 million users as of 2004" refers to a company-wide paid-subscription milestone, not MyFamily alone.)

What keeps people coming back

  1. The members are your family. This was the core driver, and no mechanic can copy it. A blogger wrote: "Kari started our MyFamily.com site in 2004, and suddenly we didn't feel so far away… We laughed a lot and teased even more… we shared joy and sorrow" (Looking Glass Letters). Her site covered one matriarch's descendants: 25 grandchildren and "more than 50" great-grandchildren.
  2. Privacy as identity. "Only your family members (or those you invite…) can access the site." Older members "resisted [Facebook] because we loved the privacy of MyFamily.com" (same source).
  3. Push, not pull. Members chose which "automatically generated notifications about new content, anniversaries, and other site changes" to receive (Familypedia, via search snippet). Version 2.0 sent "reminders… for birthdays, anniversaries, reunions" and had an ad-free "What's New" page (Oct 2007 release; CNET).
  4. Low-barrier participation for elders. Voice comments and narrated slideshows recorded through a toll-free number were "helpful for family members still tied to dial-up" (CNET).
  5. Rituals built in by the family itself. These included travel diaries, a grandmother's houseplant saga ("Tom the Tomato"), recipe books compiled from threads, and reunion planning (Slate; Looking Glass Letters). The company supplied the containers (calendar, recipes, history threads) but no programmed cadences. No streaks, badges, leaderboards or company-run events were found.
  6. The archive as switching cost. Ancestry sold it as an archive: "Unlimited storage space and SiteSafe technology keep all of your family memories safe and secure. No matter what." (Wayback, 2013). Families stayed through clunky interfaces because years of content lived there. The same lock-in later made the shutdown feel like a betrayal.
  7. The single-payer admin. "Only one person pays for the entire group." That kept friction low for relatives, but each site depended on one keystone admin.

Retention mechanics score 3/5: strong relationship pull, push notifications and an accumulating archive, but no designed rituals, progress or recognition systems.

The content engine

The company produced almost none. All the content was family UGC. Company output found:

  • Product blog (blog.myfamily.com, 2006–2008), with posts signed by named staff (Dave Quick, Shane Clark) explaining the migration and inviting comments. It went silent in early 2008: "Obviously it's been a while since we've had a fresh post" (Ancestry Insider).
  • Member surveys, advisory groups and a beta feedback form during 2.0 (Oct 2007 release).
  • A help and migration center with feature-parity matrices.
  • Commerce hooks in place of editorial content: a gift store (1999), and later prints, mugs and photo books through Qoop.

The most interesting member-made content was the admins' know-how. On the Admins Family Site they found "custom formatting (css styles…) to produce custom banners, custom backgrounds, 'hidey holes'", and the 2.0 team "marveled" that members had discovered these techniques (MyFamily blog, Mar 2007). That same customization made migration harder: power users had the most to lose. Content engine score 1/5: no education, events or editorial programme; the product was a container.

People, programs and governance

  • Governance was delegated to families. Each site admin controlled membership, and moderation was internal to the family. No public rules or moderation team were found.
  • Superusers. The Admins Family Site was a company-sponsored but member-run peer network of admins. No formal perks, titles or criteria were found.
  • Staff. The staff peak was the Bellevue business unit (2006–2010), led by an SVP/GM. In May 2010 the office closed and "none of the staff accepted an offer to move to Provo" (Wikipedia; Ancestry Insider, Jun 2014). By 2014 the only visible staff were an Ancestry community manager (Cara Longpre) on Ancestry's custhelp forums and a Member Services phone line open 11–6 ET on weekdays (FAQ).
  • Feedback loop. Member feedback did change one major decision, the April 2008 reversal of forced migration: "we have learned some very important things from you about the value of 1.0" (MyFamily blog).
  • Staff investment 2/5: heavy spending from 2006 to 2010, little before and nothing after.
  • Member ownership 3/5: families fully controlled their spaces and admins self-organized, but they did not own their data: the company could, and did, delete the text.

Growth loops

  • Invitation virality. Every new site meant an admin emailing invitations to a whole extended family, and members could spin off sub-sites ("a 'cousins-only' site… 'Mrs. Johnson's 5th Grade Class'") (Oct 2007 release). This drove 1M registrants in 140 days. A local rumour that the company paid people "a couple bucks" to create sites is unverified (Ancestry Insider, Jun 2014).
  • Network cross-sell. Free registration fed a five-email personalized nurture sequence ("We've found a lot more information about the Pearson family…"), and on-site banners converted 14–20% of new visitors into free registrants (company-wide figures, InfoWorld, May 2002). MyFamily sites linked their trees to Ancestry World Tree.
  • No SEO loop. Because the sites were private, the content never ranked in search, unlike RootsWeb's public boards.
  • Sharing out. Version 2.0 added permalinks and email shares to non-members, and the 2013 version had Facebook, Twitter and Tumblr integration and an iPhone app. These arrived years after Facebook had taken the use case.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard: 35,000 paid MyFamily subscriptions in February 2002, against 500,000+ for Ancestry. Paid site tiers cost $19.95–$99.95 a year in 2002 and $29.95 in 2013 (Feb 2002 release; Wayback 2013). At an assumed 50,000–100,000 paying groups (an estimate; no figure was published), revenue would have been at most low single-digit millions a year, a rounding error against Ancestry's $300.9M in 2010 (en-academic).
  • Company claims: the 1M users in 140 days; Media Metrix and NetRatings rankings; "nearly two million passionate and dedicated members" (2007).
  • Strategic value: MyFamily's buzz helped raise $90M+ in VC that bankrolled Ancestry's records business and the RootsWeb acquisition. That is arguably its biggest business contribution, though it is inferred, not documented.
  • Negative impact: the shutdown produced cancellations and threats of cancellation of core Ancestry subscriptions. One commenter: "you have lost my respect and my subscription" (Ancestry blog comments, Jun 2014). It also drew national press (Slate) and a Hacker News thread with 190 points (HN, Apr 2015). No churn figures were found. Score 2/5.

What they did right

  1. They saw private family social networking six years before Facebook and nailed the job to be done (private, multi-generational, feature-complete in 1999). Traction was immediate: 1M users in 140 days, and #1 in its category by Media Metrix.
  2. They gave each family ownership of its own space. Admins, invitations and sub-sites made the community feel like theirs, and families used it for 10–15 years (Spokt testimonial; blog comment by Stephen Schneider, Jun 2014).
  3. They designed for elders. Phone-in voice comments and narrated slideshows met grandparents where they were (CNET).
  4. They sponsored a superuser peer network (Admins Family Site) at almost no cost and drew on its knowledge during the rebuild.
  5. They listened and reversed course on forced migration in April 2008 with a public mea culpa ("Mistakes are a fact of life. It is the response to error that counts").
  6. The single-payer group pricing ($29.95 a year, one person pays) kept relatives free and fit how families actually share costs.

What they did wrong

  1. Neglected the product for years, then attempted a big-bang rewrite. Version 1.0 ran on "really old technology that none of the techies in the company want to touch". The 2.0 rewrite took more than two years and forced migration before reaching feature parity; Ancestry Insider argued for "baby steps" (Ancestry Insider, Apr 2008). Consequence: two codebases to support, membership falling from about 3M (2006) to 1.8M (2008), and the GM's exit.
  2. Pricing whiplash. It was free (1999), then paywalled (2001–2003), free again with ads (2007), and paywalled again (free v3 sites ended July 2010). Each switch stranded someone. A 2001 complaint cites "mixed message… about space requirements and billing" (Ripoff Report, May 2001, via search snippet).
  3. Froze it and kept charging. From 2010 to 2014 there were no fixes and no features, while Facebook won mobile photo sharing: "it had an app for phones… gradually MyFamily.com withered for our family" (Looking Glass Letters). A Spokt commenter in June 2014 called it "slowly wilting on the vine".
  4. Destroyed the archive it had marketed. The export returned photos with "long, random names and without the posts", sometimes split into "99 z files" (blog comments by Kathryn and Kathy Valentine, Jun 2014). Ancestry's community manager had promised discussions would export as .txt. Ancestry later said "it wasn't feasible to return the discussion data" (Slate). A two-person startup did export the text (Spokt Mayflower, $69 per site), which shows it could be done.
  5. Communicated poorly during the wind-down. The site suffered outages during the export window ("MyFamily.com is down again!"), the date extension to 30 September was posted on a blog but members said no email arrived (comments 133–134, Jul 2014), and the retirement was bundled with four other cuts under a "focus on core offerings" message.

Durability 1/5: it was defunct after 16 years, its peak passed by about 2006, and it left no surviving official space.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Build around members' existing relationships (family, team, class) in private, invite-only groups. The relationships give you retention for free, and every new group is an invitation loop.
  2. Push activity to members with digests, "what's new" views and date-based reminders (birthdays, anniversaries) so they never have to remember to visit.
  3. Make contributing trivially easy for the least technical member. MyFamily's phone-in voice notes are the 2007 version of voice memos in chat.
  4. Find and quietly sponsor your power admins in a peer space of their own, and bring them in before you redesign.
  5. Price per group with one payer, so everyone else joins free.

Avoid these

  1. Big-bang rewrites with forced migration. Evolve the product incrementally and never take features away from loyal groups before parity.
  2. Selling "your memories, safe forever" without an exit plan. If you sunset, ship a complete, human-readable export (text, captions, authors, dates) with months of notice and direct email to every admin. A failed export punishes your core brand, not just the side product.
  3. Freezing a paid social product while the market moves on. Either fund it (mobile, sharing) or sunset it early and gracefully. Four years of zombie maintenance converted loyalty into resentment.
