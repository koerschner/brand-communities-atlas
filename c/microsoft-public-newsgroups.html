TL;DR

  • What it was: From April 1996 to autumn 2010 Microsoft ran its own NNTP news server, msnews.microsoft.com. It carried the microsoft.public.* hierarchy, 1,900 to 3,000 public peer-support groups (one per product, version and task), plus up to 2,200 private groups for MSDN subscribers, beta testers, partners and MVPs (MSDN Magazine 2004; Microsoft 2010).
  • Why people came back: Experts answered for free, every day, and the answers were good. For the expert core, the pull was status among peers, which turned into the annual MVP award, consulting work and access to product teams. One Messenger MVP posted about 50,000 times in nine years and missed no day for most of that time (PC Review, Jun 2010).
  • What Microsoft fed it: a free server that the newsreader built into Windows (Outlook Express) could reach, MVP awards and summits, support engineers who answered under "[MSFT]" signatures, a paid "managed" tier with a two-business-day reply promise, Community Program Managers per group cluster, and Microsoft Research's Netscan analytics for spotting top contributors (Turner & Fisher, HICSS 2006).
  • Biggest mistakes: Twice Microsoft treated the volunteers as a cost line. In October 1999 it cancelled the MVP program by email and reversed itself four days later after an uproar (Wired). From 2005 it starved the groups in favor of web forums, then closed them in 2010 because usage had halved. Posts expired after 90 days on Microsoft's own server, so Microsoft never owned the knowledge base the groups produced. Google Groups did.
  • The one lesson: Recognition for the people who answer questions (the MVP model) outlived the platform it grew up on. Build the recognition program and an archive you own from day one, and when you migrate, take the expert core with you before you switch off the old space.

What the community is

A Microsoft-run hierarchy of Usenet newsgroups on msnews.microsoft.com. No account was needed for the public groups. In Outlook Express you added a news account, typed the server name, downloaded the group list and subscribed (MSDN Magazine, May 2004). The public groups also flowed out to ISPs' news servers and to Google Groups. The private groups (MSDN, betas, partner programs, MVP lounges) needed Microsoft credentials. Microsoft named the hierarchy like a product catalog: microsoft.public.excel.programming, microsoft.public.windows.server.general, microsoft.public.money, plus language branches (microsoft.public.de.money, microsoft.public.fr.*). Around 2003 a browser front end, "Microsoft Discussion Groups" (the Web-based Newsreader), added search, Passport sign-in, "Did this post answer the question?" prompts and agree-votes on product suggestions (Wayback, 2005; PC Review, 2005; PC Review, 2009).

Status: defunct. Microsoft announced the closure on 4 May 2010 and closed groups from 1 June to about 1 October 2010, least-active first, pointing each group to a TechNet, MSDN or Microsoft Answers forum (CNET; RXS closure notes). The public groups lingered on third-party Usenet servers for a while (Visual Studio Magazine, Jul 2010). On 7 Nov 2026 Eternal September answered "No such group" for every microsoft.public group I tried, while comp.* groups existed there (own NNTP check). Google Groups stopped carrying new Usenet content on 22 Feb 2024 (PCMag). The private groups were never archived outside Microsoft.

Origin and strategy

1996: leaving CompuServe for the open internet. On 9 April 1996 Microsoft announced "no-charge Microsoft-sponsored NNTP newsgroups." They went live on 15 April and replaced its CompuServe forums on 20 April, a cutover of 12 days (Microsoft press release; Indezine). The project belonged to support. Linda Glenicki, general manager of AnswerPoint, framed the goal as "accurate and timely information that is widely available and affordable." The release spelled out the staffing model: "Microsoft MVPs … will provide technical answers and foster the growth of the online community. Microsoft will stay involved … monitoring responses for accuracy and assisting MVPs as needed." The MVP award itself predates the newsgroups. It began in 1993 when Microsoft staff picked the first 38 MVPs from Calvin Hsia's ranking of the most prolific posters on the FoxPro CompuServe forum (Computerworld, 2007). The newsgroups scaled that model to the whole product line. (See the separate microsoft-mvp report.)

Business logic. The point was support deflection plus feedback. Microsoft's support manager said in 1999, "We do not make a profit on supporting our customers" (Wired). Microsoft Research described the groups as a way of "saving personnel cost by having customers provide help to each other," and as a feedback channel where expert "mentors … feel like an invaluable extension of the company's development process" (Turner & Fisher, 2006). In the developer division, community was framed in 2002 as an "ecosystem," a terrarium: "It takes just the right amount of water and the right amount of nourishment" (CODE Magazine, 2002).

2005–2010: replacement. MSDN Forums (web-based) launched in April 2005, and loyal newsgroup users sent the program manager angry mail (Panopticon Central). By mid-2005 "Microsoft has begun to shift some of its MVP resources away from Usenet" (Boston Globe, Jun 2005). The 2010 closure FAQ gave the reasons: spam and viruses, no moderation, 90-day retention, and above all "we own the app." The forums offered accepted answers, helpful votes, points and badges, RSS alerts, escalation to Microsoft, and search-engine indexing (Microsoft closure FAQ).

Trajectory: fast growth from 1996 to about 2004, alongside Windows 95/98/XP and .NET, with a plateau of 2,000+ public groups. A web front end arrived around 2003. Decline began in 2005 when web forums started up in parallel. Microsoft-measured usage halved in FY2009–10, and the groups closed in 2010. No continuous post-volume series was published; Netscan was shut down "for some time" before 2010 (Jonathan Kay).

What keeps people coming back

  • Daily habit through push delivery. A newsreader pulled new posts into the user's inbox-like client, with read tracking, watched threads and offline reading. Karl Peterson, an MVP since 1994, called it "a more hit and run manner" of use that web forums "completely destroy" (VSM, 2010). The heavy regulars logged hours a day. Doug Knox spent "up to three hours a day on Usenet" (Globe, 2005). Jonathan Kay claimed "a perfect newsgroup record … never missing a day of posts, including weekends, holidays and small surgeries."
  • Status that converted into real value. Microsoft Research interviews found that members gained "employment leads (consulting and long-term)" and reputation. One "wizard" got "a consulting gig and seminars" after other posters referred askers to him (Turner & Fisher).
  • The MVP award as a renewable status loop. It was a one-year award judged on the past year's contribution, and about 80% were renewed each year. It came with summits (almost 2,000 attended in 2007), early product roadmaps, and access to Microsoft support staff, which in 1999 MVPs called the benefit that mattered most (Computerworld; Wired). MVPs were nominated by other MVPs ("An existing MVP put my name in"), so the core recruited itself.
  • Social structure, not features. Microsoft researchers found four roles: members, mentors (the core answerers who "host their own websites, write FAQs, white papers and books"), managers (Microsoft community staff) and moguls (product-team experts whom mentors approach "carefully and reverently"). The mentor-mogul relationship was the reward. Managers made it possible "by inviting both types to engage" (Turner & Fisher). Product architects really did show up: Anders Hejlsberg posted in the C# groups around 2000 (CNET), and a Money product-team member posted explanations of decisions that the MVPs then archived (moneymvps.org).
  • Private "back rooms." The MVP lounges, beta groups and MSDN private groups (2,200+ by 2010) gave insiders a place of their own. That is where the MVP program head announced his departure in 2007 (O'Driscoll).
  • What was missing: no points, badges or accepted answers until the forums. The 2010 FAQ lists those as reasons to migrate. Recognition happened by name in the threads, through the "[MVP]" tag in signatures, and offline through the award.

The content engine

Microsoft's own content was mostly the Knowledge Base, which MVPs linked to in their replies, plus how-to pieces such as MSDN Magazine's setup guides and Office's "Discussion Groups" pages. Most of the real content came from members: MVP-run sites such as mvps.org (whose administrator organized the 1999 protest), moneymvps.org, Chip Pearson's and others' Excel sites, per-group unofficial FAQs (microsoft.public.money FAQ) and welcome posts (Sonia Coleman via Indezine). Microsoft never curated or kept this material. Its server deleted posts after 90 days (Ars Technica), so the durable archive was Google Groups and ad-supported scraper sites (PC Review, TechTalkz, narkive). The 2005 Globe column praised exactly this permanence, as Google's, not Microsoft's. Score 2/5: members produced a huge amount, but the company's own content was thin and it threw the community's content away.

People, programs and governance

  • Volunteers: MVPs and unrecognized regulars answered most questions. Bharat Suneja posted "nearly 7,000 help messages on Exchange newsgroups" in 18 months (Computerworld).
  • Staff: Community Program Managers were assigned to newsgroup clusters (Turner & Fisher). Support engineers posted under "[MSFT]" with the boilerplate "This posting is provided 'AS IS' with no warranties, and confers no rights," and some groups were "staffed weekdays by Microsoft Support professionals" (Google Groups, SBS). One former support engineer recalled logging newsgroup time to avoid "a slap on the fingers at the end of the month" (Register comments, May 2010).
  • Paid tier: MSDN Managed Newsgroups promised subscribers who posted under a registered alias a Microsoft reply within two business days (MSDN Magazine). It answered the 1999 complaint that customers wanted "a guaranteed response time and an escalation path" (Wired). The pairing of the two is my interpretation.
  • Measurement: Netscan (Marc Smith, Microsoft Research) scored authors by days active, reply-to-post and thread-to-post ratios. "Regulars are value contributors" (ZDNet/CNET). Microsoft staff used it internally, mostly to look at author profiles (Brush et al.). Researchers also used it to verify how long community leaders had been active.
  • Rules: Microsoft published a "Rules of conduct," but the groups were unmoderated. Microsoft created and closed groups at will (no Usenet vote process).
  • Feedback: Suggestion posts with "I Agree" votes in the web reader, wish-list links in MVP signatures, MVP advisory councils (promised in 1999) and summits. Staff investment 3/5: real programs and staff, but uneven: the "managed" promise was often missed, and the groups were neglected after 2005.

Growth loops

  1. Default distribution: every Windows PC shipped with Outlook Express, and Microsoft KB articles pointed to the groups (KB 150057).
  2. Askers become answerers: Microsoft Research found that people typically asked first and later started replying, and Netscan tracked when each person first replied. MVPs then nominated the best newcomers.
  3. Search via Google Groups: each answer was indexed for years, which brought searchers in, though Google rather than Microsoft captured that traffic. One 2010 commenter suspected Microsoft had "cottoned on to the fact that Google profit on all ad-clicks" (Register comments).
  4. Expert brands: MVP websites, books and columns all sent readers back to the groups.

Evidence of business impact

Hard numbers are thin. Company claims: the 1999 reversal came after customers (IT managers, company presidents) protested to Gates and Ballmer, which is evidence that customers valued the free channel (Wired). The 2010 FAQ admitted the closure would bring "efficiency through centralizing resources," meaning cost savings. Press and academic claims, not quantified: "somebody in the bureaucracy realized how many millions of dollars the MVPs were saving the company in tech support costs" (Globe, 2005). Peer support "potentially saves a company millions of dollars in support costs" (Turner & Fisher). Monetization: managed-newsgroup support was bundled as a feature of MSDN subscriptions (FMS). No deflection rates, CSAT or retention data were ever published. Score 2/5.

What they did right

  1. Went where users already were, on an open standard, at no cost. They moved off a paid proprietary service (CompuServe) to free NNTP in 12 days, and the community scaled to millions of posters (Microsoft PR; CNET 2003).
  2. Built the community around recognizing the volunteers. The MVP award turned prolific answerers into a status class with renewal pressure, peer nomination and access to the product team. It grew from 600 in 1999 to 4,000+ by 2007 (O'Driscoll) and outlived the platform.
  3. Put staff and product experts in the room. "[MSFT]" engineers, Community Program Managers and architects like Hejlsberg created the mentor-mogul pull that researchers found central.
  4. Measured contribution with data. Netscan's "regulars" metrics were years ahead of most companies' community analytics.
  5. Hybrid free/paid support. A guaranteed-response tier sat on top of peer support (when it worked).
  6. Listened when the core revolted (1999). Microsoft reversed within four days and added an advisory council (Wired).

What they did wrong

  1. Fired the volunteers by email (Oct 1999). Microsoft cancelled the MVP program reportedly fearing AOL-style back-pay suits, then reversed after a "deluge of protests" (Computerworld). Karl Peterson: "They showed no valuation at all to people who'd been designated a valued professional." This left lasting distrust: in 2010 MVPs on the MFC group recalled the episode and called for "an outcry similar to the one when the MVP program was to be axed" (Google Groups).
  2. Threw away the knowledge base. With 90-day retention, the archive and its search and ad value went to Google. This was one of Microsoft's own stated reasons for leaving.
  3. Broken service promises. MSDN subscribers reported managed-group questions "more than a week old with no replies" (Osherove, Oct 2004; TechTalkz, 2007).
  4. Starved the groups, then cited the decline. They ran parallel forums from 2005, took newsgroup links off support pages ("all but been absent from Microsoft's official support web pages," Kay), and then justified the closure by the 50% drop. My reading is that the decline partly fulfilled itself.
  5. The migration ignored how power users worked. The NNTP Bridge was a stopgap with a blank download page for some, and there was no threading or offline reading. The answer rate dropped: an MVP observed the MFC forum at 28% versus "nearly 100%" in the newsgroup (May 2010). Some experts simply left ("Perhaps I'll just stop coming here").
  6. Orphaned communities when products changed course. Classic VB experts were dropped from the MVP program ("until such community contributions were no longer deemed valuable"), and the new forums told VB6 users to go elsewhere (VSM). When Microsoft Money was discontinued (sales ended 2009, the free Money Plus Sunset arrived June 2010), its newsgroup volunteers carried users onto Answers/Q&A, where they were still handing out Sunset download links in 2024–2026 (Wikipedia; Q&A, Dec 2024).
  7. The private groups were lost. Nothing from the 2,200+ beta, MVP and partner groups survives publicly.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make recognition of the top answerers the core of the program. A yearly, renewable award, peer nomination, and real access to the product team for the people who answer most. It turned a few hundred volunteers into thousands and outlived three platforms.
  2. Put product experts in the threads regularly. Members valued the mentor-mogul relationship most. Have engineers and PMs post under visible staff tags.
  3. Measure regulars, not just volume. Track days active and replies to others (Netscan-style) to find future champions before they burn out.
  4. Pair free peer support with a paid guarantee, and actually meet the SLA, because the paid tier is judged on its worst unanswered thread.
  5. Fit members' existing tools. Push delivery into a client people already used daily (Outlook Express) made the community a daily habit, not a destination.

Avoid these

  1. Never let someone else own your archive. 90-day retention handed a decade of answers, and their search traffic, to Google and scraper sites.
  2. Don't treat volunteers as a cost line, or cancel by email. The 1999 email and the 2010 shutdown both taught experts that Microsoft could switch them off.
  3. Don't run parallel platforms and then shut the old one because it declined. Move the expert core first, match their workflow (threading, offline reading, notifications), and check answer rates before you switch off the old space.

Related communities worth adding

  • Microsoft CompuServe forums (MSKB / Microsoft Connection, to 1996) — Microsoft — the predecessor where the MVP award was born; shut down on 20 Apr 1996.
  • Novell Support Connection forums / SysOp volunteers — Novell — vendor-run NNTP/web peer support with a volunteer SysOp program, a direct analogue.
  • Borland newsgroups (borland.public.*, TeamB) — Borland/Embarcadero — vendor NNTP hierarchy with a volunteer expert team (TeamB), which predated MVPs.
  • Macromedia newsgroups / Team Macromedia — Macromedia → Adobe — vendor newsgroups whose volunteer team became Adobe Community Professionals.
  • Corel newsgroups / C_Tech volunteers — Corel — vendor NNTP support with volunteer technicians.
  • Netscape DevEdge / mozilla newsgroups (netscape.public.*) — Netscape/Mozilla — corporate public NNTP hierarchy that became open-source community infrastructure.
  • mvps.org — independent MVP collective — the member-run hub that organized the 1999 protest.
  • GotDotNet — Microsoft — 2002–2007 .NET community site, shut down; an early failed owned community.
  • Windows Live Solution Center forums — Microsoft — short-lived destination for consumer newsgroups in 2010.
