TL;DR

  • What it is: Between 2001 and about 2014, Coca-Cola España ran a members-only youth community on cocacola.es under three names. The Comunidad Coca-Cola (2001–03) and El Movimiento Coca-Cola (Oct 2003–mid 2008) were built by the Madrid studio BetyByte as a downloadable client: members made an avatar "ego", then played multiplayer games and chatted inside them. Happing (May 2008–c.2014) was a Web 2.0 rebuild by Genetsis. Membership grew from 100,000 in 2001 to about 1.5 million in 2007, which made it the case study in Sicilia & Palazón (2008) (Emerald; El Mundo, 2003).
  • Why people came back: social multiplayer games came first and the brand second. Members met strangers in Tunnel of Love, flirted in Empareja-2 and played Fútbol 11. Around the games sat rankings, a "boy/girl of the week" vote, clans, real-life meet-ups (kedadas), and PIN codes under bottle caps that earned points toward prizes. In 2004 the average session was 50 minutes, with 150,000 users a week (Cinco Días, 2004).
  • What Coke fed it: a new game every few months, a radio station, five mascot characters (Emcy, Pass, Teky, Loop, Marea) carried into TV, radio and outdoor ads, multi-million-euro TV pushes, partner prizes (consoles, phones, motorbikes, trips), and sponsored in-game billboards sold to other brands (BetyByte timeline; MarketingDirecto, 2004).
  • Biggest mistake: in 2008 Coke swapped the whole world for Happing, citing "technical problems linked to the control and management of the data". Members' avatars, games and friendships didn't carry over, and the 1.5 million members trickled across "little by little". Happing never got members talking to each other: by 2012 it was a promo magazine, and in Feb 2015 it disappeared (Cinco Días, 2008; Prestigia, 2012).
  • The lesson: build the community around things members do together (play, match, meet), tie it to a purchase signal, and own the platform and data from the first day. If you replace the experience members came for with "share your content", 90% of them will just lurk, and then leave.

What the community is

A members-only "youth meeting place" at www.cocacola.es, for Spaniards aged about 14–25 (Coke later said 13–23 because it avoids marketing to under-12s). It had three phases:

Phase Dates Builder Core experience
Comunidad Coca-Cola Mar 2001 (games), Jan 2002 (community) to Oct 2003 BetyByte Entertainment (Madrid) Downloadable multiplayer quiz games Quorum and Congenio, then avatar "graphic chats" and games: Tunnel of Love, Fútbol 11, Empareja-2, Universal
El Movimiento Coca-Cola Oct 2003 to mid 2008 BetyByte (tech), McCann Erickson and Zentropy (campaign), Universal McCann (media) The same worlds plus new 3D games (Snow Battle, Summer Battle, Letra-2), radio, PIN-code "ventajas", partner promotions
Happing ("happy" + "happening") ~May 2008 to c.2014 Genetsis and Solusoft; Webedia wrote the magazine from 2009 Profiles, photos, video, blog, "Happing Map" on Google Maps, free SMS, UGC contests. Ended as a blog/magazine

Sources: MarketingDirecto, 2001; BetyByte timeline; IPMARK, 2008; Solusoft; Weblogs SL, 2009.

A September 2002 Wayback capture of the home page shows the navigation: Juegos, Canales, Foros, Ranking, Kedadas, Clanes, Buscar personas, Ventajas (marked with a bottle-cap icon), plus Mi cuenta, Mi personaje, Mis mensajes, Mis amigos, Mis citas, Mi radio, Mis premios. A live counter showed registered users and how many were connected to each game (Wayback, 26 Sep 2002).

Status: defunct. cocacola.es/happing still served a magazine in January 2014 (posts with one comment each, ticket giveaways) and returned 404 by February 2015 (Wayback 2014; CDX). From May 2010 Coke Spain's youth relationship moved to a Tuenti brand page, and later to Facebook, Twitter and the Coke app (Comunicar preprint). No official fan-run successor exists. A 2000s fan site, "Eltunnel", tried to revive Tunnel of Love and was told that Coca-Cola owned the game (Mediavida search snippet; the page itself is behind a bot wall).

Origin and strategy

  • Why: Coke Spain had run short promotional microsites since 1997. In 1999 it launched a permanent brand site and decided to "exploit every business possibility the internet offers". In 2001 it launched the Comunidad Coca-Cola "to create the biggest meeting point for young people on the Spanish internet" (Redes&Telecom, 2002). The thesis came from BetyByte's founder, Ángel Ybáñez: "The internet is above all about relationships, even more than information… and you can own it, unlike TV or radio". Nobody seeks out information about soft drinks, so you offer entertainment instead (El Mundo, 2003).
  • Champions: Coke Spain's president Marcos de Quinto "believed in the project from the start" (El Mundo, 2003). The marketing director, Manuel Arroyo, framed it as "a new concept: individualised marketing for mass consumer products" (Cinco Días, 2004). Hugo Giralt (Interactive Marketing, Iberian Division) ran the infrastructure (Telefónica Data hosting, 2002). Marta Fontcuberta led the Happing relaunch in 2008.
  • Business model link:
    1. Purchase signal: a 14-digit PIN code inside the caps of 0.5L to 2L bottles was entered into a personal points account (MarketingDirecto, 2003). By weighting the points, Coke could even push demand toward slow-moving pack sizes (Sicilia & Palazón summary, Prezi).
    2. First-party youth database: "thanks to the databases and statistics we segment messages precisely" (BetyByte's production director, ABC, 2002).
    3. Owned media that pays: "CyberSpots" (10-second full-screen Flash ads shown on entering and leaving the games) and in-game billboards were sold to Ya.com, Uno-e, Kellogg's, Telefónica, Jazztel and others.

Trajectory: very fast growth from 2001 to 2004, gained mostly through TV and the PIN codes. Growth slowed after the 1M mark (mid-2004) and reached about 1.5M by 2007. The 2008 migration reset the active base to 200,000 uniques, and the community faded into a magazine by 2012–14. Registration counts overstate activity: the online-now counters in 2002–03 showed only 38–103 people connected at sampled moments (all early-morning or overnight captures, so they are lower bounds). Weekly actives were about 15% of registrations in 2004.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Avatars and identity. You entered as a self-designed "virtual ego", with 400,000 possible combinations of hair, eyes, clothes and personality. Its profile card was used to score compatibility with other avatars (ABC, 2002). Prizes included exclusive avatar clothing and furniture for your house in Universal (Martínez-Rodrigo & Sánchez, 2008).
  2. Social games built to start conversations. In Quorum and Congenio the winning answer was the most popular one rather than the "right" one, so the games rewarded reading the room. Tunnel of Love was a city scavenger hunt with mallet fights over objects, running on the server characters' requests. Empareja-2 was a boys-versus-girls quiz that matched couples. Every game had chat. In the community's own framing (and Sicilia & Palazón's), "the relationship between members is more important than the relationship between members and the brand".
  3. Status. Each game had a winners' ranking. The community voted on a weekly "boy/girl of the week" and on categories such as "the sexiest". Top players' nicks appeared on the home page (Prezi summary of the paper; 2002 Wayback home page).
  4. Belonging and IRL. Clans let groups talk, play as a team and share interests. Kedadas arranged real-life meet-ups. "Find people" searched members by age, sex and similar criteria. Members formed lasting friendships through these: "I've got some mates on MSN I met in these little games" (Forocoches, 2011).
  5. Points and prizes tied to buying Coke. "The more you play, the more points you win… the player never loses". There were more than 1,200 prizes a week in 2001, and PIN codes unlocked extra points and challenges (MarketingDirecto, 2001). Movimiento-era prizes included 10,000 Movistar phones, consoles, motorbikes and trips around Spain.
  6. Novelty cadence. A new multiplayer game roughly every 3–6 months: Tunnel of Love (Jan 2002), Fútbol 11 (mid 2002), Empareja-2, Universal (2002), RoadRaider and Snow Battle 3D (late 2003), Letra-2 (2004–05), Summer Battle 3D (2004/2005) (BetyByte timeline).

Retention score 4/5: the core retention loop (avatar, then games, then status, then friends) was strong and produced 50-minute sessions, but there was no member-owned creative layer comparable to MyCoke's music studios.

The content engine

  • Company-made: the games (BetyByte built its own lightweight client, 1–3 MB, with a custom TCP/UDP server), Radio Coca-Cola, news "canales", and campaign storylines told through the five Movimiento mascots in TV, radio, outdoor, internet and an MSN Messenger tab (Cinco Días, 2004). Acquisition media spend was heavy: about €3M on TV in 2000, €400,000 on an online-only push, and about €4M on the 2003 campaign. BetyByte said the online-only campaign was both the most effective and the cheapest (El Mundo, 2003).
  • Offline seeding: in 2001 three branded buses toured Spanish universities and handed out more than 500,000 game CDs (MarketingDirecto, 2001).
  • Member-made (Happing era): the "Lola" ad-remix contest drew about 2,200 user versions in a month (1.1% of uniques), 150 of which aired on TV. There were also "Final happing" collaborative story endings with winning members named on the blog, a "Fotomatón", and a "Happiness Map" (Cinco Días, 2008; MarketingNews, 2008; Wayback blog). From 2009, Webedia's staff wrote an editorial magazine (Weblogs SL, 2009).

Content score 3/5: a reliable stream of new games and promotions, but almost no member-created content during the community's best years. The Happing UGC push came too late and engaged about 1% of users.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: an outsourced studio (BetyByte, about 2000–2008) did development, art, the "BetyByte Entertainment" admin character who hosted the quiz rooms, and promotions tech (secure code validation, later sold to other brands as "BetyCodes"). Coke's Iberian interactive marketing team owned the strategy, and McCann, Zentropy and Universal McCann handled media. Under Happing, Genetsis and Solusoft built the platform and a BI layer reporting "impacts, relationships and contacts" to Coke (Solusoft).
  • Moderation: in 2008 Coke said a webmaster vetted inappropriate content for the teen audience (Cinco Días, 2008). No evidence was found of moderation policy in the Movimiento era. A suggestion box (buzón de sugerencias) was the visible feedback channel.
  • Ambassador or superuser program: no evidence found. Recognition came from rankings and votes, not from formal roles.
  • Staff investment score 4/5: a proprietary platform, dedicated hosting, multiple agencies and repeated national TV campaigns, but no in-house community managers on record.

Growth loops

  • Paid mass media into a sticky product: each TV wave produced a jump in registrations (150k to 500k in six months of 2002).
  • Purchase-to-site loop: every cap carried a code that only paid off online.
  • Social pull: matchmaking and friend features (Mis amigos, Mis citas, MSN) brought friends in, and the press credited "above all, word of mouth" (El Mundo, 2003).
  • Partner prizes: other brands paid for access, which funded better prizes, which drew more members.
  • Export: the platform was licensed to Coca-Cola's Nordic "Redzone" sites (redzone.dk, .no, .nu, .fi) from 2003 (ABC, 2002; El Mundo, 2003).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard-ish (independently judged): the Asociación Española de Anunciantes Eficacia awards, which require submitted results: a Gold for interactive media effectiveness for Comunidad Coca-Cola 2002 (Dialnet, MK 2004), and the 2004 Gran Premio a la Eficacia en Medios for "Movimiento Coca-Cola" with Universal McCann (Cinco Días, 2004; Reason Why). It also won an Eficacia Gold for interactive marketing in 2005, the Imán loyalty-program awards in 2004 and 2005, and Coca-Cola's internal award for best marketing initiative by a subsidiary (MarketingDirecto, 2004; BetyByte timeline). The underlying case books (MK Marketing+Ventas no. 192, 2004; Harvard Deusto Marketing y Ventas no. 74, 2006, Dialnet) weren't accessible.
  • Company claims: Coke "expects 30% of the increase in litres of Coca-Cola sold in 2004 to come from the Movimiento", trackable through PIN codes. More than €2M was received from advertisers on the games (MarketingDirecto, 2004). Happing's lead said in 2008 that the site was not meant to increase sales volume.
  • Unverified claims to discard: a 2016 student blog says Coca-Cola Spain "generated 3 billion euros in operating income after launching the virtual community" and got "50,000 daily hits" (socialmediarookie). No primary source supports this, and it is not a causal figure. Some papers citing Sicilia & Palazón misdescribe it as a Facebook community.
  • Evidence score 2/5: the awards confirm that effectiveness was judged, but no sales lift, retention or LTV figure is public.

What they did right

  • Played to the audience's real motive (meeting people), not to the product. Every flagship game was a social mixer, and members remember the strangers they met rather than the soda (Forocoches, 2011–2026).
  • Bridged offline purchase and online status with cap PIN codes. That gave Coke a measurable consumption signal and gave members a reason to buy (one member admits "I used to steal pincodes at the Alimerka", Forocoches, Sep 2026).
  • A steady release cadence (a new game every few months) gave lapsed members reasons to return.
  • Built low-friction tech for 2002 bandwidth: clients of 1–3 MB, and a dedicated Telefónica host with SLAs before each TV push (Redes&Telecom, 2002).
  • Turned the audience into a media business: partner prizes and CyberSpots helped fund the community.

What they did wrong

  • The platform belonged to a vendor, so Coke had to migrate. In 2008 Coke gave "technical problems linked to the control and management of the data" as the main reason for leaving the Movimiento. The games belonged to Coca-Cola, but the technology was BetyByte's. Members had to re-register on Happing ("launchMigration" and "actualiza tus datos" pop-ups on the May 2008 home page). The result was 200,000 uniques against a base of 1.5M.
  • They replaced play with "share your content". Fontcuberta admitted that "90% of users are only readers". The Lola contest drew 1.1% participation. A member in 2011 said it plainly: "Now there's just silly stuff, filming yourself acting the fool or love quotes… bah" (Forocoches). By 2012 Happing "drifted from a social network that never could be into a magazine for young people", its users "remained passive" despite free-SMS giveaways (Prestigia).
  • They fought Tuenti and Facebook instead of building on them. By 2010 Coke Spain was on Tuenti anyway, and the owned community lost its reason to exist.
  • Teen safety and reputation risks: a flirting and matchmaking game for 12–25-year-olds with open chat. Former members joke "what a nest of paedophiles that must have been" (Forocoches, Oct 2015). There's no evidence of a recorded incident, but the design invited the risk. Coca-Cola's under-12 marketing policy came later (2008) as an age line.
  • No exit for members' identities: avatars, rankings and friend graphs were lost. Fans still ask in 2014 and 2022 where they can play it again.
  • Reliability: some members believe "two or three hackers kept knocking the servers over" (Forocoches, Aug 2022). This is unverified and only member lore.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make the community about members doing things together. Short multiplayer sessions with chat built in (matchmaking, team play, scavenger hunts) created the friendships members still remember 20 years later.
  2. Give members a persistent identity they invest in: an avatar, a house, inventory and ranks. Then make rewards cosmetic for that identity.
  3. Tie a real-world purchase or usage signal to in-community rewards. For an app, reward usage milestones inside the community. This gives you attribution and gives members a reason to come back.
  4. Ship new things to do on a predictable cadence (Coke managed one every 3–6 months), and announce each with an event.
  5. Add real-life meet-ups and clans early. Small groups and IRL meetings turn a site into a social graph.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let a vendor own your platform, code or member data. Control of the data was the stated reason the 1.5M community was torn down and rebuilt.
  2. Don't "upgrade" a play community into a UGC or social network. Most members lurk (the 90/10 rule Coke itself quoted). If you remove the activity they came for, they'll leave.
  3. Don't run a migration without portability. Carry identities, friends, items and status over automatically, or expect to lose most of your active base.
