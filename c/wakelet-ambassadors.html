TL;DR

  • What it is. Wakelet is a Manchester-built tool for curating links and files into "collections," widely used by K-12 teachers. Its community has three tiers: Community Member, Community Leader (added August 2020) and Ambassador (January 2019). Around them sit a Student Ambassador course, a Schools Program, Showcase Schools, a US-only Certified Trainer program and a free yearly virtual conference, Wakelet Community Week. It all runs under the #WakeletWave brand.
  • Why people come back. For teachers it's career capital: a badge for the Twitter bio, a certificate, a speaking slot at Community Week or ISTE, beta access and a direct line to staff. Monthly ambassador "waves" and meetings keep it visible, and a yearly renewal (now tied to a points quota) keeps people active.
  • How the company fed it. For a startup of 36-45 people it put in a lot of staff: a Head of Community plus a community manager and coordinator. It ran webinars, guest blogs, resource packs, template collections and a conference whose sessions are mostly given by ambassadors.
  • Biggest mistake. Wakelet built a decade of evangelism on "free and ad-free" and stayed largely pre-revenue. It went into administration in April 2025 and capped free accounts at three editable collections from 1 November 2025. Its unpaid advocates are now asked to earn "Impact Points" through referral codes and school sales to keep their status.
  • The lesson. A status ladder and conference stage can recruit hundreds of passionate educators cheaply. But if the business model isn't settled first, the community later has to be repurposed into a sales channel, and the message the advocates spent years spreading ("it's free!") gets turned against them.

What the community is

The Wakelet Community began in November 2018 as an application-only group of "Superhumans" who tested features and spread the word (Wakelet blog via Wayback, 27 Nov 2018). It now has three levels: Community Member, Community Leader and Wakelet Ambassador (Wakelet help center). Members join free. Leaders apply and get a certificate, swag, exclusive meetings, webinar slots and the chance to make their school "an official Wakelet school." Ambassadors apply from the Leader tier to "represent Wakelet on the international stage" (community page, Wayback Dec 2023).

Status today: active, smaller and more commercial. On 10 April 2025 Wakelet Limited went into administration. The same day the administrators sold the business to Reviven Limited, a company the founder Jamil Khalil had set up in February 2025, and all 23 staff moved with it (Leonard Curtis; BusinessCloud). Reviven was renamed Wakelet Technologies Limited on 28 April 2025 (Kinescope EdTech Map). The ambassador program survived: monthly meetings were livestreamed in September 2025 [18], a "final Wakelet Ambassador meeting" of 2025 took place in December [19], and renewal emails in August 2026 confirmed "you'll remain a Wakelet Ambassador for another year and… your account has been upgraded to Wakelet Pro for one year" [20]. Origin and strategy

A Storify windfall. Wakelet launched in 2014 as a general curation tool that its founder pitched as a future Pinterest (BusinessCloud, Apr 2025). The education community came out of an accident. When Storify announced its shutdown (it closed on 16 May 2018), Wakelet built a Storify importer, and staff personally helped educators move their archives. Dexter Roona, later a Wave 1 ambassador, wrote that "James Davis at Wakelet first introduced me to Wakelet and offered me a solution to migrate all my Storify content away before it was lost forever" (infobunny, 2019; York St John TEL blog, May 2018). Teachers who ran Twitter chats were exactly the people Storify had served, and they arrived with large Twitter followings.

Why the company built it. The founder openly put growth ahead of monetization: "The best thing I can do is build a product that's got great features, get people using the product and grow it – I can monetise it later on" (2016, quoted in BusinessCloud). For a free product with a thin marketing budget, unpaid teacher evangelists were the cheapest growth engine available. The 2019 launch post asked ambassadors to "shout about Wakelet at every opportunity," put the badge in their Twitter bio and email signature, and make their own graphics, blogs and presentations (Wakelet blog via Wayback, 15 Jan 2019). A second goal was product feedback: the Community "inspired – and helped us test" bulk tweet import, notifications, QR-code sharing and collection copying [1].

Champions. James Davis (Community Manager in 2018, then Head of Community, now Head of Growth) led it throughout. A colleague's LinkedIn recommendation says he led "our Community and Ambassador programmes to harness the power of brand advocates and taking word-of-mouth marketing to new heights" (LinkedIn snippet). Misbah Gedal (partnerships, now Head of Business Development) ran the Flipgrid tie-up and the student program. In 2021 the community leads page listed five staff (Callum, James, Helen, Misbah, Teijas) (Wayback, Oct 2021). By December 2023 it listed three named community roles: Head of Community, Community Manager (Amy Kyle) and Community Coordinator (Adam Howarth) [2].

The Flipgrid partnership. Wakelet partnered with Microsoft-owned Flipgrid in about late 2018, teased a joint integration at ISTE19 and shipped the Flipgrid Shorts camera inside Wakelet in September 2019. Flipgrid's Adam Parker Goldberg credited "our incredible shared community" (BusinessCloud; Wakelet webinar, Sep 2019). The two ambassador pools overlapped heavily. Teachers routinely listed both titles: "Flipgrid student voice Ambassador, Flipgrid grid guide, @wakelet Ambassador" (X, Apr 2020). Wakelet collections became the standard way to archive and recap edtech events, Flipgrid's included. Flip itself was shut down in 2024 (see the flip-flipgrid report in this dataroom).

What keeps people coming back

  1. A visible status ladder. Member → Leader → Ambassador, each with a profile badge displayed inside the product ("These badges help you proudly display your role" (help center)) and in Twitter bios. Year-stamped badges ("My colourful @Wakelet 2021 Badges has arrived!" [21]) and certificates feed teachers' PD portfolios. Third-party badge directories list "Wakelet Community Ambassador" as an accreditation [22].
  2. Selective, staged entry. In 2021 an applicant had to be a Community Leader with at least 10 collections or 100 bookmarks, and had to build an application inside Wakelet from a template collection. The page added: "Many of our Ambassadors were successful after a few tries" [23]. Rejection with "tips for re-applying" [3] makes the title feel earned, and the application itself teaches the product.
  3. Monthly rituals. Waves were announced monthly [3]. Ambassadors meet monthly with staff; the meetings are livestreamed and members post about them ("The Wakelet Ambassador Monthly Meeting - September 2025 is LIVE now!" [18]). In December, Wakelet runs "Wakelet Wrapped" stats and a "Wrap-Up Calendar" that features one ambassador story a day [24][25].
  4. A yearly stage. Community Week gives ambassadors keynote-adjacent airtime (spotlight sessions, a 12-hour marathon) and ends with Wakelet LIVE, where the roadmap is revealed and the program includes "a tribute to the Wakelet Community" [10][11].
  5. Insider access. Beta features, direct contact with staff ("always happy to jump on a Hangout"), and the feeling of shaping the product [26]. The TCEA's ambassador blogger: "The Wakelet Team is incredibly responsive" (TCEA, Jun 2023).
  6. Renewal with a hook. Since the 2025 paywall, ambassadors get a free year of Individual Pro ($89.99/yr list price) [17][20]. Keeping that perk now requires 30 Impact Points per term [17]. This is a real switching cost, because a lapsed ambassador falls back to the 3-collection Starter plan.
  7. A pipeline for students and schools. Students earn six badges and a "profile overlay" [8]. Schools earn Showcase status by completing tasks and recruiting other schools [13]. Both pull a teacher's whole building into the identity.

Weak spots: there is no in-product social graph (no feed, follows or leaderboards that matter), so retention lives mostly in email, Twitter and Zoom. And as Twitter/X educator culture declined after 2022, the #WakeletWave amplification loop weakened with it.

The content engine

  • Company-made: the blog (how-tos, ambassador "My Wakelet Journey" guest posts), YouTube webinars (feature walk-throughs, "Community Showcase" webinars in April and September 2023 [27]), an Ambassador Resource Pack (collection), and monthly Schools webinars where Showcase Schools present [13]. Wakelet LIVE is the yearly product keynote [10].
  • Member-made, deliberately cultivated: the program asked for UGC from day one: "producing your own social media graphics, writing blog posts, putting together presentations for events" [4]. Early members wrote getting-started guides (infobunny), made YouTube tutorials, gave conference talks and wrote guest posts for Wakelet's own blog [1]. Templates and featured collections fill the Explore page and the "Community Showcase" (showcase.wakelet.com) [28]. Community Week is mostly member content; in 2024 it reduced sessions to "bite-size" 15-30 minute slots after feedback [11].
  • Now monetized: Impact Points pay 2 points per approved template or collection and 10 per approved case study [17]. The content engine has become a points economy.
  • Cadence: monthly (waves, meetings, webinars), yearly (Community Week in May-August, Wrapped and the calendar in December). Score 3/5: steady and member-heavy, but small-scale and not clearly tied to activation or paid conversion.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: typically 3-5 community staff out of 36-45 employees (2021-2023) [2][23]. That's a high share for a startup. After the 2025 administration the remaining team of about 23 still runs meetings, but the community lead now carries the title Head of Growth, which shows where the program sits strategically (Kinescope). Staff investment 3/5: strong for its size and consistent over 8 years, but shrinking and now growth-led.

Growth loops

  1. Teacher-to-teacher word of mouth via Twitter chats. Ambassadors moderated #WakeletWave chats ("Chat Moderator and Wakelet Ambassador @lieberrian will be joined by fellow Ambassador @sherryngick", May 2019), and the tool archived other chats.
  2. Conference circuit. Ambassadors presented at ISTE19 [31] and at state conferences (VSTE 2020, TCCA 2022), so the company got booth-level reach without the booth.
  3. Content as SEO. Every public collection, guest blog and tutorial is indexable. Event recap collections (Community Week, partner events) spread the brand to every attendee.
  4. Classroom → school → district. Student Ambassadors and Showcase Schools recruit peers; Showcase applicants were told that "proving that you have already invited other schools… will be a massive boost" [13]. Since 2025, 30 Impact Points reward a school purchase directly [17].
  5. Paid referral codes (2025-26). Ambassadors now post discount codes publicly ("upgrade using this: GEORGIA20 and get a 20% discount! It's worth it!!" [32]).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: almost none. Wakelet is private and never published retention, conversion or community ROI. Its filed accounts show shareholders' funds falling from £4.92M (2022) to £2.02M (2023) and staff falling from about 45 to 36. It "struggled to raise investment," remained "largely pre-revenue," and entered administration in April 2025 [BusinessCloud].
  • Company claims: 2M+ educators and students in 2018-19 [ACCESSWIRE]; "10M+" trusted users and 25M+ collections today [About]; "millions of educators… across 140 countries" in the community [Community page]. EdTech Impact users report 99% "improve teaching efficiency" (EdTech Impact). These are product claims, not community metrics.
  • Inference (estimate): the program clearly bought reach and brand affection among edtech "influencer teachers" at low cost. It could not by itself solve the lack of a paying customer. The 2025-26 redesign (Pro perk, referral cash, school-sale points) is Wakelet admitting the program now has to show revenue. No academic research on the community was found (OpenAlex budget was exhausted during this session, so the check is incomplete). Business-impact evidence 1/5.

What they did right

  1. Turned a competitor's shutdown into a community. Hands-on Storify migration help converted the exact users (chat hosts, librarians, edtech bloggers) who became Wave 1 [infobunny; 1].
  2. Made the application itself product training. Requiring a Wakelet collection built from a template, with 10 or more collections of prior use, filtered for real users and deepened their habit [23].
  3. Designed a ladder, not a single title. Adding the Leader tier in 2020 created a reachable middle step and a gate before the scarce Ambassador title [12]. School and student variants spread the identity sideways [8][13].
  4. Gave members the stage. Community Week is mostly ambassador-run, with grad credit in 2020 and mindfulness and student sessions later. It's cheap for Wakelet and prestigious for teachers [9][11].
  5. Responsive, named staff. Faces, emails and Twitter handles on the community page, and a promise to "jump on a Hangout," made the relationships personal and lasting [23][26].
  6. Survived the company's collapse. The founder buy-back kept the team, and renewals, monthly meetings and Community Week 2025 all continued, which retained a loyal international core [18][19][20]. Durability 3/5: about 8 years running, through a near-death event, but with fading reach.

What they did wrong

  1. Built advocacy on "free," then paywalled it. "From the start, we've kept Wakelet free and ad-free… we're simplifying our free plan" (founder letter). From 1 November 2025, free accounts got 3 editable collections; the rest became view-only (help center). Years of ambassador tutorials telling colleagues to make dozens of free collections went stale overnight. Comparison sites now steer teachers elsewhere ("Since November 2025, Wakelet's free plan allows 3 editable collections" (Instaboard)). Some third-party content still wrongly says Wakelet is unlimited and free (Medium, Jun 2026).
  2. Postponed monetization for a decade. The founder's 2016 plan to "monetise it later" meant the community's evangelism produced users, not revenue. When funding dried up, the company went into administration, staff fell to about 23, and the program lost budget [BusinessCloud].
  3. Turned recognition into a quota. The 30-point Impact Points rule, where cash vouchers, discount codes and 30 points for a school sale count toward keeping your title [17], shifts ambassadors from respected experts to affiliates. Classroom teachers posting referral codes risk looking like salespeople to their colleagues and districts.
  4. Depended on rented platforms. Its amplification lived on Twitter (chats, #WakeletWave, ISTE watch-alongs). As educator Twitter fragmented after 2022, reach collapsed: current ambassador posts draw tens of impressions (X API, Nov 2026). The owned hub (Circle, from about 2023) came late.
  5. Never published community metrics or closed the feedback loop publicly. There's no ambassador count, no hub size, and no "you asked, we built" record after 2018. That makes the program hard to defend internally and hard for members to see their own impact.
  6. Service frustrations among long-time users. Trustpilot sits at 3/5, with complaints about support and lockouts from former Storify migrants (Trustpilot).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Recruit from a competitor's shutdown with hands-on migration help. The people you rescue become your most grateful evangelists.
  2. Make the application an act of using the product. A template-built portfolio with a usage minimum filters for real users and deepens their habit at the same time.
  3. Build a three-step ladder with a reachable middle rung, and give each rung an in-product badge plus a shareable certificate that's useful for professional development.
  4. Run one yearly member-led conference that ends in a roadmap keynote. It's cheap to produce, gives members prestige, and reveals your best members.
  5. Put named, reachable staff on the community page and keep a monthly ambassador meeting going even when budgets shrink.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let your advocates sell "free" unless free is permanent. Settle your pricing model before you build an evangelist army, or later paywalls will turn their old content and reputations against you.
  2. Don't turn recognition into a sales quota. If you add referral rewards, keep them optional and separate from status. Tying title renewal to referral cash and school-sale points turns trusted experts into affiliates.
  3. Don't build the whole loop on a rented social network. Move rituals into an owned space (and publish its metrics) before the platform's culture shifts.
