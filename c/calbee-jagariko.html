TL;DR

  • What it is: a members-only fan site for Calbee's Jagarico potato sticks, styled as a school. Fans passed an entrance exam (an essay on their love of Jagarico), enrolled for three years, attended lessons taught by Calbee staff "teachers", and graduated. It opened in February 2007, before the iPhone reached Japan, and closed on 31 March 2021 (2, 13).
  • Why people came back: school rituals. There was daily "attendance" with a real medal for a perfect year, report cards, regional classes with peer officers, points to spend in the school store, monthly poem and photo contests, and the headline event: a year-long project in which students chose a new flavor, package, character and pun that then went on sale nationwide. That produced 13 products from 2009 to 2021 (4, 12, 14).
  • What Calbee did to feed it: brand managers wrote a near-daily blog in character as teachers, ran lessons and tastings, sent prototypes to about 500 students a year, and kept the company's habit of answering handwritten letters by hand. All of this ran on a thin crew: for two years one manager ran the whole site apart from systems and moderation (11, 9).
  • Biggest mistake: Calbee closed it wholesale, along with its parent fan club Calsapo!, and deleted every post, photo and point, rather than evolving it. The open Twitter project that replaced it drew about four times more ideas but lost the depth. Within 2.5 years Calbee opened a new gated, test-to-join community explicitly "to revive じゃがり校", then moved it to another platform in 2026 (16, 10, 22, 25).
  • The one lesson: a familiar social structure (school year, classmates, graduation) gave a low-involvement snack a community that renewed itself every April. Pair that with one real yearly decision that lands on store shelves. Keep the archive and the people when you change platforms.

What the community is

じゃがり校 (a pun on Jagarico, じゃがりこ, and 校, "school") was a closed web community on Calbee's corporate site. It started as それいけ！じゃがり校 ("Go! Jagari School") in February 2007 and was renamed あつまれ！とびだせ！じゃがり校 ("Gather! Jump out! Jagari School") on 4 April 2018 (11). Admission opened once a year: an online exam ran from December to February, with six multiple-choice questions and a 100–200 character essay on "your passion for Jagarico". Results came out in April, and the 2,000–3,000 annual applicants started as first-years (2, 34). Calbee advertised the exam on the Jagarico packaging itself, so it reached people who were already buying (3, 7).

The site's menu followed a school timetable (4, 12, 3):

  • 朝礼 "Morning assembly": a near-daily staff blog with product news and glimpses of the brand team's work, including regional products not shown on the public site.
  • 職員室 "Staff room": each teacher's diary.
  • Lessons: Japanese (a monthly Jagarico senryu, or short poem, contest with votes), English (translating product news), social studies (Jagarico history and "field trips" behind the scenes of product planning), and Morals (students post a daily good deed and praise each other).
  • ホームルーム "Homeroom": threaded boards by class and topic.
  • 購買部 "School store": students spent points earned by visiting and posting on limited merchandise.
  • Events: a school festival in autumn, new-product tastings in person, and the yearly 新商品開発プロジェクト (New Product Development Project) (1).

Official vs unofficial spaces. I found no significant fan-run space. Fan life outside the site ran through members' personal blogs (Ameba, Yahoo! Blogs), which grew out of class friendships (31, 11). Reddit has no Jagarico community of note: the Arctic Shift archive was rate-limiting during this research, and Brave turned up only snack-review threads.

Status today. Defunct since 31 March 2021. The lineage continues: じゃがりこ探検隊 ("Jagarico Expedition Team") opened on Commune in October 2023 with an entry test, and it relaunched as じゃがりこじま ("Jagarico Island") on Asobica's coorum, inside the Calbee Ruby Program app, on 8 September 2026 (21, 25).

Origin and strategy

  • Roots in letter-writing. Since Jagarico's 1995 launch, staff have answered handwritten fan letters with handwritten replies. The fan site began as a way to have that conversation with more people over the internet (10, 7). A brand manager in 2007 wanted "a way to build the brand while talking directly with customers" (2).
  • Business problem. By its tenth year Jagarico was a mature brand. The site was meant to support a rethink of the flavor lineup and to raise the loyalty of heavy users (12). Limited flavors matter for Jagarico because they refresh the core range; one limited flavor (tarako butter) sold about twice the usual volume (12). Jagarico grew from more than ¥30bn in shipments around 2013 to about ¥35bn in 2018 (29).
  • Co-creation emerged from the members. At first the site was just a fan hangout. Members' "I want to eat this flavor" posts led to member-driven product development from about 2008, and the first co-created flavor, Carbonara, launched in February 2009 (12).
  • A deliberate small-community strategy. Kobe University professor Kei Kuriki argues the site survived the shift to social media because Calbee stopped treating it as a mass channel and ran it as a "content development base". The exam and graduation kept it small and closed enough for candid, two-way talk (5). Brand manager Atsushi Matsui (じゃがりこ課 section chief from 2014) put it simply: "a hurdle gathers hot-blooded people willing to put in the effort" (4).
  • Champions inside Calbee. Before 2014 the site was slated for closure over running costs. It was saved only after "a desperate appeal" from its staff ("can we abandon the fans' passion?") (4). Calbee debated "several times" whether so much effort for about 3,000 people a year was justified (9).

Trajectory. Intake held steady at about 3,000 a year for most of the site's life, which gave a steady state of roughly 9,000–10,000 students. Applications slipped from about 2014, and the members skewed toward housewives while Calbee also wanted younger fans (4). Idea volume rose late on (2,200 in 2017, 2,992 in 2020), partly because from 2018 Calbee took flavor ideas from the public on an open landing page and kept the later steps inside the school (5). Dates of the 13 products: Carbonara 2009, Fried Chicken 2010, Cheese Curry 2011, Edamame Cheese 2012, Scallop Soy Butter 2013, Asparagus Bacon 2014, Mozzarella Tomato 2015, Omedetai 2016, Shiso Cheese 2017, Honey Butter 2018, Cheese Dakgalbi 2019, Aburi Mentaiko 2020, Garibata Soy 2021 (12, 5, 32, 14).

What keeps people coming back

  1. A familiar social structure. Matsui credits the school format with the site's longevity. Everyone has been to school, so the exam, the senior–junior relationships across grades, teaching each other and inter-class contests needed no explanation (8).
  2. Cohorts and graduation. New students arrived together every April, which "refreshed the whole site". Graduation meant Calbee never had to ask long-time fans to step aside, and it softened the usual divide between veterans and newcomers. Alumni could stay as advisers (8, 11). From 2014, graduates could comment but were barred from product development, so ideas stayed fresh (4).
  3. Attendance as a streak. Logging in counted as attendance. A full year of weekdays earned 皆勤賞 (perfect attendance), and 365 days earned 超皆勤賞 (super perfect attendance), with a real medal posted to your home. End-of-term report cards graded each student's activity (4, 2). "Perfect attendance" was the fourth most common word in class F's April 2014 posts (11).
  4. Points with tangible rewards. Visits and posts earned Calbee points, which could be spent in the school store (eco-bags, lids, the yearly calendar) or on other Calbee campaigns (4, 12). Members kept using the calendar for years (33a).
  5. Belonging beyond the product. In Masahiko Hato's netnography (he enrolled in 2014 and observed until 2018), most class-board talk was off-topic chat about local festivals, weather and cherry blossoms. Even so, identification with the class drove attachment to the brand, more than product knowledge did. Second- and third-years and alumni felt the strongest ties (11). Calbee deliberately did not restrict topics. Matsui said nobody can talk only about a snack for long (11).
  6. One real decision a year. The development project gave every cohort a public outcome: their flavor, drawing, pun and character on a national product. Students who went on to taste it felt "special joy" (27).
  7. Parasocial ties to "teachers". Brand staff played subject teachers (Matsui taught P.E.) and wrote in character, including one who "swung a bamboo sword" at rule-breakers (7, 31). At the close, past principals and staff, including one on parental leave, posted relay farewells for about a month (10).

The content engine

  • Staff-made, near-daily. The 朝礼 blog appeared "almost every day" (2). Indexed post IDs on the post-2018 site reach about 660 by December 2020, consistent with near-daily posting (estimate from search results). Lessons, field trips, a printed-style "school newspaper" (じゃがり校新聞) and monthly themed photo contests (stamps plus Calsapo! points) added to it (12).
  • The co-creation calendar as content. Each project ran in steps over about a year: April–May flavor and concept, June–July package art and pun, August–October character, November–December promotion, then launch (12). Every step was a call for entries plus a vote, followed by a staff post. Calbee shortlisted for allergens and feasibility, sent prototypes to about 500 students, and published their blunt feedback ("too strong", "this ingredient tastes grassy") (6, 4).
  • Member-made content. Senryu, good-deed posts, package drawings, puns, New Year's card art and tips such as "chill the matcha-cream flavor" made up the member side. That chilling tip turned a weak flavor's reception positive on the board within a day (11, 31). Recognition was physical: winning senryu poets got a real certificate, and winners got Jagarico lids and other gifts (2, 33e).
  • No education layer. Unlike app communities, there was nothing to teach; the "lessons" were entertainment and brand lore.

People, programs and governance

  • Staffing. Brand managers doubled as teachers. In FY2013–14 Matsui ran the site alone, apart from system building and outsourced "net patrol" moderation. From 2015 other Jagarico staff took over with his support (11). Brand managers rotated (Matsui from 2011, later Keisuke Tanisawa from April 2020) (10).
  • Student officers. About 9,000 students were too many for staff to manage, so each regional class had one student 役員 (officer) who largely ran its board. Class F's officer was affectionately called "big sis" (11). A class representative also visited Tokyo HQ for events (31).
  • Rules. The school had strict "school rules". Criticizing rival brands was banned and deleted, but criticism of Calbee's own products was allowed (11, 31).
  • Onboarding against churn. In 2015 Calbee added an enrollment guidance session to prevent dropouts and widened what alumni could take part in (6).
  • Feedback into the product. This was direct: shortlists, votes and prototype ratings decided the yearly product, and board posts let individuals' complaints be fixed one by one (12).

Growth loops

  • The package as the recruiter. Exam notices printed on Jagarico cups reached people who were already buying (3), backed by banners on calbee.co.jp from November each year (6).
  • Products as advertising. Each co-created flavor carried copy such as "Jagarico fans' No.1" and got press coverage. Students "buy first and spread it on social media" (3, 6).
  • Trade-press fame. Coverage in Nikkei, AdverTimes and President, an Ambassador Program Award in 2016 alongside Kao, Kirin, Sony Xperia and Nescafé, and academic case studies made it a reference case (28, 5).
  • A capped loop. The design deliberately limited growth to about 3,000 entrants a year, so it was never an acquisition engine. One X user found out the school existed on the same day they learned it was closing (33d).

Evidence of business impact

All of this is company or press claims; I found no published sales figures, retention data or controlled comparisons.

  • Co-created products were "first in sales among the new products launched each year" (AdverTimes yearbook, 2016) (6). Tanisawa (2022) said they "often recorded the year's top sales" among almost-monthly new flavors (10). Toyo Keizai (2017) said they "often" top the year (9).
  • Some co-created limited flavors outsold the 19-year-old core Jagarico during their run (4). Nikkei xTrend's 2015 headline put one at "1.5× the standard" (3).
  • Kuriki names Asparagus Bacon (2014), Mozzarella Tomato (2015) and Honey Butter (2018) as typical "hit products of the year" (5). Asparagus Bacon was re-released in 2025 after a fan vote (24).
  • Research costs fell because no outside research firm was needed, and the answers were of high quality (6).
  • Academic evidence: Hato's peer-reviewed netnography (広告科学 vol. 66, 2019) found that identifying with the community strengthened members' relationship with the brand even when they talked about other things (11). It is qualitative, not causal.
  • Score: business_impact_evidence 2. The claims are consistent and repeated across years, but none are quantified.

What they did right

  1. A metaphor that carried the whole system. Exam, cohort, class, officer, report card, attendance, graduation: one familiar frame supplied status, cadence, onboarding and turnover without extra design (8).
  2. Turnover built in. Three-year enrollment kept the community fresh and the veterans from taking over, while alumni status kept the elders as advisers (8, 4).
  3. Selection by effort, not prizes. The essay exam filtered out sweepstakes hunters; marketers still cited this in 2019 (33f, 5).
  4. Real authority over a real product, every year. Members decided flavor, package art, pun, character and promotion; 13 national launches in 13 years (12, 14).
  5. Letting off-topic chat happen. Calbee didn't force brand talk, and the research suggests the chatter itself built loyalty (11).
  6. Physical recognition. Medals, certificates, calendars and lids made status visible offline (2, 33a).
  7. A gentle ending. Calbee announced the closure about a year ahead, ran a month of farewell posts, and shipped one final co-created product after the site was gone (10, 14).

What they did wrong

  1. It depended on internal champions and was always nearly unfunded. It was "once slated for closure" over cost and ran for years on one manager's time (4, 11). Consequence: each budget review was a threat to its existence, and with no impact metrics published, the case for keeping it always had to be argued again.
  2. The gate wore out the core audience. By 2014 applicants were falling because heavy fans had already enrolled and graduated, and the base skewed toward housewives rather than the young target audience (4). Tanisawa later named "only a limited number of people can take part" as a reason for closing (10).
  3. The archive died with the platform. じゃがり校 ran on the Calsapo! fan-club login. When Calbee shut Calsapo! on 31 March 2021, every post and photo was deleted, points expired, personal data was erased, and newsletter subscribers had to sign up again (16, 37). A member wrote that "with Calsapo! ending, じゃがり校 disappears too" (32); another said she "took damage" twice (33c). Fourteen years of member-made content and class history went with it.
  4. Breadth replaced depth, then Calbee reversed itself. The 2021 Twitter project drew 12,883 ideas, about four times the school's few thousand. But Tanisawa admitted the character limit meant "we couldn't hear why", and there were no tastings (10, 20). I found no evidence of a 2022 or 2023 edition. In October 2023 Calbee opened じゃがりこ探検隊, a test-to-join, capped community that a young marketer pitched explicitly "to revive じゃがり校". It moved again in September 2026 to coorum inside the Ruby Program app (21, 22, 23, 25). Consequence: three platforms in five years, with each restart beginning from zero members.
  5. Web-only and pre-smartphone in design. Calbee's own successor pitch emphasized making the new site "usable in an app", which implies the old site lagged on mobile (22). (This is an inference; I found no direct complaint.)

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Borrow a social structure everyone already knows (school, club, team season), so rules, status and cadence need no explanation.
  2. Run in cohorts with a set end date and an alumni tier. It renews the community every year and gives veterans a dignified role instead of letting them take over.
  3. Gate by effort (a short essay), not by purchase or prizes, if you want contributors rather than freebie hunters.
  4. Give members one consequential, public decision on a yearly calendar, broken into small voteable steps, and ship it with their mark on it.
  5. Let staff appear as named characters who post almost daily, and make recognition physical (medals, certificates, merch).

Avoid these

  1. Don't host the community on a login or points platform you might retire. Plan an export or a migration path; deleting 14 years of member content turns a strategic pivot into a loss for members.
  2. Don't run it on one champion's goodwill without impact metrics. Calbee had claims ("top new flavor") but no published numbers, so it had to fight closure threats each time.
  3. Don't swap depth for reach in one move. Calbee's open social-media replacement multiplied ideas but lost the reasons behind them, and the company rebuilt a gated community within 2.5 years.
