TL;DR

  • What it is: the user forum, knowledge base, academy and conference around RapidMiner, the visual (drag-and-drop) machine-learning tool that began as an open-source project at TU Dortmund. It ran from about 2008 on three platforms (a self-hosted forum, Lithium, then Higher Logic Vanilla). In November 2024 it was folded into the Altair Community, and since 2025 it has sat inside Siemens' support systems.
  • Why people came back: fast, expert answers. Many came from the founder (Ingo Mierswa), core developers and a dozen power users. A tongue-in-cheek rank ladder that ends at "Unicorn" and pays 45 points for an accepted solution, staff-run modelling challenges and a free academic pipeline (free licences, Academy, certification) kept students and analysts posting. Median time to solution fell from 5–10 days to 36 hours after the 2017/18 move to Vanilla, according to the vendor's case study.
  • How the company fed it: registration for the free "Community" edition was tied to forum membership, the Marketplace and in-product "Wisdom of Crowds" recommendations. A dedicated community manager (Thomas Ott, then Scott Genzer from 2017) ran contests and roundups, and staff answered in volume.
  • Biggest mistake: repeated monetisation and ownership shocks with no plan to keep the community going: the 2013 close-sourcing, the 2016 10,000-row limit on the free edition, and above all the 2022–2026 handovers (Altair rebrand, platform merge, account and licence churn, Siemens support migration). New RapidMiner discussions fell about 97% from the 2019 peak to 2026, and most of what remains is licence-error tickets.
  • The one lesson: a product community lasts only as long as its experts. When the founder and staff answerers left or were reassigned, and the space was merged into a parent brand's generic forum, the peer-help loop collapsed within two years.

What the community is

RapidMiner (originally YALE, "Yet Another Learning Environment") was created in 2001 by Ralf Klinkenberg, Ingo Mierswa and Simon Fischer at TU Dortmund. The company Rapid-I was founded in 2006 to develop it (Wikipedia). The community spaces over time:

Origin and strategy

The community grew out of academic open source, so for its first decade the forum was the support channel. Rapid-I staff (Marius Helf, Nils Wöhler, Marco Böck, Ingo Mierswa) wrote over 40% of all comments in 2012 (see table below). Strategy then went through three phases:

  1. Open source → "business source" (2013–2015). RapidMiner 6 (late 2013) closed the latest code: "The source code of RapidMiner v6 is closed source and under copyright of RapidMiner" (forum). TechCrunch described "business source" as letting the community tinker while "the most recent version of the product is reserved for paying customers" (TechCrunch, Feb 2015).
  2. "Get-More-Open-Core" (Sept 2015). Community membership became a product tier. A free Community edition required email registration and unlocked "Wisdom of Crowds recommendations, repository and compute capacity in the cloud" and full use of the forum and Marketplace. Pro started at $1,999/year. The press release called the Community edition "the cornerstone of the Get-More-Open-Core business model" (PRN, Sept 1 2015). This is the clearest example of a B2B vendor wiring community sign-up into its freemium funnel.
  3. Academic flywheel (2016–2022). Free licences for Studio, Server and Radoop for all students and educators (C2C, 2016; K-State, Oct 2016), plus the free Academy and certification exams (edu program). The forum became the help desk for hundreds of university courses. Many 2018–2021 questions start "my teacher wants me to…".

Internal champions: founder and CTO Ingo Mierswa, who posted 266 comments in 2019 alone and personally matched contest prizes. Thomas Ott (data scientist and evangelist, author of the Neural Market Trends blog) ran the community until 2017. He was followed by Scott Genzer, a former teacher and RapidMiner consultant hired as Community Manager in August 2017 (intro post), later Senior Community Manager and now a product manager for Siemens' Rapidminer (Gesher Galicia bio).

After Altair bought RapidMiner in September 2022 for $98.6M in cash (Altair 10-K FY2022), the community became one product section of Altair's engineering-simulation community. Its stated purposes became "convergence", badges and an idea portal (Altair blog, Nov 12 2024). After Siemens closed its ~$10B purchase of Altair in March 2025, support and documentation moved to Siemens systems (support migration thread).

What keeps people coming back

  • Expert, fast answers, often from the people who built the product. The founder, core developers and the community manager answered in public. A 2019 thank-you: "Hi Mr Ingo and Mr Martin, Thanks for the help. What a great community" (thread). This was the main retention driver, and when it went, the rest went too.
  • A rank ladder with an inside joke. Points were 1 per like, 5 per answer, 45 per accepted solution, 10 per moderator promote, and 30 for passing any Academy certification exam. Ranks ran Newbie → Learner I–III → Contributor I–II → Maven → Guru → Unicorn (60 days of membership, 500 points, 60 posts). "In Data Science, a 'Unicorn' is someone who's highly talented… very few and far between" (How to Become a Unicorn, Feb 2019). Genzer told Higher Logic the ranks were "named after various elements that are known in the machine learning world… it's a bit of an inside joke" (case study). Tying forum status to certification connected the learning path to the forum.
  • Staff-run challenges. In May 2017 Genzer self-funded a $100 "Kaggle-like" modelling challenge, and Mierswa matched it with an Amazon gift card (thread). Later the community entered DrivenData's World Bank "Pover-T" contest as a team, pledging the $15,000 prize to charity (thread). Participation was thin: an earlier challenge drew three entries.
  • Belonging and rituals. Members were addressed as "RapidMiners". There were weekly and monthly "Community Link Roundups" (example, June 2017), Spanish- and Japanese-language subforums (Spanish, 2018), playful threads like "RapidMiner Chess Unicorns" (Dec 2020), and the annual Wisdom conference, which in 2016 had theCUBE interviewing customers like Siemens (YouTube).
  • In-product hooks and switching costs. Community login gated the Marketplace and the "Wisdom of Crowds" operator recommender, which "culls insights… from the millions of models created by members of the RapidMiner community" (Arnold, Nov 2014; KDD 2016 paper). Every free user therefore had a community account, and the product's in-app help pointed them to the forum (design-view docs). Process files (.rmp) shared in threads made the forum a library of reusable recipes.
  • Academic cohorts. Free licences and certification brought a steady stream of students every semester. They kept the volume up but left once the course ended.

The content engine

  • RapidMiner Academy: free, self-paced video courses and learning paths, with Professional and Master certifications. Badges were issued as Open Badges (self-hosted until June 2023, then via Open Badge Factory) (Academy; badge post). Altair later moved training to Altair Learning (learn.altair.com).
  • Knowledge base: 413 staff and member KB articles on the forum (API).
  • Webinars, YouTube and livestreams: staff webinars (e.g. "An Introduction to Deep Learning", 2017) and Thomas Ott's "Learn RapidMiner Livestream" series (2018) (Neural Market Trends). The YouTube channel is now "Altair RapidMiner How-To" with a Siemens banner (YouTube).
  • Member-made content: Marketplace extensions (e.g. the staff and community "Operator Toolbox" and an "Interpretations" extension announced in the forum (mirror home)); shared processes and "Building Blocks"; a books and consultant ecosystem (Andrew Chisholm's RapidMinerResources is quoted in the 2015 release). Under Altair, the "Altair Exchange" for scripts and workflows has 2,279 posts across all Altair products.
  • Cadence: no fixed weekly format was found beyond the roundups. The engine depended on the people involved and slowed whenever staff time was cut.

People, programs and governance

  • Team: usually one community manager (Thomas Ott until 2017, then Scott Genzer as Senior Community Manager), plus developers and data scientists answering as part of their jobs (Martin Schmitz/MartinLiebig, Marco Böck, Ingo Mierswa). Moderators deleted off-topic or abusive replies, which one user publicly resented (see Member voice).
  • Superusers: there was no formal MVP programme until Altair's. "Unicorn" was a computed rank, not a selected cohort. Partner consultants (Telcontar120, BalazsBarany, rfuentealba) used the forum as their storefront.
  • Feedback loop: "Product Ideas" (46 discussions) and "Product Feedback – Resolved" (242), a small ideas channel. Altair promised a voting "idea portal" in Nov 2024. No evidence that it launched.
  • Altair era: a generic points and badges system (5 points per answer, 10 per accepted answer, badges for profile photos and anniversaries) (rewards post). In August 2025 came "Altair RISE", a six-month, invitation or nomination programme with four reviewed ranks and an invite to product user groups (RISE). By 2026 members were being pointed to the Siemens Community Catalyst programme (Siemens). No RapidMiner-specific superuser appears in 2026 leaderboards.

Growth loops

  1. Registration-gated freemium: downloading and using the full free edition and the Marketplace required a community account, which explains the 160k → 250k → "1M" figures.
  2. Academic seeding: free licences, curricula and certification turned students into future enterprise users, and the forum handled their support.
  3. SEO: about 18k solved threads ranked for "how do I X in RapidMiner" queries. They still show up in Brave results, now under community.altair.com URLs.
  4. Vote mobilisation: RapidMiner topped KDnuggets' software polls in 2013–2014 by rallying its community. KDnuggets noted that "RapidMiner was especially successful" in 2014 and in 2018 wrote that "only one vendor – RapidMiner – had a very active campaign to vote" (2014; 2018). It worked for marketing ("RapidMiner Voted Most Used Analytics Software" (PR, 2013)), but it damaged the poll's credibility.
  5. Conference: Wisdom drew customers, partners and academics and generated press (theCUBE, sponsor posts).

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard-ish: the only metric found is from the forum vendor: median time to solution fell from 5–10 days to 36 hours after the migration to Vanilla (Higher Logic). It is a vendor case study, with no baseline method published.
  • Company claims: the Community edition as "cornerstone" of the business model (2015). Gartner Magic Quadrant Leader placements (2017–2019) were marketed alongside "a community of over 200,000 users" (PRN 2017).
  • Valuation context: Altair paid $98.6M in cash in 2022 for a company whose public story leaned on "more than one million" users. No filing or call quantifies community value. EDGAR full-text hits for "RapidMiner" plus "community" in Altair filings are routine acquisition disclosures.
  • No evidence found of community-linked retention, churn or NPS figures, or academic studies of this community.

What they did right

  1. Founders and engineers answered in public. In 2012 staff wrote about 40% of comments, and in 2019 the founder still wrote 266. Fast, authoritative answers drove "great community" thank-yous and solved-rates of about 54% (2019).
  2. Community membership was a product tier. Get-More-Open-Core (2015) gave registered members real features (cloud repository, recommendations, Marketplace). Registration was what made the community large.
  3. Gamification that fit the users. The ML-themed ranks, heavy weighting on accepted solutions (45 points against 1 per like) and points for certification rewarded the right behaviour. A transparent public formula ("no secrets here") built trust.
  4. One owner who was also a user. Hiring a long-time customer and consultant (Genzer) as community manager produced credible, playful initiatives (maple-syrup prizes, charity teams) and a 2017–2019 activity rebound.
  5. Clean platform migration (2017/18). About 40k posts moved "in a matter of weeks" with IDs preserved, and activity rose the following year (case study).
  6. Academic pipeline. Free licences, the Academy and certification created a renewable supply of new askers and some future answerers (varunm1, a 2019 top answerer, posted as an academic).

What they did wrong

  1. Moving the licence goalposts. Closing v6 (2013) was followed by the 2014–2015 activity trough: discussions fell 40% from 2013. Then the 10,000-row cap on the free edition in v7 (Aug 2016) drew "this is so nonsense" threads, deleted replies and a staff answer telling users they could stay on old versions (thread). Confusion about "business source" vs "open core" persisted into 2017 (thread).
  2. Gaming external polls. Mobilising members to win KDnuggets polls earned public criticism from the poll's editor (2014, 2018). It was a short-term marketing win that cost reputation.
  3. Contests nobody entered. "Unfortunately those were the ONLY submissions" (Jan 2018). The challenges stayed ad hoc and never became a recurring ritual.
  4. Expert dependence, then expert loss. Activity fell 42% between 2020 and 2021, before the acquisition: Wisdom went virtual, then stopped, and comment volume halved. After Altair, a few staff carried the forum. By 2025, 25% of RapidMiner questions got no reply.
  5. Merging into a parent brand's generic forum (Nov 2024). RapidMiner became a tag inside an engineering-simulation community dominated by HyperWorks and Feko. Ranks reset to generic Altair badges ("upload a profile picture: 10 points"), the Unicorn identity disappeared, and some old thread IDs now resolve to unrelated Altair posts, which breaks SEO and bookmarks.
  6. Account and licensing churn landed on the forum. The RapidMiner account, Altair One, Altair Units and then Siemens IDs produced repeated "account blocked" and "No valid RapidMiner license found" threads (2025; 2026). In 2026, 59% of RapidMiner threads are of this kind.
  7. Second migration without a destination. In early 2026 Siemens copied forum threads into its Support Center as static KB articles ("no longer maintained… may be outdated") and shut the Altair support portal on March 9, while saying the community itself would move "later". Members were left in a waiting room (roundup; migration thread).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make joining the community unlock real product value (cloud storage, recommendations, extensions), so every free user has an account and a reason to log in.
  2. Have builders answer in public, and track time to solution as the community's main KPI, as RapidMiner did (5–10 days → 36 hours).
  3. Weight rewards toward solved problems (45 points for a solution against 1 for a like) and give the top rank a name with in-group meaning (Unicorn). Publish the formula.
  4. Connect learning and the forum: free academy, certification exams that award forum points, and badges members can post on LinkedIn.
  5. Hire the community manager from your power users, and let them run small, playful, recurring challenges with founder backing.

Avoid these

  1. Changing the free tier without bringing the community along. Every licensing change (2013 close-source, 2016 row cap) showed up as anger in the forum and a dip in activity. Explain changes first and give grandfathering paths.
  2. Folding a niche community into a parent brand's generic forum. Merging brands, resetting ranks and breaking URLs removed the identity and the SEO equity that brought people in. Keep the space, the ranks and the URLs, and redirect properly.
  3. Letting a few experts carry everything. When staff were reassigned and the conference ended, there was no cultivated volunteer bench to take over, and the help loop went from 54% solved to 9% solved within six years.
