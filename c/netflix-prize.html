TL;DR

  • What it was: an open contest (2 Oct 2006 to 26 Jul 2009) to beat Netflix's Cinematch recommender by 10% on RMSE, built on a 100M-rating dataset. It had three community spaces: a public leaderboard, an owned PunBB forum, and in-person workshops. By the end it had 51,051 contestants on 41,305 teams from 186 countries (leaderboard, Aug 2009).
  • Why people came back: a scoreboard you could move once a day; a hard, unprecedented dataset; annual $50K Progress Prizes that reset the goal; and a rule forcing every prize winner to publish their method. That rule turned rivals into collaborators, then into merged "super-teams." The contest was decided by 20 minutes after almost three years.
  • What Netflix did to feed it: surprisingly little staffing. One "prizemaster" admin account wrote about 181 posts in three years. Netflix designed the incentives (quiz/test split, probe set, last call, publication rule) so the members produced the content, and it put the contest in front of researchers at KDD 2007.
  • Biggest mistake: privacy. Researchers showed the "anonymized" data could be re-identified (Narayanan & Shmatikov, arXiv Oct 2006). A class action (Doe v. Netflix, Dec 2009) and FTC questions followed, and Netflix cancelled the already-announced Prize 2 on 12 Mar 2010, ending the community.
  • The one lesson: a co-creation community holds together when the rules make sharing the winning strategy (publish-to-win, merge-to-win, daily scoring), not when staff try to hand-feed engagement. Treat member data as a liability before you hand it to a crowd.

What the community is

The Netflix Prize was a company-sponsored open-innovation contest rather than a customer community. Participants did not have to be Netflix subscribers, and most were researchers, students and hobbyist programmers. Netflix released a training set of 100,480,507 ratings that 480,189 users gave to 17,770 movies. It held back about 2.8M ratings as a "qualifying" set, split into a public quiz half (shown on the leaderboard) and a secret test half (used to pick winners) (Wikipedia; Bennett & Lanning, KDD Cup 2007). Prizes: $1M Grand Prize for a 10% RMSE improvement over Cinematch (0.9525 → 0.8572 on test), and $50K annual Progress Prizes for at least 1% improvement over the previous year (Rules via Wayback).

Spaces (all official, all on netflixprize.com):

  • Leaderboard: the public scoreboard and the main engagement surface. Teams could submit once a day (originally once a week) (FAQ; Wikipedia).
  • Netflix Prize Forum (netflixprize.com/community): a PunBB board with categories for Administrivia, Awarded Prizes, General Discussion, Ratings Dataset ("About the movies"), Algorithms ("Reading and literature references," "Learning or otherwise," "Visualization") and "Looking for Team Partners" ("A dating service? Not quite..."). Anyone could read it; posting needed a separate registration, and you did not have to be competing (FAQ).
  • IRL: the KDD Cup & Workshop 2007 (San Jose, 12 Aug 2007), where the four leading teams presented (Wikipedia; ACM). Then the Grand Prize ceremony in New York on 21 Sep 2009 (forum announcement).
  • Unofficial: team websites (BellKor at AT&T, the-ensemble.com, Commendo), personal blogs (Simon Funk's Dec 2006 SVD write-up), and Hacker News threads. After the contest a member tried to start a Facebook group and a Yahoo group to carry the community on (forum topic list, Jan 2010). No evidence either took off. No meaningful subreddit existed. r/MachineLearning threads about the Prize are sparse and retrospective (Arctic Shift search, 2011–2019).

Status today: defunct. The forum went read-only after the award. Prize 2 was cancelled on 12 Mar 2010 (Neil Hunt, Netflix blog via Wayback). From this machine, http://netflixprize.com now redirects to netflix.com (checked 2026-11-06), though search engines still index a static copy of some pages.

Origin and strategy

  • Business problem. In the DVD era, "the length of service of subscribers is related to the number of movies they watch and enjoy. If subscribers fail to find movies that interest and engage them, they tend to abandon the service" (Bennett & Lanning 2007). So recommendations were a retention lever. Cinematch had plateaued by 2006 (Wikipedia).
  • Champion. CEO Reed Hastings. HBS's case says that hiring and training were "time intensive and costly," so "Hastings decided to improve Netflix's software by crowdsourcing" (HBS case 47650). Netflix's recommendation-systems staff (James Bennett and Stan Lanning wrote the official paper) ran it through the "prizemaster" account ("From: Netflix HQ", registered 2006-08-29) (forum).
  • Design as strategy. The FAQ spells out the community-design reasoning: once-a-day submissions "to dissuade you from just training on the scoring oracle"; the quiz/test split so the board shows progress without letting teams overfit; a probe set to cut oracle calls; 100M ratings because that "fit in memory for most machines"; and Progress Prize money set at "5% yearly interest on $1,000,000" (FAQ). To collect any prize, a team had to give Netflix its source code and a non-exclusive license and "describe to the world how you did it and why it works" (Rules).
  • Business tie. R&D on the core retention engine, at a fixed price that was paid only for success. Netflix later added brand awareness and recruiting as explicit returns (see below).

What the numbers say:

  • Registration kept growing; activity was front-loaded. 43% of all forum posts (3,728 of 8,705) were written in the first three months, Oct–Dec 2006. Daily submissions, a noisy one-day reading, were in the 60–80s through 2007–mid-2008 and fell to the 20s–40s in 2009 as the race narrowed to a few mega-teams. Bennett & Lanning saw progress stall near 7% by Feb 2007 before a fresh surge (paper).
  • A small active core. Only 12.5% of registered teams (5,169 of 41,305) ever made a valid submission. Forum registrations reached roughly 10% of contestants. The community was a broad audience around a few hundred serious teams.
  • Prize moments brought new members. Forum registrations doubled in 2008 (1,698 → 3,520) after the 2007 Progress Prize papers and press coverage such as Wired's "Just a guy in a garage" profile (Wired, Feb 2008) and Clive Thompson's NYT Magazine feature (Nov 2008).

What keeps people coming back

  1. A scoreboard that moves daily, with a clear finish line. The once-a-day cap made submitting a daily ritual and stopped brute-forcing. The quiz score gave instant feedback while the hidden test score kept the ending in doubt (FAQ). Lester Mackey (Dinosaur Planet, Princeton undergrads): "In the first few months we managed to do better than some of the baselines… That was when we really got excited" (Thrillist, 2017).
  2. Seasons with resets. Annual Progress Prizes, each with a 30-day "last call," created yearly climaxes: Sept–Oct 2007 (KorBell won 8.43% on 13 Nov 2007) and 2008 (BellKor in BigChaos, 9.44%). Each raised the bar 1% (Wikipedia). Crossing 10% on 26 Jun 2009 started a 30-day sprint, which ended in a tie broken by submission time: "That 20 minutes was worth a million dollars" (Hastings, via Thrillist). The winners' test RMSEs were 0.856704 vs 0.856714 (Bell, Koren & Volinsky, CHANCE 2010).
  3. Publish-to-win made openness the dominant strategy. Because winners had to publish, the frontier was public each year, and anyone could build on it. Early on, Simon Funk (Brandyn Webb) simply posted his incremental-SVD method; Netflix itself called it "a notable event" that "helped advance the pursuit of the SVD approaches" (Bennett & Lanning). Vincent DiCarlo: "Everybody adopted Simon's ideas and then used them to come up with new ideas" (Thrillist).
  4. Blending turned competitors into teammates. The forum found early that blending different models gave a "mysterious improvement" (Thrillist). So the winning move was to merge: BellKor + BigChaos (2008), + Pragmatic Theory (2009). The Ensemble "included members from 23 original teams" (CHANCE 2010). The "Looking for Team Partners" board was the in-house matchmaking channel (forum).
  5. Status and identity. Leaderboard rank was public, career-relevant credibility ("a good showing (top 20) on Netflix… making yourself 'bankable'", member edr2, forum, Jul 2009). Team names became an inside joke (Dinosaur Planet, Just a guy in a garage, Lanterne Rouge, who deliberately went for the worst score) (Wikipedia; "Wooden spoon for worst RMSE" thread, 23 replies, 15.5K views, forum).
  6. Learning as the real reward for the long tail. Most contestants knew they couldn't win and stayed for the education: "a real education in machine learning… a lot of the leaders have been really good about sharing information" (edr2, forum, Jul 2009).
  7. Drama. Volinsky: "There was a fair amount of intrigue and backroom dealing… who was working with who" (Thrillist). Mysterious entrants who suddenly jumped up the board became stories in their own right (Wired 2008).

No evidence of badges, streaks, newsletters or notification hooks. The engagement design lived almost entirely in the contest rules.

The content engine

What Netflix produced (thin, but well made):

  • The dataset and README, plus a format-checking script and MD5s.
  • A FAQ written in a frank, playful voice that explained the reasoning behind each design decision ("if you still want to look under a streetlight...", "Think days. We're talking serious horsepower here") (FAQ).
  • Forum announcements from prizemaster: about 181 posts over three years (forum).
  • An academic status report at KDD 2007, co-hosting KDD Cup 2007 on the same data (ACM).
  • The ceremony, with a promised presentation video (forum).

What members produced (the real engine):

  • The rules required winners' papers: BellKor 2007, BellKor + BigChaos 2008, and three Grand Prize papers in 2009 (forum).
  • Blog tutorials, starting with Funk's (Dec 2006).
  • Open-source implementations "in Python, Java, C, and C++", linked from the forum (HN, Jun 2009).
  • Literature-reference threads.

Netflix curated nothing beyond pinning announcements. It got curation for free by making publication the price of the prize. After the contest the forum archive itself became the reference: "a treasure trove… the most interesting discoveries were made in the first year" (HN, 2014).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: effectively one admin persona (prizemaster), backed by Netflix's recommendation team and judges who verified submissions. No evidence of moderators beyond that, of community managers, or of an ambassador or superuser program. staff_investment = 2: Netflix spent heavily on prize money and design, very little on running the community.
  • Rules: strict and legalistic on eligibility and IP (non-exclusive license; winners keep ownership, which let AT&T researchers justify company time, per Thrillist), and liberal on everything else (any outside data allowed).
  • Feedback into product: formal and one-way. Netflix took the code. From the 2007 Progress Prize ensemble of 107 algorithms (2,000+ hours of work), it put matrix factorization (SVD) and RBMs into production, where they were "still used" in 2012 (Netflix TechBlog, Apr 2012).
  • Fairness disputes were settled by prizemaster in forum replies. That caused at least one grievance: an extra KDD dataset was allowed but never broadcast to all teams (forum, Sep 2009).

Growth loops

  • Press as acquisition. The launch got a NYT story the same day ("And if You Liked the Movie, a Netflix Contest May Reward You Handsomely," 2 Oct 2006), and the contest drew about 17K contestants by mid-December. Each milestone (progress prizes, the 10% crossing, the photo finish) produced a new wave of coverage (Wired 2009, NBC/AP 2009) and a jump in registrations (table above).
  • Academic channels. KDD workshops, papers citing the dataset, course reading lists (e.g. UMich SI 583, 2009) and O'Reilly's Programming Collective Intelligence (HN recollection) brought in students.
  • Team merging was itself a loop: each merger pulled whole teams' networks into the leading coalitions.
  • No referral program. Growth was entirely earned media and word of mouth among researchers.

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence:

  • Cost: a $1M expense in Q3 2009 under technology and development (Netflix 10-Q, Oct 2009), plus two $50K Progress Prizes, which the winners gave to charity (Wikipedia). About $1.1M in total.
  • Production use: the first-year SVD and RBM went into production. A linear blend of the two scored 0.88 RMSE versus Cinematch's 0.9525. The Grand Prize ensemble was not deployed: "the additional accuracy gains… did not seem to justify the engineering effort" (TechBlog, Apr 2012; Wired, Apr 2012).
  • Legal cost: a class action under the Video Privacy Protection Act (Case C09-05903, filed 17 Dec 2009), settled, and voluntarily dismissed on 19 Mar 2010 (10-Q, Apr 2010). Plus FTC conditions on future research data use (Neil Hunt).

Company claims:

  • "You look at the cumulative hours and you're getting Ph.D.'s for a dollar an hour" (Hastings, NYT, 22 Sep 2009).
  • "The $1M Prize delivered a great return on investment for us, not only in algorithmic innovation, but also in brand awareness and attracting stars… to join our team" (TechBlog Part 2, Jun 2012).
  • 75% of viewing comes from recommendations (TechBlog Part 1). That is a claim about the recommender as a whole, not the Prize.

Not found: any retention or churn figure attributed to the Prize, or a count of hires who came from it. business_impact_evidence = 3: filings confirm the cost and the legal fallout, Netflix confirms production use, and ROI is asserted rather than measured.

What they did right

  1. They designed the incentives instead of staffing engagement. The daily cap, quiz/test split, probe set and last call made a fair, gripping game with almost no staff (FAQ). The result was 51K contestants and a three-year run.
  2. Mandatory publication. It made Netflix's community a shared research commons and kept the leaders from hoarding, which brought newcomers in and pushed progress (Rules; CHANCE 2010: "many of the top teams (including ours) had little or no experience in collaborative filtering when they started").
  3. Escalating seasons. Progress Prizes broke an open-ended goal into yearly arcs, each with a public climax and a published solution.
  4. An unmatched asset as the draw. "It was the data set… that had number-crunching nuts salivating. There was nothing like it at the time" (Thrillist).
  5. IP terms that let employers say yes. Winners kept ownership and Netflix got only a non-exclusive license, so corporate labs (AT&T, Commendo, Opera Solutions) could commit staff (Thrillist).
  6. A human voice at the end. The cancellation was signed by a named executive. HN noticed: "A person has chosen to identify himself with the decision" (HN, Mar 2010).

What they did wrong

  1. Under-protected member data, the fatal error. Rating dates plus a few public IMDb ratings were enough to re-identify people. The re-identification paper came out within weeks of launch (arXiv cs/0610105; IEEE S&P 2008), yet Netflix announced a sequel with more demographic data (ages, ZIP codes, genders; Wikipedia). The result: Doe v. Netflix, brought by a closeted lesbian mother (Wired, Dec 2009), FTC scrutiny, and the sequel cancelled (NYT, Mar 2010). The community lost its next season and simply ended.
  2. Optimized a proxy the business then left behind. RMSE on DVD star ratings mattered less once streaming produced viewing data. The grand-prize algorithm was shelved, and critics called the contest a "failure" (Wired 2012). The FAQ had warned that RMSE "doesn't deal with… what order recommendations should be made."
  3. Late-stage concentration. Kitchen-sink ensembles ("blending hundreds of predictive models") and mega-mergers pushed small teams out. Daily submissions roughly halved from 2008 to 2009. A Kaggler later observed that contest winners optimize for winning, "not create a solution that could be deployed" (HN, 2013).
  4. Uneven information in the forum. Material rulings, such as the KDD data being allowed, were made in forum replies rather than broadcast to all teams, and that left some members calling the outcome unfair (forum, Sep 2009).
  5. Orphaned the community. Members begged for the archive to be kept ("I'd like to see the whole thing archived for posterity"). Prizemaster promised the forum would "remain open for the foreseeable future" (forum, Jun 2009). It went read-only, and the domain now redirects to netflix.com. Netflix told Thrillist in 2017 it "isn't particularly interested in reliving the past glories" (Thrillist).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Put the engagement in the rules. A visible score, a rate limit that creates a daily habit, and a hidden final judge did more than any staff could have. Ask what the "submission" and the "leaderboard" are in your own product.
  2. Make sharing the price of winning. Require top contributors to publish how they did it (templates, guides, code). That turns rivalry into a commons and pulls newcomers up the curve.
  3. Run seasons, not open-ended campaigns. Annual checkpoints with a last-call sprint, a public climax and a published recap keep a multi-year effort alive.
  4. Lead with an asset nobody else offers. Here it was data at a scale nobody else had released. The money got attention; the asset kept people working.
  5. Design for team formation. A partner-finding board, plus rules that allow and reward merging, lets solo contributors become coalitions, and coalitions are what keep people around.

Avoid these

  1. Don't hand members' customer data to a crowd without adversarial privacy review. One re-identification paper turned into a lawsuit and FTC scrutiny, and ended the program.
  2. Don't let the contest metric drift from the business metric. Check every year that what the community optimizes still matches what the product needs, or you'll pay for work you shelve.
  3. Don't orphan the archive or make rulings in side channels. Broadcast rule decisions to everyone, keep the community's archive alive, and plan the sunset with the members.
