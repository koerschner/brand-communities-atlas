Cambrian House

Cambrian House (cambrianhouse.com, IdeaWarz) · The Cambrian House Inc. (Calgary, Alberta)

The loudest crowdsourcing community of 2006 to 2008 attracted tens of thousands of idea people but almost nobody to build, and folded within two years

Peak

About 42,000 members, 6,200+ ideas and 500+ crowdsourced projects (Nov 2007, Crunchbase company profile); 6,935 ideas by May 2008 (TechCrunch); Wikipedia later claims 50,000+ members and 7,000+ ideas

Now

Gone. The community was folded into VenCorps in May 2008 and the old site was to 'dissolve' in three months; VenCorps is listed as permanently closed. The platform lived on as Chaordix, which ran LEGO Ideas from 2014.

How it pays off for the company

Planned: the crowd would supply ideas, market tests, code and art for free, paid later in royalty points (a share of product profits), while Cambrian House sold the products as a 'mutual fund for web businesses'. Actual: total royalty payouts of about $800 by Dec 2006 and a biggest single payout of $400; no product reached real scale. The lasting money came from licensing the community software itself (Chaordix).

TL;DR

  • What it is: a Calgary startup (public beta 27 to 28 June 2006) that tried to crowdsource whole software businesses: members submitted ideas, voted on them in "IdeaWarz" tournaments, then built the winners for "royalty points" convertible into a share of profits. It called itself the "Home of Crowdsourcing" and grew from about 8,500 members (Dec 2006) to 42,000 (Nov 2007) (Globe and Mail; Crunchbase).
  • Why people came back: contest rhythm (weekly and monthly tournaments with a winner, C$100 prizes, celebrity-named brackets), the lottery-ticket promise of royalties, member ownership (a co-op share for every member, an elected council), and lots of staff attention: about 40 employees for a community of thousands.
  • What the company fed it: a chatty staff blog, videos, swag, stunts (1,000 pizzas delivered to Google), a member currency (Cambros, 1 = US$1) and a marketplace (the Bazaar), and frequent rule changes to make voting "care".
  • Biggest mistake: it optimised for ideas, which were plentiful and cheap, and never solved for builders. The CEO's own post-mortem: "we became a destination people loved to bookmark more than they loved to actively visit… The limiting reagent in the startup equation is not ideas, but amazing founding teams" (TechCrunch, 12 May 2008).
  • The one lesson: a co-creation community only retains people if contributions regularly turn into something shipped. Voting and ideation are easy engagement, but without a reliable path from idea to product (staffed by the company or by vetted teams), the members' reward stays hypothetical and activity decays to bookmarks.

What the community is

Cambrian House was both the company and its community site, cambrianhouse.com. A stealth teaser (a Flash puzzle at cambriancode.com) ran in spring 2006, and the public beta opened at the end of June 2006 (push.cx, 1 Jun 2006; push.cx, 27 Jun 2006; Mashable, 12 Jul 2006). The tagline: "You think it, crowds test it, crowds build it, we sell it, you profit" (Globe and Mail, 17 Dec 2006).

Official spaces (all company-run):

  • Ideas section and IdeaWarz: idea submission, comments, and head-to-head voting; IdeaWarz also had its own site, ideawarz.com.
  • Projects ("branches"): teams formed around winning ideas, with forums, discussion groups, PMs and a development collaboration area branded "NorseForge" (the brand used Viking imagery) (Wayback homepage, Dec 2006).
  • Bazaar (from May 2007): a marketplace where members bought and sold ideas, businesses and jobs, paid in Cambros (CH blog, 18 May 2007).
  • Governance: an elected Community Council (from autumn 2006) and the Cambrian House Co-op (from April 2007).
  • Blog, videos and podcast, plus offline meetups (drinks in Silicon Valley, BarCamp Calgary, a Don Tapscott talk).

Unofficial spaces: no fan subreddit or independent forum turned up; member discussion outside the site appears mostly as Hacker News comments (2007 to 2011), where members pointed others to Cambrian House as the place to post startup ideas (HN search).

Status: defunct. In May 2008 the community was redirected to VenCorps, a crowd-picked venture fund with Spencer Trask; Cambrian House Inc. became a holding company whose main asset was the Chaordix crowdsourcing platform (CH blog, 19 May 2008; mathewingram.com, 13 May 2008).

Origin and strategy

Co-founder and CEO Michael "MJ" Sikorsky, a serial software entrepreneur, built Cambrian House after selling his previous company. Open-source collaboration inspired him, and he believed collaboration was a way of "reducing risk and increasing capital efficiency" during a startup's early stages. He pitched the company as a "mutual fund" for web businesses (Globe and Mail). Jeff Howe's June 2006 Wired article "The Rise of Crowdsourcing" gave the idea its name, and the site leaned on the term heavily, branding itself "Home of Crowdsourcing" by mid-2007 (Wayback homepage, Jun 2007).

The community was the business model, not a support or retention layer:

  • R&D and market research: the crowd generated and filtered ideas. Staff first screened submissions for mass-market web software, then IdeaWarz ranked them, then a "market test" with prototypes and pre-orders followed (Globe and Mail).
  • Labour: members contributed code, art and copy for royalty points, not wages.
  • Revenue: Cambrian House sold the resulting products and took "50% off the top for project management, sales and marketing" (Canadian Business, search snippet; page blocked).

Funding: US$2.6M in early 2006 and a second round of about $5.3M closing in December 2006 (Globe and Mail); $7.75M invested in total by May 2008 (TechCrunch). Shelley Kuipers joined as President in February 2007 and later became CEO of the Chaordix business (PR.com, 9 Apr 2009). Harvard Business School wrote it up as a teaching case on community-led product development and on whether to add a prediction market (HBS case 608-016, Mar 2008).

Trajectory: very fast sign-up growth through 2006 and 2007, driven by press and stunts. Engagement did not keep pace. Sikorsky said traffic was "scarily VC-ish" (a spike, then a fade) and the site became a bookmark rather than a daily destination (TechCrunch). The gap between Howe's "some 5,000" and the 42,000 registered a few months later suggests that most sign-ups were inactive (an inference, not a reported metric). No DAU/MAU data was published.

What keeps people coming back

  1. Contest cadence (IdeaWarz). The first version was an open-ended pairwise battle among all ideas. In autumn 2006 staff admitted it had "no finality" and moved to time-boxed tournaments explicitly to add "rhythm and ritual" and make voters "care" about outcomes, citing Threadless (CH blog, archived Oct 2006). By December 2006 that meant 16-idea brackets halving weekly (Globe and Mail). Then came monthly tournaments named after celebrities ("Reid Hoffman", "Oracle of Omaha", "Friendly Giant" winners on the blog, 2007). Finally a weekly vote opened every Wednesday at noon, with a C$100 Cambro prize, plus a quarterly "Tournament of Champions" pitched as American Idol, with expert wildcards (CH blog, archived Mar 2008). This was the strongest habit loop on the site.
  2. Lottery-ticket economics. Each idea started with 1,500 royalty points, up to 150 for the idea author, and code or creative work earned 5 to 90 points per contribution. A calculator promised that a well-performing idea could pay "$153,600 over 3 years" (Mashable). Royalties were typically 10% of gross profit, shared among contributors (Globe and Mail).
  3. Status and recognition. "Glory points" rewarded community-helping work that would not earn revenue; badges and awards appeared on profiles (beta user #9 got a "Peter will always be #9 to us" award) (push.cx). The blog featured weekly winners, profiled members and posted photos of members in IdeaWarz t-shirts.
  4. Ownership and belonging. From April 2007 every member could claim a share in the Cambrian House Co-op, with a vote and "patronage dividends" scaled by glory points. The pitch was "How many community members benefited when Flickr was bought? Zero" (Get your stock page). A member reported that the co-op owned 1% of the company and was run by 2 employees and 3 members (HN, 28 Jul 2007). It mirrored the elected council of 2 staff and 3 members, created in September 2006 to mediate grievances (CH blog, 30 Sep 2006).
  5. Staff presence. One member noticed that "if nobody else cares to comment on your ideas, they might even throw in their own employees to do so" (HN, 20 Sep 2007). With about 40 staff, the ratio of staff to active members was unusually high.
  6. Member economy. Cambros (bought through PayPal, 1 = US$1) let idea owners pay team members directly, and the Bazaar let them trade ideas and services (CH blog, 18 May 2007).

Missing: any shipped product that members used every day. Prezzle and the Robinhood Fund produced small revenue, and Gwabs (a desktop fighting game built with Hothead Games) was still "working away" in late 2008 (Wayback homepage, Dec 2008). Switching costs were close to nil: a member's ideas could be posted anywhere.

The content engine

  • Staff blog with three streams: "Buzz" (press and stunts), "Community" (site updates, apologies, member features) and "IdeaWarz" (winner announcements, "5 tips on pitching your idea for an IdeaWarz tournament"). It ran several posts a week in 2006 and 2007 (CDX listing of cambrianhouse.com/blog). The tone was candid and playful, including "Website update report" posts and a public apology plus free "I got spammed" t-shirts after an email bug flooded members (CH blog, 8 Sep 2006).
  • Video and podcast: a company video page that an HN commenter rated among the best startup videos (HN, 28 Feb 2008), a "Primordial Soup" podcast, and a staff-made 48-hour sci-fi short (CH blog, 29 Apr 2008).
  • Stunts as acquisition content: "Feeding Google", a multi-part saga that ended with 1,000 pizzas delivered to Google unannounced; a charity laptop auction (FollowTheWhiteLaptop); and a goat-weighing contest (Wikipedia; HN, 23 May 2007).
  • Thought leadership: posts arguing that crowdsourcing rewards "should be equal to the value that you add", with three rules: transparency, staff passion (employees "spend every minute possible in your community") and time (CH blog, 6 Feb 2007).

There was no education layer for the hard part (how to form a team, scope an MVP or ship). Member-made content was the ideas themselves. Members promoted their ideas on their own blogs (crowdsource.wordpress.com, 12 Sep 2006), and the company paid glory points for widgets on members' sites.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: about 40 in December 2006, with a VP of Communications (Jasmine Antonick) and community-facing developers (Globe and Mail; GamesIndustry.biz, 1 Feb 2007). An early employee recalls Calgary dev-ops chaos (push.cx, Oct 2006) and a "flintstoning" culture of doing things manually first (push.cx, Jul 2006).
  • Advisors: Sean Wise, a venture consultant from Dragons' Den, joined the board of advisors in 2007 and later ran VenCorps (HN, 8 Aug 2007; CNET, 4 Jun 2008).
  • Council and co-op: as above; members elected for terms of about six months.
  • Gatekeeping: staff pre-screened ideas against mass-market criteria, and "wildcard" experts were added to tournaments.
  • Feedback loop: frequent public "what's wrong with IdeaWarz" posts asked for member input. But one long-time member says the site never improved "like the community wanted" while the company "were always hiring" (HN, 14 May 2008).

Growth loops

  • Press and buzz: Wired/Jeff Howe, the Globe and Mail, CBC, the Financial Times, Mashable, Digg front pages ("We've been Digged" posts) and conference panels (Mesh, Web 2.0 Expo). The company claimed its daily reach broke Canada's top 100 sites in July 2006 (blog post title in the CDX index; page unreachable).
  • Idea-promotion sharing: owners were told to "promote your idea" on Facebook, by email and on blogs to win votes (CH blog). This was a built-in viral loop, the same one Threadless used.
  • Ownership as a recruiting hook: "Join. It's free. You even get stock" (homepage, Jun 2007).
  • SEO: thousands of public idea pages were still being linked from HN years later as a "Snopes for ideas" (HN, 27 Jul 2011).

These loops brought in idea submitters and voters, not developers, which was the population the model needed.

Evidence of business impact

Hard evidence: weak and negative.

  • Royalty payouts were tiny: about $800 in total by December 2006 and $400 as the biggest single payout (Globe and Mail; Canadian Business).
  • "A year after my visit, Cambrian House had failed to bring a single product to market" (Jeff Howe, Slate, 2012). That is too strong: Prezzle and Robinhood Fund were earning in 2006 and Prezzle was still paying a member in December 2008 (HN). No product made meaningful revenue.
  • $7.75M was raised, and the May 2008 asset sale was reported (and disputed by the CEO) as a fraction of that (TechCrunch). Employees who had invested lost their money, according to a former employee (HN, 13 May 2008).
  • Spin-offs with a crowd origin: Mob4Hire (crowdsourced mobile testing) was proposed on Cambrian House and initially built by members (BrainStation/Techvibes, Jan 2009). GreedyorNeedy went to MakeGood Technologies along with a Chaordix licence in July 2008 (e-ChannelNews, 21 Jul 2008).
  • The real asset was the software. Chaordix, spun out in 2009 under Kuipers, served P&G, IBM, American Airlines and others ("a community of over 100,000") by 2012 (Calgary Herald, 27 Sep 2012). It moved LEGO CUUSOO onto its platform as LEGO Ideas in 2014 (Wikipedia: LEGO Ideas). By 2019 it was credited with 28 LEGO Ideas sets, "90 per cent selling out in their first release" (a company claim, Calgary Herald, 16 Dec 2019). Crunchbase now lists Chaordix as permanently closed and Tracxn calls it "acqui-hired", but no dates were found.

What they did right

  • Turned voting into a ritual. The move from open-ended ranking to time-boxed weekly brackets, with a named winner, a prize and a season final, is a well-documented fix for "no finality" (CH blog).
  • Took member ownership seriously. The co-op, the elected council and contribution-weighted dividends were years ahead of later "community-owned" experiments (Get your stock).
  • Built in public. Transparent posts about what was broken, quick apologies and a sense of humour earned goodwill. Even an outside developer who found its unsecured dev site came away a fan (push.cx).
  • Crowd-tested demand before building. Market tests and pre-orders (Gwabs went into production because pre-orders exceeded expectations) (Globe and Mail). This "wisdom of crowds" half worked, by the CEO's own account.
  • Kept the platform when it failed. The tooling became Chaordix, which ran LEGO Ideas, the successful co-creation community of the next decade.

What they did wrong

  • Rewarded the wrong scarce input. Ideas were abundant (6,935 of them). Execution was scarce, and royalty points worth "maybe someday" could not attract skilled builders. "The heavy lifting kept falling back on us, or a few select community members… a vicious cycle" (Sikorsky, TechCrunch). Mashable predicted exactly this in July 2006 (Mashable).
  • Over-promised economics. A $153,600 calculator against $400 actual top payouts damaged trust in the core incentive.
  • Diffusion and too many pivots. In two years the model shifted from IdeaWarz v1, to brackets, to monthly tournaments, to weekly voting with a quarterly final, Cambros, the Bazaar, a prediction-market idea and then VenCorps. Products ranged from gift wrap and charity to film funding, desktop games and mobile testing. A member: "they seemed to have a clear strategy, but just not follow it" (HN).
  • Hiring instead of shipping the platform. Members saw a 40-person staff but a site that did not improve; a former employee says the CEO brushed off a TechCrunch introduction (HN).
  • Recruited "anyone who had an idea" rather than the tech community (HN). Quality suffered: duplicates and low-quality ideas (Wikipedia), with projects like "congress is corrupt, help me get rid of congress" (HN, Mar 2009).
  • Branding friction. Sikorsky admitted "a lot of people found it hard to type, hated the vikings" (mathewingram.com).
  • An abrupt ending. Members got three months' access before the community was redirected to VenCorps, a different product (picking startups for reputation points) (CH blog, 19 May 2008; CNET). VenCorps itself later closed.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Time-box participation into rounds with a winner. Weekly brackets, a named champion and a season final give a reason to return on a schedule. Cambrian House's own diagnosis of "no finality" applies to any idea portal.
  2. Make ownership concrete and proportional. A co-op share, an elected council and contribution-weighted rewards gave members standing. Copy the structure, but tie it to real, near-term value.
  3. Narrate the build in public. Candid update posts, apologies with swag, and staff commenting on member work kept goodwill high even when the product was rough.
  4. Use the crowd for demand-testing, where it worked. Voting plus pre-orders is a cheap, credible signal; keep execution with accountable teams.
  5. Treat the community tooling as a product. The idea-and-vote engine outlived the community and powered LEGO Ideas. Companies running co-creation should invest in the pipeline (submission, voting thresholds, review, royalties) as core infrastructure.

Avoid these

  1. Don't make members' payoff depend on something the community can't deliver. If shipping needs skilled teams, recruit or staff them; otherwise royalties become IOUs and engagement decays to bookmarks.
  2. Don't over-promise rewards. One inflated calculator undermines every later incentive.
  3. Don't let constant pivots and side projects dilute the core loop. Pick one product domain and one participation path and finish things; members judge you by what ships.
