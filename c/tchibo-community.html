TL;DR

  • What it is. A members-only social layer inside Tchibo's German webshop at community.tchibo.de, launched at the end of October 2016 alongside the relaunched TchiboCard loyalty program. It took over from the Tchibo ideas co-creation site, which has 301-redirected here since then (see tchibo-ideas). Members post coffee moments and recipes, apply for product tests, enter quizzes and sweepstakes, and climb five levels that pay out loyalty points. In March 2024 Tchibo put membership at about 1.0 million [1][2][3].
  • Why people come back. Mostly for a feeling of being seen. A small in-house editorial team, using first names, tries to react to every member post, and members say this matters to them. Around that sits a dense calendar: a quiz sweepstake every Wednesday, a recipe of the month, product-test calls, quarterly digital events, invitations to Hamburg HQ, and since January 2024 levels and badges that unlock perks [3][4][5].
  • What Tchibo feeds it. A team that grew from 2 people (team lead Pascal Ritzel and an intern) to 8, including interns and working students. The team builds the campaign pages itself and supplies daily editorial posts, coffee films, event kits, test parcels, podcasts, a YouTube series and physical goodies such as a members' calendar and a Christmas booklet [3][6][7][8].
  • The biggest mistake. It is a club on Tchibo's terms. Joining requires a webshop account, a TchiboCard and a newsletter subscription. Members hand Tchibo perpetual, unpaid rights to everything they upload. Complaints are banned. The weekly sweepstakes draw prize hunters as much as fans. Peer content gets buried, and the team admits a costly broadcast video format flopped [2][3][9].
  • The lesson. For a mass-market brand, the most durable retention engine was a small, named, always-replying staff team plus "apply to take part" formats wired into the loyalty program. Formats where members help shape the product beat polished content the company broadcasts at them, and the team's own post-mortem says so [3].

What the community is

  • Where. community.tchibo.de, branded "TchiboCard Community" (Tchibo also says "Tchibo Community"). It is linked from the Tchibo webshop and app, and the Google Play listing advertises "exclusive promotions, competitions and product tests as a member of the TchiboCard Community" [10]. Anyone can read it. Only adults with a German address who hold a Tchibo webshop account, a TchiboCard and a newsletter subscription can post [2].
  • Sections today (navigation, Nov 2026) [1]:
    • Start feed. Member and staff posts with likes, comments, "save" and emotion tags. Badges such as "Publikumsliebling" (audience favourite), "Kaffee-Kenner" (coffee connoisseur) and "Community-Sternekoch" (community star chef) appear next to names.
    • Produkttests. Calls to apply, each with its own terms. Testers are picked "per jury decision", get a parcel, fill in a questionnaire, and see their reviews published on a results page.
    • Community Café. Coffee knowledge, recipes, events and the Kaffee-Botschafter (coffee ambassador) program.
    • Kochbuch (cookbook). Members' recipes, a "Kochecke der Profis" (pros' corner) and a personal cookbook.
    • Also present. "Tchibo Momente" photo categories (since 6 Nov 2017), community birthday greetings, and a "Community Insider" status [11][12][13].
  • Who runs it. Tchibo GmbH, Marketing Germany [14]. The team sits in CRM: Pascal Ritzel's titles are "Lead CRM Manager (Tchibo Community)" and "Teamleader TchiboCard Community" [15][3].
  • Unofficial spaces. None of note. r/Tchibo has 75 subscribers and 44 posts, about machines, not the community [16]. The most active outside space is a sweepstakes-hunter thread on Gewinn-Portal.de that posts the answers to the community's weekly quiz (330,571 views, 110 replies, Jun 2021 to Nov 2026) [9].
  • Status. Active. October 2026 brought a "Geburtstagsmonat" (birthday month) for the 10th anniversary, with games (wheel of fortune, memory, estimation, survey), a two-day in-person birthday event, a YouTube series ("OH, Community!") and a new WhatsApp channel [17][18][1].

Origin and strategy

  • The idea (early 2016). In the team's words: "In the store you can talk to our store colleagues; online we don't have that, so we need an interaction option online." The goal was "a meeting place for our Tchibo fans, designed together with them". Pascal Ritzel compared it to an empty apartment the fans were invited to furnish [3].
  • Lean launch (end of 2016). "We went live at the end of 2016 with a basic community platform with a few basic functions," then added features one at a time. The team was two people, Ritzel and an intern. They "came up with all campaigns ourselves and implemented them, i.e. also built the web pages", so they could learn fast [3]. The first Wayback capture of community.tchibo.de is dated 28 Oct 2016, and the anniversary is celebrated in October [19][4].
  • Business context. Lebensmittel Zeitung reported on 30 Sep 2016 that Tchibo was relaunching its customer card to fight falling store revenue and footfall [20]. The old PrivatCard became the TchiboCard, with TreueBohnen ("loyalty beans", 2 per euro, worth 1 cent each), and the community launched alongside it as the card's "exclusive" social layer [21][2]. Ritzel had relaunched Tchibo ideas in 2013 and ended up running both [22].
  • The company's framing. Tchibo calls it "das Loyalitätsprogramm" and "our social media channel directly in the Tchibo webshop", meaning owned rather than rented social [4][3]. A Tchibo CRM manager describes it as "the largest and most varied participation program in Germany", built "not only for but above all with our members" [23]. That is a company claim with no comparison offered.
  • The data motive is explicit. Its terms let Tchibo analyse community activity for "advertising tailored to you personally" in the Tchibo account [2]. In 2026 Tchibo told Lebensmittel Zeitung that its 16-million-member card has a weakness: it only produces personal purchase data when it is scanned [24].

What keeps people coming back

  1. Staff who answer everyone, by first name. "We look at all posts and try to react to all posts… that is the standard our team sets." Members said they valued "that the real employees behind the scenes at Tchibo react to our posts". Tchibo uses no outsourced agency for moderation, and the community used the informal "du" from day one [3]. Staff sign their posts (Sophie, Philine, Thuy Trang, "Community-Sternekoch" Christian) and share personal life. Team members' puppies, Ruby and Mara, became recurring characters, and members proposed Ruby as the community mascot [3][1]. A departing staffer's farewell post in Nov 2026 shows how personal the ties are [1].
  2. Progress that pays in the loyalty currency. Since January 2024, five levels are earned through posts, likes, comments, profile completion and taking part in campaigns. Each member sees an activity score on their profile, and nobody is ever downgraded ("Take that, Deutsche Bahn!"). Each level unlocks something [3][1]:
    • Level 2: surveys, exclusive sweepstakes, a birthday surprise.
    • Level 3: highlight campaigns and product tests, plus 500 TreueBohnen (€5).
    • Level 4: the right to apply to the ambassador program, plus 500 TreueBohnen.
    • Level 5: limited VIP events, plus 500 TreueBohnen.
    • Badges: for example "Café Insider" after 10 quiz entries, "Produkttester", and "Community Kenner" (community expert) for the onboarding quiz. Badges can bring "preference in campaigns or shopping vouchers" [2].
    • Insider status: a "Community Insider" tier promises "a direct line to the team" [35].
  3. Apply-to-take-part rituals. A weekly "Highlight der Woche" quiz sweepstake every Wednesday, recipe challenges such as cookie recipes in Advent, regular product-test calls, and seasonal advent calendars [9][1]. Every test ends with a public results page of ratings and reviews [32].
  4. Events with escalating intimacy. Quarterly digital events (Easter, Day of Coffee, cocktail parties, Christmas) are led by Tchibo's coffee sommelière and designers. Selected participants receive parcels; from about Dec 2025 a livestream let everyone join because of "the great rush" [31][36][30]. In-person "Tag des Kaffees" visits to the Hamburg roastery and coffee lab are raffled. The ambassador called hers "my biggest highlight" [3]. The 10th birthday was a two-day in-person event where "new friendships were made" [17]. A guest writer at the Easter event put it this way: "man kennt sich – klar" ("people know each other, of course") [36].
  5. Status roles. Kaffee-Botschafter (coffee ambassador) is a term of a few months; Angelika served January to April 2024. Ambassadors do monthly tasks (coffee cocktails, smoothies, facts, reports) and pass the role on to the next person, which creates a status ladder with turnover [3][37].
  6. Peer warmth, explicitly cultivated. The netiquette puts "friendly treatment first", and the feed is full of small personal posts: weather, sourdough recipes, hopeful affirmations with supportive replies [1]. Since 2024 the platform has a friends function and feed filtering, after an ambassador asked for it on the podcast [3][1].
  7. Weak switching costs. A personal cookbook, badges, level and accrued status live in the account. Status is never lost, so leaving costs little, but the earned position is a quiet reason to keep logging in.

The content engine

  • Staff editorial feed. A "Redaktion" (editorial team) of four to five named people posts several times a week: monthly highlights, studio shoot diaries, gift tips, quiz prompts, a "Kaffeerezept des Monats" (coffee recipe of the month, for example the Mellomokka in Nov 2026) and test results [1].
  • Education. The Community Café has four pillars: knowledge, recipes, events and ambassadors. It includes 12 coffee films "from seed to cup". "Rezept des Monats" picks a member's recipe, pairs it with Tchibo coffee and machines, and films it [3].
  • Member UGC, curated. The cookbook (since 2018), Tchibo Momente photo themes (since 2017), test reviews, and event photos [13][12]. The cake contest drew about 1,000 member recipes. An internal jury picked three, Tchibo's cake supplier baked them, and "Lena's Nusskuchen" was sold at coffee counters in all stores for a month [3]. In 2025, 300 members co-blended a specialty coffee with roaster Hoppenworth & Ploch that went on sale [29].
  • Physical artefacts. A members-only annual calendar, nearly scrapped after its first edition, came back after members "practically screamed for a second edition". A "zauberhaftes Weihnachtsheft" (Christmas booklet) was revived the same way. There are also postcards and flowers sent to VIPs [3].
  • Company channels. The corporate podcast "5 Tassen täglich" (episode #97/98, May 2024) [4][7], the "OH, Community!" YouTube member portraits (Sep–Oct 2026) [18], a WhatsApp channel (Nov 2026) [1], blog recaps of events on tchibo.com [30][31][36], and a community newsletter [1].
  • What flopped. About three years before 2024 the team made a "digital Coffee-Date": expert answers to member questions filmed at a rented coffee bike, partly at the harbour. "Incredibly elaborate… and in the end it didn't go down that well in the community." Conclusion: "community campaigns that invite participation, where you can really shape the Tchibo brand, go down really well," so the team shifted to those [3].

People, programs and governance

  • Team. Pascal Ritzel, a former social-media consultant (Stage Entertainment musical communities, Carlsberg), led it from 2016 [22]. Community manager Patricia ran the Community Café. Sophie was the first moderator hired. Tanja hosts events. The team was 8 strong in May 2024, including interns and working students [3][31]. A weekly "Community-Team-Kaffee" every Thursday reviews members' highlight posts [3]. Tchibo experts appear at events: the coffee sommelière Indre Berendes, designers, product managers and sustainability staff [36][30][38].
  • Kaffee-Botschafter program. Launched with the 2020 Café ("you can become the first Tchibo coffee ambassador"). Members can apply from Level 4 via a questionnaire, serve for a few months, test new coffees exclusively and post monthly content [39][37][3]. Perks are product and experience based. No cash is mentioned.
  • Rules.
    • The netiquette bans insults, bullying and racism. Violations can lead to profile deactivation [1].
    • It "is not a forum for exchanging complaints about Tchibo products". Questions go to customer service [2].
    • Members may not post AI-generated images in challenges, and must label realistic AI imagery elsewhere (added recently) [2].
    • Members grant Tchibo unpaid, unlimited rights to all content, including in print, radio and TV, with authors' remuneration claims (§§32 ff. UrhG) excluded. Tchibo may reuse names and profile pictures in the webshop for advertising [2].
    • Testers who blog must label posts "Werbung" (advertising) [32].
  • Feedback loop. Regular member surveys ("Umfragen"), test questionnaires, a survey on TreueBohnen expiry, and designers telling members that "feedback, wishes and also constructive criticism from the community" shape products [4][40][36]. Visible product effects are limited: a cake, a coffee blend, test-based copy on product pages, and features the team says it adds at members' request (friends function, calendar) [3][33].
  • Governance mistakes acknowledged. The team reports system outages when features "worked too well" and load spiked: "then an alarm bell goes off" [3].

Growth loops

  • The loyalty base as the funnel. Every TchiboCard holder (8.7M in Germany, 2024) is a prospect, and the app and card pages advertise the community [28][10]. The required newsletter opt-in also turns community sign-up into CRM growth [2].
  • Freebie and sweepstakes sites. Gratisalarm, Monetenfuchs and Gewinn-Portal regularly post "Tchibo sucht Produkttester" (Tchibo is looking for product testers) and the weekly quiz answers. They bring sign-ups, but of prize-seekers [41][42][9].
  • Commerce surfaces. Product pages carry community-test claims ("15 community members were selected… enthusiasm"), and a member's recipe was sold in stores [33][3].
  • Owned media. The podcast, the YouTube series, blog posts that end with "want to join? here", and the WhatsApp channel [4][18][36].
  • No evidence of member referral programs or meaningful SEO-driven acquisition.

Score rationale.

  • Retention mechanics, 4. A rich, well-designed mix: rituals, never-lost levels paid in loyalty currency, events and named staff. Peer bonds stay secondary.
  • Content engine, 4. A daily staff feed, films, recipes, events, test results and owned media, plus curated UGC.
  • Member ownership, 2. No member moderators or member-run spaces, complaints banned, and Tchibo owns all content.
  • Staff investment, 4. An 8-person in-house team, events, parcels and physical goods.
  • Business impact evidence, 2. Only company claims and indirect signals.
  • Durability, 4. Ten years, growing, and celebrated by the company.

What they did right

  1. A lean launch, co-designed. Two people went live with "a few basic functions" and added profiles, a moderator, tests, a cookbook, a café, levels and friends in the order members asked for them [3]. Ten years of survival backs this up.
  2. Human, named, in-house hosting. A policy of answering every post with no outsourced moderators, the informal "du", and staff sharing personal life. Members explicitly cite this as why it feels like "sitting on the couch with friends" [3][4].
  3. Gamification tied to an existing currency and never punitive. Level-ups pay real loyalty points and unlock participation rather than discounts, and no member is ever downgraded [3][1].
  4. "Apply to take part" scarcity. Selected testers, ambassadors, event seats and VIP mail create recurring hope and pride. Results pages close the loop publicly [32][31].
  5. Killing what didn't work. The team dropped the expensive Coffee-Date video format for participatory formats, and revived the calendar and Christmas booklet because members asked [3].
  6. Escalating real-world contact. Virtual events led to raffled HQ visits and then a two-day anniversary meetup. A livestream opened events to everyone once demand outgrew the seats [31][17].

What they did wrong

  1. A heavy entry toll and data-first terms.
    • The cost to members: joining needs three accounts or opt-ins, including the marketing newsletter. Activity is analysed for personalised advertising, and all uploads are licensed to Tchibo forever, unpaid [2].
    • The consequence: high friction for casual fans. There is no evidence of backlash, but it caps the community's reach to people already willing to be marketed to.
  2. Banning complaints. "Not a forum for complaints" keeps the space sunny. It also gives up support deflection and the credibility that comes with honest criticism, while Tchibo's service draws harsh public reviews elsewhere (Trustpilot, 2026) [2][45].
  3. Sweepstake gravity. A weekly prize quiz lets an outside forum farm answers (330k views) [9]. Likes count toward activity, which rewards shallow engagement. The team insists "we are not a sweepstakes platform", which suggests it knows the risk [3].
  4. Peer content buried. Ambassador Angelika: excellent member reports "sometimes go under in the flood of 'how do I make coffee'". A friends feature was only "on the roadmap" in 2024 [3].
  5. Broadcast content that missed. The Coffee-Date video series, by the team's own account [3].
  6. Reliability. Outages when campaigns drew too many users at once [3].
  7. Ambassador load. Monthly deliverables made the role "sometimes quite stressful" [3].
  8. Lost co-creation lineage. The successor dropped the open idea-to-shelf contest of Tchibo ideas (see tchibo-ideas). Co-creation survives only in occasional, Tchibo-scoped contests (a cake, a coffee blend) [3][29].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Launch small and let members furnish the room. Ship a bare platform, then add features (profiles, a moderator, recipes, levels, friends) in the order members demonstrably want them.
  2. Staff it with named humans who reply to everything. A small in-house team signing with first names, plus a weekly ritual to review highlight posts, is the cheapest durable retention mechanic in this case.
  3. Pay progress in an existing currency, and never take it away. Tie levels to points or perks your product already has. Unlock participation (tests, events, ambassador applications) rather than just discounts. Don't demote people.
  4. Make "apply to take part" your core loop, and always publish the results. Test panels, ambassador terms and event seats create hope, status and a reason to check back. Results pages close the loop.
  5. Prefer participatory formats to polished broadcast content. Tchibo's expensive video series flopped, while a members' recipe going on sale in every store became the story it retells.

Avoid these

  1. Don't make the community a data toll booth. Mandatory newsletter opt-in and perpetual content rights tell members whose space it is. That caps trust and reach.
  2. Don't let sweepstakes become the heartbeat. Weekly prize quizzes and like-based activity points attract prize hunters and shallow engagement. Weight progress toward contributions that others value.
  3. Don't ban criticism and bury good peer content. Give complaints a moderated path, and build discovery for members' best posts (follows, featured members) early, not in year eight.
