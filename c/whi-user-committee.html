TL;DR

  • What it is: the free user association for COMPANY, the HR, payroll and attendance suite that Works Applications built (1996) and Works Human Intelligence (WHI) has owned since the 2019 carve-out. It started in February 2001 with 30 companies and had 1,136 member organizations and 21,356 registered individuals at the end of July 2026 [1][2][3].
  • Why people come back: every member runs the same product with no customization, so a configuration trick or workaround from another company can be copied at once. Add yearly rituals tied to the HR calendar (the year-end tax adjustment subcommittee every year), direct access to WHI developers, and a peer network that HR staff otherwise lack [1][2].
  • What the company does: WHI pays for everything (membership is free), sends developers and consultants to subcommittees, runs the members-only @CONNECT site, two annual summits and live participatory sessions, and since 2022 has had a dedicated department that turns member surveys into proposals to government [2][6][9].
  • Biggest mistake: the predecessor, Works Applications, put its money and attention into the AI ERP "HUE" (launched at the committee's own 2014 annual meeting). The resulting losses forced the 2019 sale of the HR business. Bain Capital, the buyer, said 1,000+ major customers were "not receiving full value" at that point. The community kept customers loyal but could not stop the vendor from neglecting them, and the split left two separate "User Committees" [10][11][12][13].
  • The one lesson: give the community a job bigger than the product. Here, members change national rules that cause them pain (a standard form for daycare employment certificates, removing My Number IDs from tax notices). That makes membership worth more than a support forum, and it ties the product to the rules it implements [2][8].

What the community is

The ユーザーコミッティ (User Committee, "UC") is the user association of COMPANY, the enterprise HR suite used by about 1,200 large corporate groups in Japan (about 5.7 million employee records, end of 2025) and, by WHI's count, one in three Japanese companies with 3,000+ employees [14]. WHI describes it as "a user association our users join" for researching product use and operating cases, sharing information between users, and exchanging opinions. It also says it uses member feedback to improve the product and "gathers opinions toward revising related laws and makes proposals to government" [2].

Spaces and formats (all official):

  • Subcommittees (分科会): by theme, industry (for example hospitals), product and region. WHI developers "actively take part" [2][15]. The year-end tax adjustment subcommittee meets every year (2020: 106 organizations, 187 people) [2].
  • Annual all-member meeting: the 定例会 (the 18th was held on 29 Aug 2014 [10]). It later became the 総会 inside User Committee Week (fully online in 2021–2022 [6]), and is now two flagships: the User Committee Sharing Summit (Feb–Mar, hybrid, three days) and the COMPANY User Summit (late August, in person at a Tokyo hotel, with an after-party) [3][16][17].
  • @CONNECT: the members-only site with case studies, subcommittee reports, survey reports, members-only talks and videos, and an online community for questions between members [2]. Event URLs ran on Salesforce's community hosting (works-hi-uc.force.com) in 2022 [6].
  • Government-relations work: surveys and proposals, and meetings between members, legislators and ministries (since 2015) [8][9].
  • Member-run governance: a steering committee (幹事会) chaired by a customer. In 2009 the chair came from Suntory HR, with vice-chairs from NSK and Mitsubishi Chemical [1]. In 2026 the chair was an honorary member from Furukawa Electric's shared-services subsidiary [17].

Unofficial spaces: I found no meaningful independent space (no subreddit, Hacker News threads or fan forum; HN search for "Works Applications" returned 0 stories). The community is Japanese-language, enterprise-only and gated, so outside traces are a handful of personal blogs and speaker announcements [18][19].

Status: active and growing in registered members. Events still sell out large venues (COMPANY User Summit 2025: 235 organizations, 491 people [17]).

Origin and strategy

  • Product model as the strategy. Works Applications sold COMPANY on "no customization" and "free version upgrades": instead of building add-ons for each client, it put every client's legal and business requirements into the standard product [11]. That made a user association unusually useful: in WHI's words, "because users use the same product without customization, operating and configuration know-how they share can be adopted in their own company immediately" [2]. It also fed product development. Works Applications said in 2018 that because all customers use the same product, it "absorbs each company's evolving processes and returns them to everyone as standard features" [20].
  • Founding and early growth. The UC was set up in February 2001 [21] with 30 companies. By December 2008 it had about 630, was growing by 80–90 companies a year, and more than 90% of customer companies took part [1]. Naoko Ohnoki (now an executive officer heading Customer Relations) joined in 2003 when the association had "100-plus" companies and was given the job of "seriously activating" it. Management told her "you can do anything, as long as it raises customer satisfaction." She grew it past 1,000 companies, left to set up a customer-satisfaction research function, then came back [4].
  • Business-model tie. COMPANY is sold as licenses plus maintenance or cloud fees on long contracts. Retention is the economics: WHI cites a 98% annual renewal rate and a 12-year average tenure, against a typical five-year replacement cycle for enterprise packages [14]. The committee is also free support capacity (peers answer each other) and a sales asset: press releases about new customers routinely list "using the User Committee to learn from other companies' operations" as a reason for buying [22].
  • Policy as product strategy. HR software in Japan is shaped by tax and social-insurance procedures. Since 2015 (before My Number started) WHI and the UC have gathered members' pain points and taken them to government. In 2022 WHI created a dedicated department for this work [9].

Trajectory: the number of member organizations has been flat at about 1,100 since the early 2020s, roughly the whole customer base of about 1,200 groups (the two counts use different units, so this is an estimate). Registered individuals more than quadrupled, from 4,900 to 21,356, between Aug 2022 and Jul 2026. With the organization count flat, that looks like a push to register many people per customer on @CONNECT rather than new customers (my inference; WHI gives no active-user or engagement numbers). Event attendance of about 500 per flagship is steady, not growing.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A ritual tied to the HR calendar. Japanese payroll teams face the year-end tax adjustment (年末調整) every year. Since at least 2009 the UC has run a tax adjustment subcommittee every February, where members file requests and developers explain upcoming legal changes [1][2]. Each legal change (electronic deduction certificates in 2020, electronic resident-tax notices in 2024) gives members a reason to come back [2][9].
  2. One product means one shared vocabulary. The 2009 chair from Suntory: "In conversation you can use 'COMPANY' as a common language" [1]. Because nothing is customized, a tip from one company works at another.
  3. Peer benchmarking HR staff cannot get elsewhere. The live "COMPANY 1000-knock" format runs pre-event surveys and real-time polls on questions like flex-time core hours, remote-work allowances and side-job rules, and members vote up questions for discussion [3]. Participants valued "hearing other companies' real situations and candid worries" [3].
  4. Direct line to developers. Developers sit in subcommittees and hear requests firsthand. A member of the sister Works Applications UC calls that "the great appeal of the User Committee, and what's amazing about Works" [15].
  5. Status and recognition. In 2005 the UC created a "Hall of Fame" (殿堂): honorary membership with a commemorative watch for key contributors, who keep access after leaving COMPANY-using roles. Honorary members still chair the steering committee and speak at summits in 2026 [17][18].
  6. Belonging and IRL bonds. A 2009 member blog describes the annual meeting as "like a reunion… like going home for the summer fireworks." The Kansai members pre-booked the second and third after-parties, and the night ended with a fifth round at Yoshinoya [18]. In 2026 the summits still promote in-person networking and after-parties, and tell attendees to bring business cards [16][17].
  7. Visible impact. Members see their survey answers turn into national rule changes (see Governance). That is a stronger reason to join than any badge.
  8. Switching costs. Twelve-year average tenure [14] plus years of shared know-how, networks and lobbying through COMPANY make leaving costly. Nobody else has a peer group that runs the same configuration.

No gamification, streaks or leaderboards: no evidence found, and none would suit this audience.

The content engine

  • @CONNECT (members-only): case studies, subcommittee reports, survey reports, members-only lectures and videos, online Q&A [2].
  • Survey-to-report pipeline: subcommittee surveys become reports for members and sometimes public press, for example the 2021 tax adjustment report (106 companies) [2] and the May 2020 finding that "59% have no policy yet" on electronic tax adjustment [26].
  • Member-led case talks at summits: for example UACJ's HR process redesign (about 2,300 hours saved), Sapporo Breweries' "no-rating" appraisal [6], and Persol's COMPANY Ver.8 migration for 32,000 employees across about 30 companies [27].
  • Live participatory formats: "Teach! Talk! Brag! COMPANY 1000-knock," a viewer-participation live stream with chat and real-time polls, with member commentators [3][6]. Over 80% said they would recommend it [3].
  • Older formats: a printed newsletter (会報誌) and links to the "HOT program" user training [1].
  • Public layer: WHI's open "business columns" on regulatory topics (resident-tax notices, employment certificates) draw on the government-relations work and serve as SEO [28].
  • Who makes it: WHI's Customer Relations division historically [4]; by 2025 the UC was run by the marketing department, alongside PR and government negotiation and advocacy [29][30]. Members supply the cases and the survey data.

People, programs and governance

  • Member-run front, vendor-funded back. WHI calls the UC "run by HR departments of large corporations for over 20 years" [3], with a customer chair and vice-chairs [1][17]. WHI pays all costs ("membership is free… all costs are borne by Works Applications") [1].
  • Staff: a UC secretariat (uc@works-hi.co.jp) [17]; an executive sponsor (Ohnoki, executive officer) [4]; developers and consultants in subcommittees [1][2]; a government-relations department since 2022 [9]. Total community headcount: no evidence found.
  • Honorary members (Hall of Fame), since 2005: the closest thing to an MVP program [18].
  • Feedback loop to the product: requests go straight to developers in subcommittees. Because there is one standard product, every accepted request ships to every customer in the next free version upgrade [1][11].
  • Feedback loop to government (unusual):
    • Employment certificates for daycare: a survey of about 240 member companies found each spent 88+ hours a year hand-filling municipality-specific forms. Results went to the Cabinet Secretariat, and in October 2016 two Diet members met seven member companies. The Cabinet Office and Ministry of Health issued a standard form on 8 Aug 2017 [8]. Ministers later announced a unified form with online submission from autumn 2023 [31].
    • My Number removed from employer copies of resident-tax notices (from FY2018) [2][32].
    • Electronic resident-tax notices (2024): WHI lobbied the Ministry of Internal Affairs and eLTAX from autumn 2022. A UC steering company said: "As User Committee stewards we have pursued this Government Relations work together" [9].
    • WHI also co-signed the 2021 "Born Digital" proposal on year-end tax adjustment with SAP Japan, OBC, PCA, Miroku and Yayoi, delivered to the Digital Reform Minister [33].

Growth loops

  • Reference selling: new-customer releases (Kowa 2022, Shinko Wire 2022, Tobishima 2023, Human Holdings 2023, Saiseikai Central Hospital 2023) name the UC as a reason or benefit [22]. Industry subcommittees (for example 50+ hospital customers) reassure buyers in the same vertical.
  • Evangelists: a 2009 member who had led an implementation hosted prospect visits and gave talks for the product [18].
  • Policy wins as PR: each regulatory change is announced as "realized through our users' voices," which builds credibility with HR buyers [8][9].
  • Large marketing events (predecessor era): COMPANY Forum (yearly from 2004, 16,000–17,000 attendees in 2014–15) [23][34] fed prospects into the customer base, which then joined the UC.
  • No referral program, public forum SEO or social sharing loop: no evidence found. The community is closed.

Evidence of business impact

  • Company claims (no independent check): 98% annual contract renewal and 12-year average tenure [14]; #1 share in ERP HR and payroll for six straight years (ITR) [14][25]; "one in three" large companies [14]. Works Applications said in 2020 that its UC activities lead to "94% continued-use rate" for its own (post-split) customers [13].
  • Participation as a proxy: more than 90% of customer companies participated in 2008 [1]; about 1,136 member organizations against about 1,200 customer groups in 2026 [2][14].
  • Event NPS-like signal: over 80% would recommend the 2023 live session [3].
  • Third-party view: Bain Capital's case study says that before 2019 "1,000+ major corporate customers… were not receiving full value." Bain then built "a best-in-class customer success organization… to drive retention and lifetime value" [12]. So the strong community coexisted with under-served customers.
  • No causal study links UC participation to renewal, and no academic research was found. Score 3: strong retention numbers, but company-reported and not attributed to the community.

What they did right

  1. Built the community on a product decision. No customization means peer advice can be copied directly, and that is the core value [1][2].
  2. Kept it free and fully paid by the vendor, which got 90%+ participation by 2008 [1].
  3. Put real ownership with members (customer chair, steering committee, honorary members) while the vendor kept running it; 25 years without a public governance blow-up [1][3][17].
  4. Gave members direct access to developers, with one product so feedback visibly reaches everyone [1][15].
  5. Turned shared pain into policy wins, a reason to belong that no competitor's forum offers [2][8][9].
  6. Anchored rituals to the customer's work calendar (yearly tax adjustment subcommittee, February and August summits) [1][2][3].
  7. Survived ownership change: the 2019 carve-out did not break it. Membership and events grew under WHI [3][4][12].
  8. Adapted to COVID with online weeks (1,000+ in 2021) and participatory live formats, then returned to in-person networking [6][17].

What they did wrong

  1. The vendor's attention moved elsewhere. Works Applications launched HUE at the UC's 18th annual meeting (2014) [10] and spent heavily on it. It lost ¥17bn in FY2018 and nearly became insolvent [35]. Bain says COMPANY customers weren't getting full value [12]. Consequence: a forced sale and contract upheaval for multi-module customers [11]. The community kept loyalty but could not substitute for product investment.
  2. The community split in two. After 2019, Works Applications kept its own "User Committee" for HUE and other ERP products (319 companies in 2020; 63 organizations and 104 people at its 2025 annual meeting) [13][36], under the same name and heritage. That splits the network and confuses customers who use both.
  3. Early warning on scaling. A 2009 honorary member wrote that installs were outpacing UC sign-ups, so "the participation rate may be falling," and wondered whether the traditional format had reached "a turning point" [18]. WHI later moved to online formats and mass individual registration, but has published no engagement rates.
  4. An opaque headline metric. Registered members jumped from about 6,000 to 21,356 in under four years while member organizations stayed flat [2][5][24]. With no published active-user data, it is unclear how much of that is engagement.
  5. Closed by design. Nearly all user knowledge sits behind the @CONNECT login. That limits public SEO, outside visibility and member voice beyond WHI's curated quotes (this report relies mainly on company sources as a result).
  6. The product still gets end-user complaints (for example a complex UI for occasional employee requests) that a committee of HR administrators may underweight [37].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Make peer knowledge directly transferable. Standardize the product, or at least shared templates and configurations, so one member's solution works for the others. That is where most of the value comes from.
  2. Tie rituals to the customer's work calendar: a subcommittee before every recurring crunch (here, the year-end tax adjustment), so the community is where people prepare.
  3. Put developers in the room and close the loop visibly: requests heard, then shipped to everyone in the next release.
  4. Give members a mission beyond the product, such as collective advocacy on the external rules that cause their pain. Survey members, publish the data, and take it to whoever sets the rules.
  5. Let customers own the front of the community (customer chair, steering committee, a hall of fame for contributors who keep status after changing jobs) while the company pays for and staffs everything.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let a new bet drain the core customer base. A loyal community buys time but cannot make up for underinvestment in the product (the HUE episode).
  2. Don't confuse registrations with engagement. Track and publish active participation, especially when headline membership jumps without new customers.
  3. Don't lock all knowledge behind a login. Keep a public layer (anonymized cases, columns) so the community helps acquisition and prospects can see it.
