Peak

2015–2017: membership doubled every year (51,838 → 121,158 → 178k), ~5,400 new projects in year two, the Prize paid $250k–$500k a year and drew 1,000+ entries (2016); 43 Hack Chats a year in 2019–2020 (Hackaday blog; Hack Chat transcript index)

How it pays off for the company

Brand and audience for an electronics-industry data and advertising company. Hackaday's engineer audience supports Supplyframe's ad and partnership sales (Findchips, sponsored contests), and chip makers and distributors such as Microchip, TI, Mouser and DigiKey pay to sponsor the Prize and contests. No direct revenue; the editor-in-chief said Supplyframe subsidizes Hackaday's editorial independence.

TL;DR

  • What it is: Hackaday.io is a free project-hosting and social site for hardware hackers. Supplyframe, an electronics-industry data and advertising company, launched it in February 2014, seven months after it bought the Hackaday blog. The Hackaday Prize (2014–2023) was its yearly flagship contest. Around both sit the weekly Hack Chat, the Hackaday Superconference in Pasadena (since 2015), Hackaday Europe, and a Discord server.
  • Why people came back: a spring-to-November Prize season with several challenge rounds, cash for finalists and a big grand prize (a trip to space in 2014). The other draw was the chance to get a project onto the hackaday.com front page, read by a huge audience. Smaller tools did the rest: project logs, "skulls" (likes) and follows, real-time project chats, and a sold-out conference where Prize winners were crowned.
  • What the company fed it: money and staff. The Prize paid out $250k–$500k a year at its peak, plus a world tour in 2015, World Create Day in 2016, dedicated community managers, the Hack Chat guest series, HackadayU courses in 2020, and the DesignLab residency. The editorial blog stayed independent, ran no advertorials, and steered readers to .io projects every day.
  • Biggest mistake: slowly abandoning the platform while still claiming its peak numbers. Spam overran the site from about 2017 to 2019 (roughly 95% of project IDs from that era are now deleted, by our sampling). The Prize ended in 2023 with only "more focused contests" to replace it, and Hack Chat dwindled from 43 sessions a year to zero. In mid-2026, private messages were redirected to Discord with no clear warning that message history would become unreachable. The homepage has shown the same "6,515 members" chat counter since 2021.
  • The one lesson: a recurring, well-funded contest attached to a media audience is a superb way to start a UGC community. But contest-driven participation is rented, not owned. When the prize money and staff rituals go, the platform drains unless the product itself has become the habit.

Origin and strategy

Phillip Torrone founded Hackaday in 2004 at Engadget/Weblogs Inc. (Wikipedia). When the blog came up for sale in 2013, an engineer at Supplyframe, a Pasadena company selling search, data and advertising to the electronics supply chain, "bumped it up the chain". Supplyframe bought it on 25 July 2013, telling readers its "platform and products are used by millions of engineers every month" (Hackaday, Jul 2013).

Supplyframe CTO Aleksandar Bradic's team built Hackaday.io as "the first product from our software lab". They framed it as Brian Benchoff's idea of a "virtual hackerspace": not a place to post finished builds, but somewhere "you can start with an idea and get feedback as it develops, find collaborators, iterate" (Show Me The Data Year 1, Feb 2015). The stated motive was partly defensive: to avoid "a dystopian future in which we're all hosting our projects using the (fictional) Microsoft Maker Suite 2020." The company also deliberately did not seed the site with content.

The Prize was the growth engine. Supplyframe launched it on 28 April 2014 with a grand prize of a suborbital trip to space, or about $196k in cash (PRWeb; SatNOGS). Entries had to be documented on hackaday.io, a point one Hacker News commenter spotted on launch day: "Interesting attempt to jump-start a social network. More tasteful than web ads" (HN, Apr 2014).

How it ties to the business: Supplyframe's money comes from SaaS and media sold to component makers and distributors (Findchips, design-to-source data). Its expected 2021 revenue was about $70M, and Siemens paid $700M for it (Siemens, May 2021; Adafruit interview, Jun 2021). Hackaday and Hackaday.io give the company an engineer audience and goodwill. The Prize was co-funded by the same vendors Supplyframe sells to: Atmel, Freescale, Microchip, Mouser and TI in 2015 (Hackaday, Nov 2015), DigiKey in 2022 (DigiKey, Nov 2022), and DigiKey gift cards in today's contests. An HN commenter close to the business summed it up: "Findchips advertising and the SaaS tools… is their moneymaker. Hackaday is basically subsidized through the rest of Supply Frame" (HN, May 2021). Editor-in-chief Mike Szczys said much the same in his farewell: Hackaday never ran advertorials thanks to "the strong backing of our parent company Supplyframe" (Dec 2021).

Champions: Bradic (CTO, built .io), Szczys (editor 2013–2021), Sophi Kravitz (hired in 2015 "to crank on the 2015 Hackaday Prize", later ran .io community; tag), and Majenta Strongheart (Head of Design and Partnerships, later Director of DesignLab, the face of the Prize from 2020 on; Hack Chat, May 2020).

Project-creation estimate (ours). Hackaday.io project IDs are sequential, and we dated several of them: #20000 in Feb 2017, #167845 in about Sep 2019, #180000 in May 2021, #190152 in Mar 2023, #197237 in Aug 2024, #205000 in Feb 2026, #207307 in Nov 2026. We sampled 12 IDs in each band. About 92% of 2014–15 IDs still resolve, but about 95% of IDs created between 2017 and 2019 now return "410 Gone", which we read as mass deletion of spam or empty pages. About half of post-2020 IDs survive. New IDs ran at roughly 57,000 a year in 2017–2019 (the spam wave), 5,000–7,000 a year in 2019–2025, and about 3,000 a year in 2026. Allowing for deletions, that suggests roughly 2,000–3,000 surviving projects a year now, against about 5,400 in the site's second year. This is an estimate from a small sample. It points to a platform whose real creator activity peaked around 2015–2017 and has roughly halved since. The marketing numbers kept rising only because they count dormant and spam accounts.

What keeps people coming back

  1. The Prize season (2014–2023). Each year had a launch video and spring kickoff, then several themed challenge rounds over the summer. Finalists in each round got small cash awards ($500 in 2022–23; $1,000 to each of 120 finalists in 2017), and the grand prize was announced at Supercon in November (2017 contest page; 2023 finalists). This gave entrants a reason to post project logs every week for months, and documentation quality was a judging criterion (Make:, 2020). Year-two data showed a burst of activity around the Prize deadline but also steady growth outside it (Year 1).
  2. The front-page carrot. Hackaday editors write up .io projects daily, and a Prize entry often meant a dedicated article. Supplyframe published the long tail: half of all projects got 272+ views and 100+ followers in 2016, the top 100 got 17,500+ views (Year 2). The pitch was "no matter the kind of project you put on Hackaday.io, it is very likely to get significant exposure."
  3. Light social status. Skulls (likes), followers and view counts were added despite the team's own doubts ("We tend not to be among the most 'social' people on the block"). Follow edges grew tenfold in a year (Year 2). In 2016 likes even became money: $1 per like, up to $200 per project, in the Prize's first round (2016 contest).
  4. Weekly live chat. The free-form "Hacker Channel" (775 members by early 2016) and then a weekly Friday Hack Chat with a guest expert (Eben Upton, Joe Kim, satellite hunters) gave regulars a standing appointment (Hack Chat FAQ).
  5. Members' own rituals. Members organised their own contests on the platform, such as the Square-inch Project (nearly 80 entries), the Traveling Hacker Box, and a contest to give away a Supercon ticket (State of the IO).
  6. IRL anchor. Supercon combines talks, workshops, a custom hackable badge and an SMD soldering challenge, and it sells out every year. The add-on (SAO) contest feeds back into .io: winning designs are mass-produced and handed to attendees (SAO contest 2024).
  7. Switching costs were weak. Projects are public and free; many makers keep files on GitHub and use .io for the build log (Hackaday, May 2022). The private message history was the main lock-in, and that is what the 2026 Discord move broke.

The content engine

  • hackaday.com is the engine: daily posts by 30+ writers, long-form features and the weekly Hackaday Links, all independent of advertisers (Szczys, 2021). Prize coverage ran almost daily in season, for example the 2023 entries posts (category).
  • Weekly Hackaday Podcast, still running in Oct 2026 (episode 392) (link).
  • Hack Chat guest series, with transcripts that double as reference content (index).
  • Prize production: launch videos (2015's "moisture vaporator" shoot), the 13-city Hackaday Prize Worldwide tour in 2015, World Create Day in 80 locations in 2016, and online "Collaboratorium" lightning talks (2015; State of the IO).
  • HackadayU (Jun 2020–2021): live courses by community instructors on Ghidra, KiCad and more, recorded for later viewing; proceeds went to charity, $4,200 from the summer 2020 session (Hackaday, Aug 2020).
  • Curation of member content: staff-curated lists (data loggers, COVID-19 projects), project guidelines, and contest pages run by the Lutetium account (Sophi Kravitz lists; guidelines, 2020).
  • Conference talks on YouTube; Mike Ossmann's 2015 RF workshop video passed 140,000 views (Remoticon tag).

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: a Supplyframe software team for .io, community managers (Kravitz; the Lutetium account, whose code of conduct names managers "Dusan, Dan"; profile), and DesignLab events staff under Strongheart. We found no headcount figures.
  • Judges were famous hardware names, such as Sprite_tm and Raspberry Pi's Eben Upton, giving the Prize prestige (2014; 2018).
  • Residency: grand-prize winners got time at the Supplyframe DesignLab in Pasadena (2016).
  • Nonprofit partners (2020–21): Conservation X Labs, UCP Los Angeles, CalEarth and Field Ready set the challenges, and each got a $10,000 donation (Hackaday, May 2020).
  • Ambassador or superuser program: no evidence found. Recognition came through editorial features and Prize finalist status, not titles.
  • Moderation: project guidelines from 2020 flag "multiple submissions of low-value projects by a single user in a short time frame" (guidelines). The creator-status application (Dec 2024) asks for real name, photo and a social link before posting (page).
  • Feedback loop: a "Feedback – Hackaday.io" project page where staff say "we read what you wrote"; in 2022 most posts there were unanswered bug reports (thread). The Year 2 roadmap (UX refresh, mobile app, BOM management, idea marketplace, Tindie integration) never visibly shipped beyond small pieces (Year 2).

Growth loops

  • Media → platform: the blog's daily audience was steered to .io projects, contests and the Prize. This is the main acquisition loop, and it grew the site from zero to 120k members in two years without pre-seeded content (Year 1).
  • Prize press: winners got coverage in Make:, Hackster, AMSAT-UK and the MIT Media Lab news site (e.g. MIT, 2021). Sponsors issued press releases (PR Newswire 2018).
  • SEO and public pages: all .io content is public, and most visitors never sign up (246,270 uniques against 51,838 accounts in early 2015).
  • IRL: world tour stops, World Create Day, monthly Hackaday LA and MakeIt NYC meetups (State of the IO).
  • Commerce (intended): linking .io projects to Tindie listings. ODrive is one example of a project that "started as a Hackaday Prize entry and became a commercial product" (Make:). Supplyframe sold Tindie in April 2026, ending that loop.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none found. Supplyframe is private and Siemens doesn't break out Hackaday. In the 2021 interview, Siemens declined to answer every question about Hackaday.io staffing, content and DesignLab events (Adafruit, Jun 2021).
  • Proxy evidence: vendors kept paying. Five chip and distribution sponsors co-funded a $500,000 prize package in 2015, and DigiKey sponsored again in 2022 (2015; DigiKey). Supplyframe's anniversary page lists the Hackaday purchase as a brand investment "in the global community of engineers, hackers, and innovators" (Supplyframe, 2023).
  • Social impact claims: SatNOGS used its 2014 winnings to bootstrap the Libre Space Foundation (SatNOGS, 2019). Several winners and finalists became products (ODrive, Jumperless).
  • Prize budget trend (a signal of declining investment): a grand prize worth about $196k in 2014 (space trip or cash), a $500k package in 2015, more than $300k in 2016, $250k in 2017, more than $200k in 2018, and a $125k grand prize in 2019. The grand prize fell to $25k in 2021, then rose to $50k in 2022–23 (2023 total retail value $130k). Today's contests pay $150 gift cards (2016; 2017; 2018; 2019; 2021; 2023 rules; 2026).

What they did right

  1. Launched the platform and a big contest together, with the contest requiring the platform. In the first year, 723 Prize entries became the site's founding content, and the "trip to space" gave mainstream press a hook (2014 list).
  2. Kept the media brand clean. No advertorials and editorial independence preserved readers' trust; even HN skeptics in 2021 called it "the only blog I know which has maintained a high quality" (HN).
  3. Published their own numbers in public. The "Show Me The Data" posts told every newcomer that the average project gets an audience, which is a strong reason to post.
  4. Gave the Prize a purpose. "Build Something That Matters" (2015), assistive tech and nonprofit challenges (2020) kept it from becoming a gadget beauty contest, and winners like Eyedrivomatic, BYTE and the Braille module gave real social proof (2015; 2023).
  5. Built a small, sold-out flagship event and kept it small on purpose. Supercon guards its "very high signal-to-noise ratio" and has outlasted every other program (tag).
  6. Made room for members to run their own programs: the Square-inch Project, Hacker Channel and Traveling Hacker Box were all member-led.

What they did wrong

  1. Let spam overwhelm the platform for years. By our ID sample, roughly 95% of 2017–19 projects are now deleted. Members complained publicly about webbot spam flooding the global feed in 2022 (Ken Yap), and the creator-status gate didn't arrive until December 2024. Consequence: the global feed, the main discovery surface, was untrustworthy for years.
  2. Paid for likes. The 2016 "$1 per like" round, and community voting more broadly, invited gaming. Readers described "bot accounts" and paid account farms voting in earlier rounds (comment, Jun 2015), and Hackaday conceded the voting algorithm was criticised as "broken" (voting tag).
  3. Shrank the Prize, then ended it with no replacement of similar size (Nov 2023). The "more focused contests" now offer $150 gift cards. The site's JavaScript still references "prize2023" (Hack Chat page source).
  4. Let the weekly ritual die quietly. Hack Chat fell from 43 sessions a year to 3, and after an "extended hiatus" (tag) none has run since March 2025.
  5. Botched the messaging migration (mid-2026). hackaday.io/messages started redirecting to Discord. Members say the in-feed notice did not say old messages would become unreachable, and some found out only by clicking a notification email: "I did not appreciate that existing messages would become inaccessible" (thread, Jul 2026). It also moved an open-source crowd onto a closed platform, which Hackaday's own writers joke about ("thousands of immoral heathens that have joined our Discord server"; Sep 2026). The Discord holds 3,939 members, under 0.4% of the "million" claim.
  6. Vanity metrics frozen in place. The homepage said "over 65,000 members" from 2016 to 2020, while the company's own posts reported 178k–400k, and has shown "6,515 members" for chat since 2021. Unmaintained counters signal neglect.
  7. Roadmap neglect after the 2016 promises (mobile app, UX refresh, BOM tools) and long-standing editor bugs (feedback).
  8. Divesting a sister community badly. Tindie went dark for about two weeks in April 2026 after an unannounced sale, with sellers unable to see orders. The Tindie spokesperson admitted "a year or so on cruise control" (Hackster). Members tied that sale to Hackaday.io's fate: "Playing 'follow the money'" (Paul McClay, Jul 2026).

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Start the platform with a recurring, funded challenge that requires posting on it. A staged season (rounds, small finalist payouts, a live finale) creates months of weekly posting, not a one-off spike.
  2. Use your media or content channel as the acquisition engine. Feature members' work prominently and often; a shot at the front page is a stronger motivator than badges.
  3. Publish honest participation data ("the average project gets 170 views") so newcomers believe they'll be seen.
  4. Pair the online community with a small, sold-out yearly event where online achievements are celebrated in person. The hackable badge and SAO contest are good examples of connecting the two.
  5. Recruit outside experts as judges and live-chat guests to borrow credibility and give regulars a weekly reason to show up.

Avoid these

  1. Don't let the rituals lapse quietly. When a weekly chat or flagship contest ends, members read it as abandonment. Replace it with something of similar weight, or explain the decision openly.
  2. Don't migrate members' data or channels without an explicit export window. Moving DMs to a third-party chat app without saying "your history will become unreachable" burns trust that took a decade to build.
  3. Don't pay for likes or let spam run. Gate posting early (rate limits, creator review). Fix or remove public counters that stop updating; frozen numbers advertise neglect.

Scoring rationale

  • Retention mechanics 3: a strong seasonal contest and weekly chat at peak, but both are gone, and on-site social features are thin.
  • Content engine 4: a daily independent editorial blog, weekly podcast, conference videos and contest coverage. The blog remains excellent even as the platform declines.
  • Member ownership 3: member-run contests and channels existed, but the platform, Prize rules and data stayed company-controlled.
  • Staff investment 3: heavy in 2014–2019 (world tour, community managers, HackadayU); thin on the platform since about 2022, while events and editorial are still funded.
  • Business impact evidence 1: no revenue, retention or sponsor ROI figures; only proxy evidence from sponsorships.
  • Durability 3: 12 years and still online with sold-out events, but its core habits (Prize, Hack Chat, messaging) have been removed.
