Smartisan fans (Chuifen)

Smartisan fans / "Chuifen" (锤粉, "hammer fans"; also 锤友 "hammer friends") and the Smartisan forum (bbs.smartisan.com) · Smartisan Technology (锤子科技), phone business sold to ByteDance in 2019

Peak

2014–2018: a 1.1M-post forum with about 2,000 posts a day (May 2015, Wayback), fan-run viewing parties in 180+ cities (Oct 2016, Sina), and 37,000 people paying 100–1,000 yuan each to hear Luo Yonghao launch a phone at the Bird's Nest (May 2018, a Guinness record; SCMP/Huxiu)

Now

Official spaces are gone. ByteDance stopped Smartisan phone development in Jan 2021 (LatePost), and the forum stopped registrations on 16 Oct 2021, then shut on 15 Nov 2021 with all content made unviewable (Baidu Baike/IT之家). Remnants are a small volunteer-run 锤友论坛, open-source ports of Smartisan OS apps (2026), and Luo Yonghao's personal following, which he now aims at livestream selling and AI products.

How it pays off for the company

Mainly zero-cost marketing and demand: fans paid to attend launches and pre-ordered (30,000+ T1 reservations the night of launch, 2014), evangelized, crowdfunded billboards and ran unpaid viewing parties and shop help. The forum also handled ROM distribution, public betas (众测) and support. Ticket revenue was donated to open-source foundations, so it was brand theatre, not profit. None of this fixed supply-chain, quality or cost problems: about 3M phones sold in total, 400M+ yuan lost a year, about 600M yuan of debt.

TL;DR

  • What it is: "Chuifen" (锤粉, hammer fans) were the followers of Luo Yonghao, a former New Oriental English teacher and internet celebrity, and of the phones his company Smartisan (锤子科技, "Hammer Technology") made from 2012. The company's own space was a Discuz forum, bbs.smartisan.com, from 2013 to 2021. Most of the real community lived in fan-run WeChat/QQ groups, city meetups and Luo's Weibo, and once a year at a ticketed launch event the fans called their "New Year's celebration". [1][4][14]
  • Why people came back: identity and a person, not product mechanics. Fans saw Luo as their "spiritual idol" and "another me" who said what they couldn't. Launch events were pilgrimages, and being mocked by "hammer haters" pushed them closer together. Recognizing a stranger's Smartisan phone or its default ringtone felt like "meeting an old friend". [1][14]
  • What the company fed it: spectacle more than infrastructure. Luo gave 2–3 hour keynotes in rented arenas, which fans paid to attend (4.85M yuan at the Bird's Nest, 2018). He posted constantly on Weibo. The forum offered ROM downloads, public betas and photo contests. Sixth Tone found that Smartisan's engagement with fans had dwindled about two years after its founding. Before 2017, local fan events were "organized by fans themselves, at considerable personal cost". [14][15][20]
  • Biggest mistake: the brand was the founder, and the products kept breaking the promises he made on stage. The T1 shipped about two months late on a 3G-only spec. Five months after launch, Smartisan cut the price by more than 1,000 yuan, though Luo had vowed in 2013, "if it's below 2,500, I'm your grandson". Later models compromised on design, and many fans "became fans, then haters" (粉了又黑). When the money ran out, ByteDance bought the team, then shut the phones (Jan 2021) and the forum (Nov 2021), and deleted ten years of member content. [2][3][18][25]
  • The lesson: a charismatic founder can light a community for nearly free, but loyalty tied to a person and an ideal ("情怀", sentiment) is spent each time the product falls short. Build the community around what members do with the product, and make sure its archive survives the company.

Origin and strategy

Luo announced on Weibo in April 2012 that he was "registering a company to make phones". Before that he was a celebrity: the "Lao Luo quotes" (老罗语录) from his New Oriental classes, the Niubo blog site, and the 2011 smashing of Siemens refrigerators in protest at faulty doors. Many first fans were "Luo fans" (罗粉) who became hammer fans once he built a phone. [1][2]

Smartisan copied Xiaomi's playbook: ship a ROM first, build a following, then sell hardware. Smartisan OS launched on 27 Mar 2013 as a ROM for the Galaxy S2/S3/Note 2, HTC One X, Mi 1/2 and Meizu MX2 [30][31]. The forum was the distribution hub: thread 38 is a flashing tutorial for the Galaxy S III [31]. In May 2015 the forum's boards still included "adapted devices" (适配机型) and per-app feedback boards for the Smartisan calendar, notes, clock, mail and calculator [5].

The strategy was never "community as product engine" in Xiaomi's sense. It was founder as media channel. The NYT wrote in 2015 that for Luo, with 10.6M Weibo followers, "every post is an advertisement" [32]. Luo later said he spent little on marketing and advertising "because we had no money" [1]. Fans took on the marketing work. In 2014 Luo himself said this was a liability: "if, in the end, my personal brand is still bigger than the company brand, I'd consider it a failure", and he promised to "talk less" [17]. He never did.

No named community lead or team appears in any source. The forum ran on official accounts ("锤子科技", "锤子科技解答员", later "SmartisanCare" and "坚果手机") and volunteer moderators [5][11][13].

Trajectory: the forum was mid-sized for Chinese phone brands (about 128K members against Xiaomi's 10M in 2014). Its activity tracked product launches. It peaked in the T1/T2 era (2014–2016). By 2018–2019 the home page carried sponsored content (snail-sleep pillow trials, an office-suite ad, Smartisan store posts about humidifiers), and by 2021 nobody was posting. The offline fandom peaked at the 2018 Bird's Nest event. The supply-chain collapse and layoffs followed in late 2018. [1][3][8][9]

What keeps people coming back

  1. The founder as the product. Fans called Luo a "spiritual idol, an enlightener of idealism", and one said, "when I don't dare to speak up, Luo Yonghao is like my projection, another me." Reading Luo's books, listening to his old lectures and using a Smartisan phone formed "a kind of collective tacit understanding". One fan tattooed the logo on his chest, and Luo begged others not to: "It will make people think we're a cult." [1][14]
  2. Launch night as the annual festival. Sixth Tone called the fans "a guerrilla force... only meeting once or twice a year to watch livestreamed launches, an event they've dubbed their 'New Year's celebration'." A 16-year-old rode the train 11 hours to a launch "like a pilgrimage". [1][14]
  3. Recognition signals and in-group trust. Fans recognized each other by the default "米店" ringtone and the distinctive hardware. One fan who ran a shop said being a hammer friend worked like a built-in Sesame credit score: strangers would pay first or ship first. [1]
  4. Being besieged. Luo's feuds with rivals brought trolls (Luo said rivals sent "tens of thousands of paid commenters"), and buyers who posted praise got piled on. Sixth Tone: "the criticism toward Luo has pushed them closer together." That kind of glue holds a group together but also wears it out. [14][17]
  5. Local chapters run by volunteers. Before 2017, all hammer-friend events were organized by fans at their own cost. Fuzhou's organizer held unboxing meetups for 2–3 years. In 2017 Smartisan's new provincial retail partners were asked to "help local hammer-friend organizers". [2][20]
  6. Forum mechanics (thin). Standard Discuz tools: daily check-in (签到专区), tasks (任务) and medals (勋章), public-beta recruitment for each OS version, "forum invites you to review new phones" programs, monthly "outstanding photo" picks and a graduation-season photo contest. Feedback was pushed off the forum early: from 2016, pinned posts told users to "report bugs through Settings > About > User feedback". [5][8][11][12][13]

There was no visible loop like Xiaomi's weekly build ritual in which forum input demonstrably shipped. Jiemian notes the OS was built around Luo's taste ("if you don't like it, don't use it"), which "filtered for loyal fans" but limited the user base. [30]

The content engine

  • Launch keynotes as entertainment. SCMP described Smartisan as "transforming its annual product launches into variety shows", and Luo got "consumers to pay to hear his sales pitch". Full replays were collected on Bilibili. Ticket income went to open source: about 1M yuan to OpenSSL (2014), OpenResty (2015), OpenSSL and OpenBSD (2016–2018; 212,000 USD confirmed by OpenSSL in Dec 2016). The donations doubled as a brand story. [15][21][22][23]
  • Founder Weibo. This was the main daily content channel. Fans' event posts were amplified by Luo: "When we posted on Weibo, Lao Luo would help retweet, and then everyone joined the group." [2]
  • Official forum content. OS changelogs, feature explainer videos (Big Bang, One Step, Idea Pills, Jun 2017), purchase FAQs, launch replays. [7][12]
  • Member content. Tutorials (one member's OTG and lens guides drew around 100K views), redesigned icons, wallpapers and themes, fan-designed back stickers, "Teacher Luo livestream sticker packs", and photo essays shot on Smartisan phones. In 2021 a member's nostalgic piece, "The silhouette of the T series", was among the few new hot posts. [3][7][8]
  • Paid and sponsored content (late period). Partner product trials (众测) and office-software promos appeared on the 2018–2019 home page. [8][9]

Score, content engine 3/5: world-class launch spectacle and a lively UGC phase, but no steady education or how-to program, and the founder was a single point of failure.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff: no evidence of a dedicated community team. The visible official accounts were 锤子科技, 锤子科技解答员 ("answerer"), SmartisanCare and 坚果手机. Board moderators such as "pozz" and "olpp" ran the Nut board (2015). [11][13]
  • Rules: forum regulations banned "attacking other phone systems and their users", along with commercial spam and doxxing. That rule cut against the founder's own public style. [4]
  • Fan leaders: unpaid and unrecognized by any formal program. Sixth Tone, contrasting the two fandoms: "Unlike Xiaomi, which regularly checks in with chapter heads and supplies them with bundles of freebies, Smartisan's engagement with fans dwindled two years after its founding." A Shanghai leader added: "They would love to have a better relationship with the fans, but they probably don't have the money." [14]
  • Fan-run retail: fan shop owners such as Du Lei got no offline support. When seven or eight shop owners went to headquarters, "the company admitted it was in difficulty, but whether to keep the stores open was their own decision." Volunteers came to train his staff for free. [1]
  • Feedback: public betas (OS 2.0 in 2015, 3.0, 4.0 in 2017, 7.0 in 2019) were recruited on the forum, but bug reports were routed to the in-phone feedback tool. [11][12] No source documents fan ideas that shipped.
  • Score, staff investment 2/5: spectacle at the top, very little paid support underneath.

Growth loops

  • Founder virality, then word of mouth. Weibo posts, feuds and quotable keynotes created awareness for free [32]. The same feuds created a large hater base [14].
  • Fan-funded advertising. Before the 2018 Bird's Nest event, 224 Hefei fans crowdfunded 11,152 yuan for 15 days of billboards ("Lao Luo, just be serious; we'll help you win") [1].
  • Viewing parties and unboxing meetups brought curious locals to see scarce phones [2][20].
  • ROM-first funnel. Smartisan OS on other brands' phones (2013–2016) was meant to convert ROM users into T1 buyers, as MIUI had done for Xiaomi [30][31].
  • What broke the loop: fans struggled to recommend the phones. A Shanghai group leader: "I've been having trouble recommending Smartisan to my friends... for Smartisan, every improvement is negligible." [14]

Evidence of business impact

Hard numbers: about 1.7 billion yuan raised in eight rounds; losses of 462M (2015), 428M (2016) and over 400M yuan (2017); about 3M phones sold across seven models by 2018; 580K units in Q3 2018 [2][28][29]. The Nut Pro sold 1M units in six months in 2017, "more than the previous five years combined" [2]. The company left about 600M yuan of debt [3]. ByteDance paid "hundreds of millions of yuan" (上亿元) for the Nut team and some patent rights in late 2018 [26].

Community-attributable: launch-ticket revenue (about 1M yuan in 2014, 2M+ in 2016, 4.85M in 2018), all donated [21][22][23]. There were 30,000+ launch-night T1 reservations [41], and fans bought heavily ("Buy, just buy. That's the best way to support"), including accessories and luggage [1].

Afterlife: Luo's personal following outlived the company. His first Douyin livestream (1 Apr 2020) sold 910,000 items worth over 110M yuan to 48M viewers. That money went toward the roughly 600M yuan debt, which he says he has now repaid [3][45][47]. The fandom was monetized through Luo, not through Smartisan.

No retention, churn or NPS data was found. Score, business impact evidence 2/5: demand at launches is documented, but the commercial result was failure.

What they did right

  1. Made launches into events people paid for. A 37,000-person, Guinness-recognized phone launch with paid tickets is still unique in tech [15][16]. The donation to open source turned ticket revenue into a values story [21].
  2. Gave the fandom a strong, nameable identity. Hammer fans, hammer friends, the ringtone, the T-shirt worn to an Apple store as "performance art" [1]. Identity this strong produced tattoos, fan-run stores and crowdfunded billboards without any budget [1][14].
  3. Compensated early buyers when cutting prices. After the Oct 2014 T1 price cut, buyers from 18 Oct onward got cash refunds of the difference, and earlier buyers got 800 yuan off the T2 or a 500-yuan store voucher [19]. It limited a betrayal, though it didn't prevent one (forum threads in 2015 still argued that delay vouchers couldn't be used on the Nut phone [11]).
  4. Distinctive, opinionated software that fans still rebuild. OneStep, Big Bang and the Smartisan launcher were open-sourced or cloned, and in 2026 volunteers have ported a dozen Smartisan OS apps. The design taste outlived the company [37].
  5. Public betas run through the forum (OS 2.0 in 2015 drew 30K views and 773 replies) gave invested users early access [11].

What they did wrong

  1. Founder over brand. Luo recognized it in 2014 and said he'd "talk less" [17]. He kept feuding. Fans say his stances "form a battlefield" that the products couldn't escape [2], and outsiders saw "a smooth-talking fat man selling idealism" [1]. Consequence: a hater base, troll attacks on buyers, and a product judged by its founder.
  2. Promises the product couldn't keep. "East hemisphere's best smartphone" (later forced by advertising law to "the world's second-best") [36]. Then came a T1 that shipped about two months after launch on 3G only, with fewer than 1,000 units on day one, a 1,000-yuan price cut, and a lawsuit from a fan [2][18]. A fan wrote: "This is the last time I pay for sentiment." [2] Consequence: churn among the most loyal early buyers.
  3. Starving the volunteer layer. Fan organizers paid their own costs until 2017, and fan stores got no support [1][20]. A rival fan group split off in Shanghai in 2015 [14]. Consequence: brittle local chapters that depended on personal passion.
  4. Turning on your own fans. When fans called the Nut 3 ugly, Luo posted that it "will definitely sell well" and called critics "idiots" the next day. "This time the hammer fans became the target of Luo's sharp tongue." [2]
  5. Letting the forum rot, then deleting it. By 2019 the official space was mostly store promos. ByteDance closed it on 15 Nov 2021, after which nobody could "view previously published content" [3][4]. Ten years of tutorials, ROM guides and member history were lost. Volunteers rebuilt a small replacement within days [24].
  6. The acquisition orphaned the community. ByteDance kept the brand for one more phone (Pro 3, Oct 2019) without Luo. Many fans "didn't even finish the launch video", and launch tickets went from bought to given away [1]. Phones were paused in Jan 2021, and Luo answered a fan's "Nut is gone" with "Mm, good thing" [2][26].

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Turn launches into fan rituals with a date. Smartisan showed people will pay to gather if the event is entertaining and theirs. A fixed annual or seasonal event gives a dispersed fanbase a reason to meet. [14][15]
  2. Give members recognizable signals. Names, sounds and objects ("hammer friend", the ringtone) let members find each other offline and build trust. [1]
  3. Back your volunteer organizers early. The fan energy was there (180 cities). Xiaomi gave chapter heads materials and recognition, while Smartisan left fans to pay. Fund and recognize local hosts from day one. [14][20]
  4. Make good on promises publicly. The T1 compensation scheme was the right instinct. When you break a promise, compensate visibly and quickly. [19]
  5. Let enthusiasts extend the product. Open-sourcing OneStep and Big Bang, and fans porting apps, kept the brand alive after the company died. Give power users hooks, such as ROMs, themes and plugins, that they can own. [37]

Avoid these

  1. Don't let one personality be the community. Personal loyalty doesn't transfer to the product, the company or an acquirer. "Most Smartisan fans are Luo's fans." [14] Cultivate peer-to-peer and member-product ties that would survive the founder.
  2. Don't spend loyalty faster than the product earns it. Each delay, price cut and compromise drew down the 情怀 (sentiment) account until fans were "粉了又黑" (fans, then haters). [2]
  3. Never shut a community without an archive or a handover. Deleting the forum erased its value and pushed members to rebuild from scratch. Export, read-only mode or handing it to volunteers costs little. [4][24]
