TL;DR

  • What it was: Kewpie's first members-only online community, opened on 30 Oct 2012 at dining.kewpie.co.jp. It was a general "everyday cooking" space themed on seasonal vegetables, not on a single product. In Feb 2017 it moved onto eLife's multi-brand LA VIDA platform. It grew to 20,834 members (FY2023) and closed on 28 Jun 2024 (2012 release, eLife case, farewell post). The coordinator's first guess of "still running" is wrong.
  • Why people came back: low-effort daily habits. Members logged what they cooked on a calendar ("Minna no Cooking Diary"), posted a "today's good thing" photo, voted in one-click polls, took monthly vegetable quizzes and answered a monthly talk theme set by a named staff host, "Hana". Points and like-earned medals fed product prize lotteries. Departing members described it as an "oasis" and a neighbourhood chat (farewell comments).
  • What Kewpie fed it: an editorial team of Kewpie employees (the "Ohanashi Dining editorial department") wrote seasonal vegetable columns, monthly special themes and new-product notes. Kewpie also ran product-monitor and sampling campaigns, and a few members-only factory tours and R&D lab cooking sessions (2012 release, 2013 factory tour blog, 2018 lab meetup blog).
  • Biggest mistakes: the community never got a strategic job beyond "dialogue," and it was moved twice without protecting members. The 2017 migration to a shared platform cut reported membership from 12,953 to 8,981 and, by members' own account, made the space harder to find. Kewpie then ran two overlapping communities and closed the smaller one with a month's notice and no migration path.
  • The one lesson: a low-pressure ritual (log today's dinner, share a small good thing, answer this month's question) keeps a loyal core for a decade at very low cost. Without a clear business job and a home of its own, though, it is the first thing cut when the brand consolidates.

What the community is

  • Official space, era 1 (30 Oct 2012 to about Jan 2017): a member site at dining.kewpie.co.jp built by Kewpie. It launched with six areas (2012 release, Nihon Shokuryo Shimbun):
    1. Shun Yasai Hiroba (seasonal vegetable square): growing regions, how to choose produce, how to eat it, with member tips invited.
    2. Minna no Cooking Diary: members' daily dishes published on a calendar.
    3. Minna no Kinenbi Dining (anniversary tables): photos of birthday and anniversary meals.
    4. Henshūbu dayori: news from an editorial team "made up of Kewpie employees", including new-product introductions by developers and food-education topics.
    5. My Page: saved recipes from Kewpie's recipe site and recommendations.
    6. Campaigns: points earned by commenting and posting, spent on prize draws and members-only factory tours. By 2016 the menu had added Kyō no ii koto ("today's good thing"), One-click survey polls and a Basil Garden corner (basil2016.php; its content was not archived) (Wayback, Aug 2016).
  • Official space, era 2 (1 Feb 2017 to 28 Jun 2024): community.lavida.jp/kewpie, a "brand page" on LA VIDA, eLife Inc.'s shared community and word-of-mouth platform, which also hosts Fancl, Suntory, JAL and other brands. The client of record was To Ad Kewpie, the Kewpie group's in-house ad agency (eLife case). The launch banner told members "moving from the old site" what had changed (Wayback, Feb 2017). Sections were Special Content (a monthly theme), Seasonal Vegetable Square, Today's Good Thing, Cooking Diary, One-click Survey, Everyone's Q&A, Editorial News and Campaigns. LA VIDA supplied cross-brand points and badges (about page snippet).
  • Shutdown: announced on 3 Jun 2024. The community ended on 28 Jun 2024 and stayed readable until 30 Sep 2024. The farewell post sent members to Hi! kewpie, the Kewpie Mayonnaise Fan Club, LINE, X and Instagram (farewell post). From FY2024 Kewpie's stakeholder-dialogue table lists only the fan club (live page). The LA VIDA platform itself carried on with other brands. Its community site was taken offline on 21 Oct 2026 for an emergency security fix (LA VIDA notice via r.jina.ai).
  • Unofficial spaces: none found. The only outside footprint is sweepstakes blogs, prize-aggregator listings (Koubo, mechatoku) and an Instagram hashtag, #おはなしダイニング. There is no subreddit, and Reddit is irrelevant for this Japanese audience.
  • Sibling: the much larger Kewpie Mayonnaise Fan Club, founded in 2014, ran in parallel for ten years.

Origin and strategy

Kewpie launched Ohanashi Dining ("chat dining") in Oct 2012 to "deepen dialogue with customers." The goal was a "warm community where you can picture the smiles of the people who cooked and the people who ate," built by Kewpie and members "bringing tips and ideas together" (2012 release). The CSR reports from 2013 to 2016 filed it under "dialogue on the website," next to the customer-service centre, and described it as a two-way community where customers interact with each other (CSR 2013 p50, CSR 2016 p26).

The strategic logic is implicit but clear. Kewpie's dressings and mayonnaise are eaten mostly on vegetables, and its corporate identity centres on salads and vegetables. A community that makes people cook and enjoy more vegetables grows the category without pushing products. To Ad Kewpie told a 2023 peer meeting that it is "a community that does not usually put our products forward" (eLife off-site meeting, Apr 2023). The member terms also allowed member data to be used for "analysis and research for site operation, service improvement, product development and marketing," and for surveys (member terms, Wayback 2012).

I found no named internal champion. The 2017 move to LA VIDA suggests a cost-saving shift from a custom build to a rented, shared platform; that is an inference. Two years later Kewpie built its mayo-specific fan club with a different vendor (QON's Beach) and a much sharper strategy. From then on Ohanashi Dining was the junior community.

Trajectory. Growth was slow and steady: roughly +1,500 to +3,000 registrations a year, with a 31% drop at the 2017 platform move. The fan club, by contrast, added 20,000 to 40,000 a year. The rise after 2020 likely reflects COVID home cooking (one member says remote work brought her back) and campaign sign-ups (Kewpie said the 2023 Paysanne monitor campaign "attracted many new members"). The active core was small. Ordinary content drew hundreds to low thousands of views, and only 13 people reached the higher medal tiers in 2022. Prize-linked posts drew about 1,000 to 1,500 comments, so the prize-motivated audience was about 5–7% of registrations (estimate).

What keeps people coming back

  1. Tiny daily rituals with no skill bar. The cooking diary turned "what I made tonight" into a public calendar, and "today's good thing" invited any small happy moment: plum blossoms, a souvenir snack, the vegetable harvest (Wayback, Feb 2017). One member cooked "4–5 times a week, looking at the recipes, to keep my mind sharp." Another remembered her daughter's first gyoza being chosen as the monthly cover image (farewell comments).
  2. A monthly editorial calendar. Each month brought a Special Content theme ("new life and cooking," "cooking with mayonnaise," "parent-and-child cooking"), a seasonal vegetable column, polls and seasonal days such as Vegetable Day (31 Aug), Mayonnaise Day (1 Mar) and the winter solstice (examples, 2, 3). Two runs of a vegetable quiz series (to at least round 14 by Dec 2019, then restarted in Dec 2022 and reaching round 18) were among the most praised features at closure (quiz round 1).
  3. A named host the members named. The staff "navigator" was nicknamed by member vote in Mar 2017 (1,377 entries): "Hana." She signed posts and took part in threads until the end (nickname post). Farewell comments thank "Hana-san and all the staff" by name.
  4. Peer warmth. Members describe a neighbourly, judgment-free tone: "a place where I could join casually with one line or a long text." Nothing more was needed than "loving Kewpie products in common" to open up (farewell comments).
  5. Extrinsic hooks. Points from posting and commenting bought entries into prize draws. The prizes were product sets and, in the early years, factory tours. Likes earned medals (15 types × 3 tiers), and silver and gold holders got a yearly gift. Kewpie admitted that many members didn't know the medals existed, and it kept the criteria secret ("we cannot answer 'how many more until a medal?'") (medal post). Most campaigns were "comment on this article to enter a draw for 10 people." That kept comment counts high but drew sweepstakes regulars, one of whom noted that "the survey for entering the prize draw was the same every month."
  6. Rare IRL access. A members-only tour of the Goka factory, with a tasting of new products (27 Aug 2013), and a "follower-only meetup" with a cooking class taught by R&D staff at Kewpie's Sengawa Kewport lab (24 May 2018, described as the first such meetup) (2013 blog, 2018 blog, Instagram hashtag mirror). Members were also pointed to Kewpie's "Family Waku-Waku Cooking" kids' classes, though these were not exclusive to members (2020 notice).

Not present: no streaks, levels, certification or ambassador tier, no member-created sub-groups (the rules even banned calls for offline meetups), and no product co-creation.

The content engine

  • Company-made: written by a small editorial team of Kewpie employees under To Ad Kewpie, on a steady monthly cadence. The output was one seasonal-vegetable article (history, nutrition, choosing, storing, plus a quiz), one or two special-theme articles, two or three one-click polls with results write-ups, "keepsake" basics (how to boil eggs, how to cut vegetables), new-product introductions, and one or two prize or monitor campaigns (vegetable article examples, poll results). In 2023 there was even an "AI dressing diagnosis" quiz (snippet). There was no video, no live sessions and no newsletter beyond campaign and quiz emails.
  • Member-made: dinner photos in the diary, good-thing photos, Q&A threads ("how do you store dressing?", "vegetables for beer pickles?") and campaign entries such as overhead food photos and recipe ideas. Staff recycled them into roundup posts ("Your egg sandwiches!") and the monthly cover (roundup snippet). The 2017 "Mayonnaise Magic" recipe contest was the closest thing to a member-content programme (snippet).
  • Product tie-in done softly: monitor campaigns sent members new products to cook with and post about, for example Kewpie Half with aemono seasonings (2017) and a 30-person monitor for Paysanne Salad Dressing, which To Ad Kewpie presented as a success in 2023. In the 2023 case the content led with the dish, not the bottle. Peer community managers praised that approach, and Kewpie said it reactivated lurkers and drew new members (eLife off-site, Apr 2023, 2017 monitor blog). Members remembered it at closure: "I'd never have met Paysanne salad if I hadn't learned it here… now my family makes it again and again."

People, programs and governance

  • Team: "Ohanashi Dining editorial department" (Kewpie employees) plus the community staff persona Hana. It was operated by To Ad Kewpie on eLife's LA VIDA service from 2017. The "LA VIDA Ohanashi Dining secretariat" handled campaigns. Team size is not disclosed. To Ad Kewpie joined eLife's community-manager peer "off-site meetings," moderated by Shinya Mizuno, formerly community lead at Kagome's &KAGOME (eLife off-site).
  • Rules: Kewpie group employees could not join. Commercial activity, political or religious posts, personal information, petitions and calls for offline meetups were banned. Member data could be used for marketing and product development (terms, 2012).
  • Ambassador or superuser programme: none. The medal gift to about a dozen top contributors a year was the only recognition tier.
  • Feedback loop: polls on new products ("which of the spring 2024 pasta sauces do you want to try?"), surveys attached to every prize entry, and monitor reviews. I found no published case of a product or ad changing because of Ohanashi Dining, in contrast to the fan club's "Mayo Sauté" insight.

Growth loops

  • Prize campaigns syndicated by aggregators. Koubo, Koubo Guide and mechatoku listed nearly every Ohanashi Dining giveaway (Koubo 2023, mechatoku 2024), which pulled in sweepstakes hobbyists.
  • LA VIDA cross-traffic. Users of other LA VIDA brand communities saw Kewpie activity in shared feeds and profiles ("posted to Kewpie Community Ohanashi Dining" appears on LA VIDA profiles). This was likely the main post-2017 acquisition channel (inference).
  • Monitor campaigns for new products, which Kewpie said brought in many new members (2023).
  • Weak spots: there was no SEO strategy. On the shared domain, Kewpie's brand content was mixed in with other companies'. There was no social amplification of member posts and no referral mechanic.

Evidence of business impact

  • Hard evidence: none. Kewpie published only member counts, as a line item under "customer dialogue" in its CSR and sustainability reports (FY2015–FY2023, table above).
  • Company claims: the Paysanne Salad Dressing monitor campaign generated "a great deal of interest… submissions from members who have had little action in the past… many new members" (eLife off-site, Apr 2023).
  • Member self-reports (in prize-incentivised comments, so read them generously): learning new uses for dressing and mayo, more vegetable repertoire, trying products first met in campaigns, and "I'll keep using Kewpie." One said the information "even helped my investment decisions."
  • Revealed value: Kewpie closed it in 2024 while keeping the mayo fan club and building kewpie ID and Hi! kewpie (launched Sep 2022; 30k IDs in its first year) (Hi! kewpie release, kewpie ID 30k). That implies management saw more value in a first-party-data membership plus one flagship brand community. This is an inference; no closure reason was given.

What they did right

  1. Built the habit on the customer's life, not the product. Seasonal vegetables, dinner logs and small good things suited Kewpie's salad-and-vegetable positioning and gave members something to post every day. That carried an 11.7-year run with organic growth to about 21k.
  2. A consistent, member-named host. Letting members name the staff persona in 2017 (1,377 entries) created attachment that shows in the farewell comments seven years later.
  3. Soft-sell product integration. Monitor campaigns that led with the dish produced real adoption stories (Paysanne salad) and reactivated lurkers, and a peer panel of community managers singled them out as a model.
  4. An education format with a light game layer. The vegetable quiz series and "keepsake" basics articles were the most-praised content at closure, which shows that learning can retain people as well as chat does.
  5. A graceful close. Kewpie gave about four weeks' notice, posted a thank-you campaign that collected 1,522 comments, kept the archive readable for three months and pointed members to successor channels.

What they did wrong

  1. The platform move shed members and identity. Moving from its own site to LA VIDA in Feb 2017 cut reported members from 12,953 to 8,981 (−31%) within the fiscal year (CSR 2017, CSR 2018). A long-time member said that once it became LA VIDA and "started handling things other than Kewpie, it got a bit confusing, so I've been visiting less." She added that LA VIDA's campaign pages didn't even show deadlines (farewell comment, 28 Jun 2024). Kewpie later repeated the same mistake with the fan club's 2025 migration, where membership fell by about 94%.
  2. Two overlapping communities, with the smaller one starved. From 2014 the mayo fan club got the strategy, executive champion and press. Ohanashi Dining stayed a generalist with no business question to answer, and it lost the consolidation.
  3. Recognition mechanics nobody knew about. Medals had secret criteria, many members had never heard of them, and only 13 people reached the gift tier in 2022. That gave no status or aspiration to the wider base.
  4. Prize dependence. Most participation calls were "comment for a chance to win." That inflates numbers (about 1,000 to 1,500 comments on prize posts against hundreds of views on ordinary content) and trains members to show up for draws. Some noticed the boilerplate ("the survey was the same every month").
  5. An abrupt end with no migration path. Members were told on 3 Jun 2024 that it would end on 28 Jun. Nothing carried over: diary history, medals and friendships were all lost. Several members wrote that they were hoping to see "members I stopped seeing along the way" before it closed. Members said they would keep buying, but the social graph was dropped.
  6. No measurement. In 12 years Kewpie published no engagement, purchase or retention evidence, which left the community defenceless in a portfolio review.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Give members a daily log that takes 30 seconds (here, "what I cooked" and "today's good thing"). Low-skill, life-adjacent posting keeps a core for years.
  2. Run a monthly editorial calendar tied to the seasons and holidays your product lives in, with one quiz or learning hook each month. Education was the most-praised feature at closure.
  3. Let members name the host persona and keep that persona stable. It cost almost nothing and created lasting attachment.
  4. Integrate products through use cases, not ads: sample the product to members with a dish or task to make, then feature their results.
  5. When you close, close gracefully: give notice, hold a thank-you thread, keep a read-only archive and point people somewhere specific.

Avoid these

  1. Don't rent a shared, multi-brand platform if your community's value is its own identity. Moving here cost about 31% of members and made the space feel "confusing." Every migration should carry over accounts, history and status.
  2. Don't run a community without a business job and a metric. A "dialogue" line item in a CSR report won't survive a portfolio review against a sharper sibling.
  3. Don't make recognition secret or prize-driven. Hidden medal criteria and comment-to-win draws inflate activity without building status or ownership.

Score justifications

  • Retention mechanics 2: pleasant rituals (diary, monthly theme, quizzes) but weak progression. The medals were hidden, and there were no streaks, cohorts or tiers.
  • Content engine 3: a reliable monthly staff editorial with seasonal vegetables, quizzes, polls and monitors over 11 years, but text-only and low-reach.
  • Member ownership 2: members posted and voted. They could not create groups, organise meetups (banned) or co-create products.
  • Staff investment 3: an employee editorial team, a host persona, a few IRL events and two platform builds, but no executive champion and outsourced tooling from 2017.
  • Business impact evidence 1: only member counts in CSR tables, plus one anecdotal campaign claim.
  • Durability 3: almost 12 years of operation and steady growth, ended by consolidation rather than collapse.
