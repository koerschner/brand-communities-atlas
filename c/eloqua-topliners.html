TL;DR

  • What it is. Topliners is Eloqua's customer community. It launched on Jive in January 2011 and was open to anyone, not just customers. It used three idea-to-execution tabs ("Imagine It, See It, Do It"), later joined by Code It and Know It. Since July 2023 it has lived on as the Eloqua category inside Oracle's all-products Cloud Customer Connect forum.
  • Why people came back. At its 2011-2014 peak, people came for peer answers to hard platform questions, a tightly built status system, and ties to real life. Bunchball points, levels (up to "Ninja", then "Rock Star"), leaderboards and badges were wired to things that mattered: Eloqua University certification, attending Eloqua Experience, and Markie Award wins. Release notes were gated inside a customer-only "Eloqua Insiders" group, and members planned meetups at the conference.
  • How the company fed it. Eloqua had one strong champion, Heather Foeh ("Director of Customer Culture", a former customer). She measured the community against renewals in CRM, used the corporate content machine and the annual conference to feed it, and made every certification and award show up on members' profiles.
  • Biggest mistake. After the acquisition, Oracle moved the community between platforms four times: Eloqua Jive, then Oracle Technology Network Jive (Feb 2015), then Vanilla (around 2021), then Cloud Customer Connect (Jul 2023). The first move wiped members' points, connections and notifications and buried Eloqua talk in the all-Oracle activity stream. The champion had left in 2014 and the conference was folded into Oracle-wide events. My count of active threads (about 10 a month in late 2026) against roughly 280-300 new discussions a month in 2011-2013 suggests activity fell by around 95%.
  • The one lesson. A community's reputation system and identity are product assets. Tie them to revenue metrics so they survive budget reviews, and never migrate a community in a way that resets members' status or merges their space into someone else's.

What the community is

  • Owned forum and support community. Topliners went live on 18 January 2011 at topliners.com / topliners.eloqua.com, built on Jive's customer-support community platform. It was "open to anyone who wants to share and learn best practices -- not just Eloqua customers" (launch release). The name comes from "top-line revenue." The structure followed a marketer's workflow: Imagine It (strategy), See It (customer success stories), Do It (how-to and support), then Code It for developers (Foeh interview, 2012) and Know It for release and product information (Perficient, Sept 2013; Relationship One).
  • Groups. The 2012 homepage shows groups such as Eloqua Advocates, Eloqua 9 Clients, Eloqua Champs - Demand Generation, and Eloqua Users with Salesforce Integration (Wayback, Dec 2012). It also had the customer-only Eloqua Insiders group, which still gated the Eloqua Release Center years later (Oracle docs), and an Eloqua System Status blog (Pedowitz).
  • AppCloud marketplace. In September 2013 Oracle moved the Eloqua AppCloud app catalog into Topliners (CMSWire; Ad Age).
  • Ideas portal. "Dream It" let members submit and vote on feature ideas. It was renamed the Eloqua Idea Lab after the 2023 move (Oracle, Aug 2023).
  • In-person spaces. These included the annual Eloqua Experience conference with the Markie Awards (Markies began in 2007; Adrian Chang) and city Eloqua User Groups (EUGs), each with its own Topliners page (Relationship One). Some EUGs were run by partners or users.
  • Today. The Eloqua category inside Oracle Cloud Customer Connect was created on 24 July 2023 (Vanilla API via r.jina.ai). Partners still call it "the new Topliners" (Marketing Cube).
  • Unofficial spaces. Essentially none. r/Eloqua-type subreddits on Arctic Shift have 1-2 subscribers and under 30 posts (Arctic Shift subreddit search, Nov 2026), and Hacker News has no relevant threads. The real unofficial layer is the partner ecosystem (Relationship One, Marketing Cube, Tegrita), which runs user groups and webinar series of its own.

Origin and strategy

  • Champion. Heather Foeh was an Eloqua customer before joining in 2008. As "Director of Customer Culture" she was "responsible for nurturing and communicating with Eloqua's amazing advocates, as well as managing ... Topliners" (Jive spotlight, Oct 2012; Foeh interview). She calls launching Topliners "one of the proudest moments in my career" (her site). She held Eloqua and Oracle roles from 2008 to 2014 (PRWeb).
  • Strategic framing. CEO Joe Payne pitched it as harvesting "a massive amount of ingenuity in our customer base." Jive's sales chief described it as bringing "together customer support, social business and SEO... support is marketing" (launch release). It also fit Eloqua's broader content-marketing identity (Joe Chernov's content team, the "It's All About Revenue" blog), which was building toward the August 2012 IPO (Washington Post).
  • Business model link. The community's job was subscription renewal. Foeh wrote in August 2012: "it all comes down to revenue... For us, this comes down to renewal rates... I look at it quarterly." She pushed Topliners usernames into Salesforce contact records through the Jive Cloud Connector so she could compare renewal rates for accounts with and without community members. Her stated goal was "make sure that every one of your customer accounts has at least one user on your community," with targeted outreach to accounts that had none (Eloqua blog, 10 Aug 2012).
  • Secondary goals. Support deflection, and acquisition: "many prospects actually join Topliners first before becoming an Eloqua customer" (Foeh interview).

Trajectory. Activity grew fast in 2011 and plateaued in early 2012. Gamification lifted it from July 2012, and it peaked around 2012-2014. Member counts kept rising after 2015 only because Oracle widened the scope to all its marketing products. On every Eloqua-specific measure the trend has been downhill since. My estimate is that the roughly 10 active threads a month in late 2026 compare with roughly 280-300 new discussions a month in 2011-2013, a fall of about 95%. That rests on two different metrics, so treat it as an estimate. Comments per discussion today are about 2.6 (9,922 / 3,761). For comparison, Marketo's community claimed 60,000 members in 2016 (Marketo Nation entry in this dataroom), about twice Topliners' 30,000 across all Oracle Marketing Cloud products.

What keeps people coming back

  1. A status system tied to real achievements. Bunchball's Nitro (through Jive's Advanced Gamification Module) arrived in July 2012 with "newsfeeds, points, badges, challenges and leaderboards." Members leveled up "through the ranks to ultimately become a Topliners Rock Star." Eloqua had to add the Rock Star level "because so many people reached the previous top level, 'Ninja'" (Bunchball release). The design detail that mattered: the most points came from completing Eloqua University Masters certification, and special badges went to conference attendees and Markie finalists and winners. That made the community the public record of a member's career progress. Badges were also built to be shared on LinkedIn (Bunchball case). Later, badges were uploaded about monthly for Masters, each of the five B2B "tenets", and Luminary status (member thread).
  2. Points from peer feedback and nearby rivals. Points rewarded "contribution quality, based on peer feedback." Leaderboards were built as "mini communities" that showed each member who sat just above and below them (Bunchball case). The December 2012 homepage shows how it played out: "Amused that in a few moments, I will be ahead of stuart ormiston in points on Topliners! Bring it on, UK!" (Wayback). Foeh said the lift held: "It's not like it took a dip after the newness wore off" (Ragan).
  3. Rituals around the conference. Threads like "How do you pronounce your name? EE12" got members arranging to meet at Eloqua Experience. After the event, session decks went into the Insiders group (Foeh interview). "Complete this sentence: My favorite thing about Eloqua Experience 2012 was..." drew 57 replies (Wayback). Members "arrange[d] real-world meet-ups at industry events" (Bunchball).
  4. Shared identity. Members called themselves "Topliners" and the forum their "home away from home" (TrustRadius, 2014). Foeh's reading was that "customers are able to connect quickly... because they already have something in common – the Eloqua platform — and they experience the same common hurdles."
  5. Exclusive and useful information. Release notes, the E9-to-E10 side-by-side guide, and the Release Center sat inside the customer-only Insiders group (Modern Marketing Today; Oracle docs). System status lived there too, so admins had to visit routinely.
  6. Local user groups. These carried the network offline. One SoCal user-turned-consultant wrote: "Members email each other for advice all the time." By 2016, Relationship One alone ran user groups in seven metros (Relationship One, May 2016).

Switching costs. Members' accumulated reputation and certification badges lived on Topliners. Oracle's 2015 migration effectively destroyed that asset (see below).

The content engine

  • Education. Eloqua University offered courses, "Power Hours" and the Masters and Luminary certifications, all linked to community badges (Bunchball). Certification was gated to customers with an education pass (member thread, 2014) and cost about $2,600 a year by 2022 (Ledger Bennett). The Luminary required "author[ing] a thought-leadership article" (LEAD2CRM), a cert requirement that also produced community content.
  • Product documentation inside the community. The 2012 feed shows an "Eloqua Documentation" account posting best practices into product-documentation spaces (Wayback). Code It held the developer resource guide (REST, Bulk and SOAP APIs).
  • Recurring formats under Oracle. These included Customer Briefing / Product Series webinars (2016-, e.g. "Habits of Eloqua Legends", 2020), release briefings and roadmap updates by product leaders (replay page; roadmap), and Tip Tuesday, a weekly staff post that invited members to submit their own tips (2022; example). The 2023 "Eloqua Wisdom" best-practice series came from an Oracle staffer (LinkedIn).
  • Member content. Success stories went in See It (for example, ZoomInfo's renewal-rate campaign; Modern Marketing Today), along with crowd-sourced threads like "What would you do differently if you got a clean instance of Eloqua?" Members were rewarded through points and peer ratings. I found no evidence of a formal template or UGC program beyond that.

People, programs and governance

  • Team. Early on, a small team led by Foeh. Later Oracle "Customer Programs" staff ran it (Adrian Chang hosted the 2015 migration and the 2016 upgrade), along with product managers and support engineers who answer in the forum today. No team size has been published.
  • Superusers. The gamified ranks (Ninja, Rock Star) and the Top 10 leaderboard served as the superuser program. One staffer posted that he "is slowly being pushed out of the Top 10 Topliners and that is a good thing" (Wayback). In 2015 an "Eloqua User Group program" relaunched in beta (RSS mirror), and threads appeared on a "Rewards and Recognition Program" and on "how to handle previous Top Participants" after the migration (OTN thread incoming links). Today Cloud Customer Connect runs an all-Oracle reputation ladder (medals, crowns, "Top Thinkers", Hall of Fame; home page) with nothing specific to Eloqua.
  • Moderation. As engagement rose, members became "more self-moderating and mutually helpful," so moderators spent less time on upkeep (Bunchball). On Oracle's shared platform, posts went into pre-moderation queues. One member asked "How is this feature ever going to scale?" (OTN thread). Customer Connect now asks question authors to mark Yes or No on whether they were answered (category page).
  • Feedback loop. Members raised feature requests through Dream It / Idea Lab. Staff point people there even from Tip Tuesday threads (example). I found no published idea-to-shipped statistics.

Growth loops

  • Open-to-prospects SEO and proof. Unlike most customer communities, Topliners was public and free to anyone. A Brady Worldwide marketer recalls: "When we were scoping out Eloqua... I was told about a new online community that was open to the public... A month or so later we signed a contract" (one-year release). Eloqua said prospects "regularly cite Topliners as a reason for choosing Eloqua" (Bunchball).
  • Account seeding. Account managers, support and email templates targeted accounts with no community user (Foeh blog).
  • Conference and awards. The Markies collected public case studies and peer voting (3,000+ People's Choice voters in 2013). Winners became advocates and speakers at user groups (Relationship One recap).
  • Partners. Agencies such as Relationship One and Marketing Cube ran user groups and pointed clients to Topliners. They still publish "how to access the new Topliners" guides.

Evidence of business impact

Most of the evidence is company and vendor claims. They come from a gamification vendor's press release and case study, and the effect is correlational:

  • Renewals. "82 percent of renewing customers have at least one user on Topliners, while Topliners participation is far lower among non-renewing customers." In surveys, customers cited "their ability to belong to Topliners as a major reason for renewing" (Bunchball, Mar 2013). Engaged accounts are likelier both to join and to renew, so selection bias is obvious. Foeh herself framed the goal as grabbing "just a bit of the renewal causation credit."
  • Support deflection. "For every 100 conversations started in the Topliners Do It section, they save 40 phone calls." At about 300 conversations a month, that is roughly 120 calls saved monthly (same source). That is modest in absolute terms.
  • Engagement. A sustained 55% increase in average active users after gamification (Bunchball case).
  • Hard evidence: none. Searches of Eloqua's IPO prospectus turned up no community metrics. No academic study was found (OpenAlex budget was exhausted; Topliners shows up only as a cited example in gamification statistics roundups such as Yu-kai Chou's). Post-2013 Oracle has published no figures.

What they did right

  1. They measured the community against renewals from year one, feeding community identity into CRM and reporting quarterly (Foeh blog). That gave the community a revenue story long before "community-led growth" was a phrase.
  2. Gamification tied to real-world credentials, not just posting volume: certifications, conference attendance and Markie awards earned the most visible badges, and quality points came from peer ratings. The result was a sustained 55% lift and a ceiling raised from Ninja to Rock Star (Bunchball).
  3. Open to prospects and non-customers, which turned the community into a sales asset and an SEO surface (launch release; Brady quote above).
  4. Information architecture built around the job, not the product: Imagine, See, Do, then Code and Know. Outsiders praised it as "community done right" (Perficient).
  5. A customer-turned-community-lead with a title about culture, not support (Jive spotlight).
  6. Online and offline loops reinforced each other: the conference fed threads, threads arranged meetups, and decks came back to the Insiders group.

What they did wrong

  1. Destructive platform migrations (2015, ~2021, 2023). On 1 February 2015 Topliners was moved onto Oracle Technology Network's shared Jive instance with Oracle single sign-on. Members reported: "I've lost all my connections, my settings, what I was following... Worst off I lost all my points and content that I'd published!" (13 likes). They asked "where is the Topliners leaderboard?" and found the activity stream flooded with non-Eloqua Oracle posts (OTN feedback thread). Notifications didn't carry over (Definitive Results, Mar 2015). By July 2016 Oracle conceded: "You can't find content. The search function isn't consistent. You scroll through content that is irrelevant" (upgrade webinar). Another move followed around 2021 ("Topliners is on the Move!", snippet). In July 2023 came the final fold into Cloud Customer Connect, which launched with commenting disabled ("you may not yet be able to comment on any posts"; Oracle) and renamed Dream It. Consequence: the reputation capital behind the 2012-14 flywheel was wiped, and the Eloqua identity was diluted.
  2. The champion left and the conference was folded in. Foeh left Oracle in 2014 (PRWeb). Eloqua Experience skipped 2014 and became Oracle's Modern Marketing Experience and then Modern Customer Experience. The Markies widened to other Oracle products from 2014 (Adrian Chang), and by 2023 they were a dinner at CloudWorld (Marketing Cube). The in-person ritual that powered the online one disappeared.
  3. Scope creep. "Topliners was originally just for Eloqua, but Oracle eventually combined multiple support networks in one place" (Pedowitz), adding Responsys, CrowdTwist, Infinity, Maxymiser and Unity. CrowdTwist's section was then shut in February 2023 (category page snippet).
  4. Gating and price. Certification, the main points source, needed a paid education pass costing about $2,600 a year, which narrowed who could climb the ladder (Ledger Bennett). The Release Center needed Insiders-group approval, then an Oracle ID with "areas of interest" registration.
  5. Product momentum. The community's decline tracks Eloqua's own. Reviewers wrote "Eloqua used to be a leader" (TrustRadius, 2018), and trackers show roughly 3,500-5,200 companies using it (Datanyze; TechnologyChecker). A community can't outgrow a stagnating product.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Wire community identity into your CRM and report renewal rates for member vs non-member accounts every quarter, with a goal of at least one active member per account. It is the cheapest way to make a community's value legible to finance.
  2. Award your biggest badges for off-forum achievements (certification, event attendance, customer awards, case studies), and score posts on peer-rated quality. The community then becomes the public record of a member's professional progress, which is far stickier than post counts.
  3. Show members the people just above and below them on the leaderboard, rather than one global top-10 that most people can never reach.
  4. Leave the community open to prospects and searchable. Real peer answers are your best sales collateral and SEO.
  5. Organize spaces around the user's job (imagine, see examples, do, build) rather than your org chart, and loop your flagship event through the forum before, during and after it.

Avoid these

  1. Never migrate in a way that resets reputation, connections or notifications, or that merges a tight community into a bigger generic one. Carry points over, keep the community's own activity stream and name, and don't launch with commenting turned off.
  2. Don't let the community depend on one champion and one event. When Foeh left and Eloqua Experience was folded into Oracle-wide events, nothing replaced them.
  3. Don't put the main status ladder behind a paywall (an education pass of roughly $2,600 a year) if you want broad participation.
