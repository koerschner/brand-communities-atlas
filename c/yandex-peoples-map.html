How it pays off for the company

TL;DR

  • What it is: a free web editor (n.maps.yandex.ru, launched April 8, 2010) where anyone with a Yandex account draws roads, buildings, addresses, entrances, transit routes and businesses. Since April 2015, moderated edits go straight into Yandex Maps, Navigator and the company's other location services. Around the editor sits an owned community: volunteer moderators and experts, a club forum, chats, an annual award, meetups and frequent group editing drives. (Habr, 2015; Cyclowiki)
  • Why people come back: they see their own town on a map tens of millions of people use. They climb a visible ladder (expert, then moderator, per map layer and per territory) and collect badges for edit counts, years of service, events and yearly titles. Yandex also releases new aerial imagery and immediately turns each release into a timed group editing drive with live counters and leaderboards. Retention at the core is unusually durable: of the 52,500 people who edited in the year to April 2026, about 600 had started in 2010 and 3,300 before 2015 (Telegram, Apr 8 2026).
  • What Yandex feeds it: a staff team that publishes rules, imagery and panorama releases, recaps and awards nearly every week. The team also gives away merchandise and gifts, ran office meetups in Moscow, St Petersburg, Kazan, Minsk, Yekaterinburg, Nizhny Novgorod, Saratov and Krasnodar (2011–2020), and keeps adding new layers and new kinds of objects for people to map.
  • Biggest mistake: the April 2015 relaunch. Yandex merged the People's Map into Yandex Maps and imported old snapshots of objects (some from December 2014). Descriptions, photos and edit history were dropped, and the result simplified or erased months of volunteers' work. Comments under the announcement read "Years of volunteering down the drain!" (Club, 2015). Close behind are moving the community's home four times (ya.ru, the Yandex blog club, Yandex Q, VK) and a club rule that forbids posting that you are leaving the project.
  • The lesson: a contribution community lasts when the company keeps handing members new "jobs" (layers, imagery, theme weeks) and new status, and turns them into time-boxed social events. Yandex's closed licence and top-down product decisions cost it goodwill with the open-data crowd, but did not stop the community. Recognition and the reach of a dominant local map were enough.

What the community is

The People's Map (Narodnaya Karta, abbreviated NYaK in the community; English name Yandex Map Editor) is Yandex's crowdsourced editor for the base map of Yandex Maps. Edits are drawn on satellite and aerial imagery bought by Yandex, with Yandex panoramas and the members' own road photos as further sources. Every edit is checked twice: first by a volunteer "people's moderator" or a Yandex employee, then by Yandex's cartographic production department, before it is published to Maps, Navigator, Transport, Taxi and other services (Cyclowiki; Yandex Help). In 2016 publication took 7–10 days on average (Club, 2016).

Status today: active and staffed. The channel posts several times a week. In September–October 2026 Yandex opened its HD road layer to volunteer experts, ran a two-week group edit in Samarkand with 97 editors, and updated the rules twice (Telegram, Sep–Oct 2026).

Origin and strategy

  • Why Yandex built it. "At the time Yandex.Maps depended entirely on map data suppliers. By launching the People's Map we wanted to give people the chance to influence the completeness and quality of the data themselves" (Habr, Yandex Maps team, Apr 8 2015). Russian towns outside the big cities had no usable digital maps. The cartographers Yandex profiled in 2015 mapped places like Vyshny Volochyok and Donskoy "practically alone", and 14 of 18 town maps Yandex added in one update were mostly community-drawn (Yandex blog, Jan 19 2015).
  • Phase 1 (2010–2015): a separate layer. On launch day, users drew 17,415 buildings and 35,000 km of roads across 1,856 cities (Habr, Apr 9 2010). After one year the map had 2.1M objects (Cyclowiki). From March 2012 the data appeared as a fourth layer on Yandex.Maps and filled in where Yandex's own coverage was missing.
  • Phase 2 (April 8, 2015 onward): the editor of Yandex Maps. The two datasets were merged, routing attributes were added, and the People's Map "is now the data editor of Yandex.Maps" (Club, 2015). Yandex N.V.'s 2015 annual report says: "In 2015 we integrated our Public Map, a crowd-sourced service that presents user-generated local maps and data, into our main Maps as a map editor feature" (SEC 20-F, filed Mar 2016).
  • Business model link. Volunteer data feeds navigation (Navigator), ride-hailing (Taxi), transit routing and delivery. Group edits are "often run to collect data for other Yandex services ... ahead of launches in these cities", as with Tbilisi and Yerevan before Yandex Taxi arrived. Theme weeks gather specific object types that will soon be used in the company's products: speed limits, traffic lights, speed bumps, rail crossings, entrances with flat ranges (Cyclowiki; Telegram, Jul 20 2022).
  • Champions. No single executive champion is documented publicly. The first named community manager, "akbars", took on the role on November 2, 2010 (shtosm.ru, 2014). Staff moderators of the club (e.g. "Lobov Sergey", "Fleget") appear throughout the threads.

Trajectory: yearly edit volume has been flat at about 18–20M since 2021, and annual active editors at roughly 50,000–56,000. The base of active mappers is now growing outside Russia (Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Belarus). The owned discussion layer shrank sharply: about 10,000 club participants in 2014 against about 1,400 in the replacement Q community in 2021. For example, 190 mappers made 145,000 edits in one two-week July 2025 drive (Telegram, Jul 23 2025).

What keeps people coming back

  1. Your work shows up on the map everyone uses. Edits feed the dominant consumer map in Russia, which has about 95M monthly users by Yandex's figures as cited in pntr.io, 2025 (company claim, secondhand). Even critics conceded this is the hook. "People come to the project not because it's alive but because it's Yandex. As with Google Map Maker, being part of something great, of maps everyone uses, outweighs the closed licence, over-zealous moderators and a depressed community" (shtosm.ru, Apr 2014).
  2. A territorial status ladder. Regular editors can become experts (bulk edits and deletes in a layer). Since Feb 26, 2016 that status is granted per layer. Above them are people's moderators, who review edits in a territory they know, mentor newcomers, arbitrate disputes and fight vandals. Rights are requested per layer (19–20 of about 21 layers) and are lost if a moderator stops working for more than a month (Cyclowiki; Yandex Help). The profile shows "Moderator" plus the territory and layer icons (Yandex Help). New permissions are rationed as rewards: transit route editing for experienced users (2017), indoor plans (2019), HD road lanes for selected experts (2026).
  3. Dense achievements. Badges come in families: edit-count tiers from 5 to 2,500,000 edits, a years-of-service badge for each year up to 13, one-offs (e.g. "Eye of the People" for 10,000 and 1,000,000 road photos, "Patient mentor", "Media star", "Pre-2015 veteran"), badges for co-edits and each theme week, a badge for each city meetup, and monthly moderator awards (Cyclowiki; Yandex promo page). Some are deliberately scarce. In 2026 only the first HD-layer editors before November 1 get a "rare" badge (Telegram, Sep 30 2026).
  4. Staged, time-boxed social events. Co-edits ("sovredy") start the day new aerial photos are published. Each city gets a live counter in the editor, mappers "book" a patch in the comments, and Yandex posts midpoint and final results with leaders by name. Typical runs draw 60–200 participants and 50,000–145,000 edits over 7–14 days, and members also organise their own co-edits after a staff review (Cyclowiki; Telegram 2023–2026). A 20-day "co-edit marathon" in spring 2025 drew 350 mappers and 385,000 edits (Telegram, Apr 15 2025). The channel's own narration shows the drama: "Some heroes pushed the counter past 29,000 in the last minute" (Telegram, Sep 22 2023).
  5. A seasonal calendar. Rituals recur every year: the April 8 birthday (badges for editing that day), new-year quests in the editor (~50 players in 2021), the new-year tree-mapping tradition, the December People's Map Award, an online new-year meetup during COVID, and the "My year" recap button (Telegram, Dec 2021).
  6. Recognition with physical rewards. The annual award named 70 winners in 2021, 72 in 21 categories in 2023 and 88 in 21 categories in 2024 ("more than half of the categories include people who joined this year"). Gifts are sent by email and post, and in February 2024 extra gifts went to active mappers who had not won (Telegram; Telegram, Feb 28 2024).
  7. Peer and parasocial ties. Office meetups with staff and developers, where members also gave talks, ran from 2011 to January 2020. They then stopped until a Tashkent meetup on June 4, 2026. Chats and moderators handle most peer support (Cyclowiki; Telegram, May 26 2026).
  8. Switching costs. Members accumulate edit counts, badges, rights and "their" towns, and none of it can be exported. The data is Yandex's under the user agreement (OSM forum, 2010). The same feature that critics call lock-in keeps veterans in place.

The content engine

  • Rules and help. A detailed, versioned rulebook (the drawing rules for each layer), with changes announced in batches every few weeks (Yandex Help; Telegram, Sep–Oct 2026). Even OSM partisans praised it: "the drawing rules, the absence of which leaves the OpenStreetMap community in anarchy" (shtosm.ru). Four training videos were published in 2013, and in-person trainings for moderators started in 2017.
  • Releases as content. Yandex turns its own data work into a steady stream of posts. Every satellite, aerial or panorama release becomes a travelogue-style article ("let's take a virtual walk through Yoshkar-Ola"), and every editor release gets notes. This gives the community something new to draw almost every week.
  • Recaps and spotlights. Midpoint and final co-edit recaps with charts and leader names, yearly recaps, five-year "chronicles" (2020, 2025), member interviews on the Yandex blog (2015), and award write-ups.
  • Member content is encouraged and rewarded. A December 2021 contest on Yandex Q paid merchandise for the best explanatory posts (Telegram, Dec 23 2021). The award had a "Booker of the People's Map" category for club content and an "Improver" category for building improvements. Members built the "Additional tools" browser extension, which Yandex promoted in its own community (Yandex Q, Feb 2022).
  • Weakness: much of this material lives on rented or since-retired Yandex platforms. The forum, Q, Messenger chats and Instagram have each been archived or reset in turn.

People, programs and governance

  • Staff. A community team (named community manager since 2010, staff moderators in the club and chats) plus the cartographic production department, which does the second-level review, rules and imagery (Cyclowiki). Team size is not published. Yandex also hires paid "junior cartographers" on piece rates to moderate volunteer edits. A 2023 account by one of them: "There are people who do this work for achievements and extended editor functions but without money ... only Yandex monetises the product" (Pikabu, Mar 15 2023).
  • Volunteer roles. Experts and people's moderators, as described above. There were also user-organised co-edits and mentors in the School of People's Cartographers (April 2, 2019). It trained 25 students, of whom 16 became experts and 4 moderators, then closed on October 1, 2019 "because of a shortage of mentors and a large number of students" (Cyclowiki).
  • Rules and moderation. The club rules ban publicly discussing exploitable vulnerabilities. They also ban "statements about leaving the project, as well as messages capable of causing negative consequences for the Club, the service or the community", punishable by a warning or a 7-day ban (Club rules, 2017). Since February 12, 2021, even experts' and moderators' edits are no longer auto-approved (Cyclowiki). State rules apply too: military sites, lighthouses and runways are forbidden objects, and in August 2024 editing was restricted in the Kursk region.
  • Feedback loop. Bug-report and feature-request threads in the club, the "Ya.Board" for wishes (2019), surveys (with a badge), application forms for new rights, and requests for new imagery. Members filed 1,107 imagery requests in one year to October 2026 (Telegram, Oct 22 2026). Members who supported OSM complained in 2014 that staff answered mostly "we're working on it" while developer work had stalled since 2012 (shtosm.ru).

Growth loops

  • Product funnel from 95M map users. Error reports from Yandex Maps users become "hypotheses" that mappers resolve, and casual users can suggest edits from the Maps app. Mappers in turn generate imagery requests and road photos (Cyclowiki; UzDaily, 2026).
  • Mass-participation weeks pull in lapsed and first-time users. The April 2020 stay-at-home week drew many "who don't draw regularly. Many of them made their first edits", and overall activity rose by more than a quarter (Cyclowiki).
  • Geographic expansion. Interface languages (Kazakh and Uzbek in 2019), CIS theme weeks and the 2026 Tashkent meetup seed national communities ahead of Yandex's commercial expansion.
  • Press and SEO. Media profiles of members ("Media star" badge), local press, and Q and club answers indexed by search. No referral program was found.

Evidence of business impact

  • Company statements (claims, not audited numbers): most city maps in Russia were drawn by users: "detailed schemes of all Russian cities (most of them drawn by People's Map users)" (Club, 2015). Volunteers mapped Tbilisi and Yerevan ahead of the Taxi launches. The 20-F (2016) treats the merged editor as a feature of Yandex.Maps, but gives no financial figures.
  • Throughput as a proxy: about 18–20M edits a year by about 50,000 people. The 2025 figures were 580,000 moderated edits, 43.5M road photos and 100,000 resolved user error reports (Telegram, Dec 2025). In Uzbekistan, 1.75M edits since 2020 power "navigation, delivery, and emergency services" (UzDaily, 2026).
  • No hard evidence found on cost savings, retention of map users, or revenue tied to the community, and no academic study of the community was found (OpenAlex search, Nov 2026). Score for business-impact evidence: 3 (strong operational proxies and a filing mention, no money figures).

What they did right

  1. Made contribution matter. The 2015 merge was painful, but after it every accepted edit reaches Maps and Navigator. Staff say so in every birthday post: "once on the map, this information becomes useful to millions of people" (Telegram, Apr 2024).
  2. Built a long, granular status ladder with territory and per-layer ownership, and kept rationing new privileges (transit, indoor plans, HD roads). Each new permission reactivates veterans. When applications for HD roads opened, 60+ came in within a day (Telegram, Jan 30 2026).
  3. Turned company operations into community events. Each new aerial photo release becomes a counter-driven, leaderboard-backed co-edit, and each new product need becomes a theme week. Week results are concrete: 10,000+ traffic lights in 2015, ~120,000 entrances in 2017, 51,000 speed bumps from 1,100+ mappers in 2022 (Cyclowiki).
  4. Kept up an unbroken staff cadence for 16 years. Weekly posts, yearly awards with gifts, and recognition that deliberately includes newcomers. The core proves durable: 600 editors active since 2010 were still editing in 2025–26.
  5. Wrote clear rules. A strict, well-documented rulebook lowers disputes and gives moderators authority. OSM critics admitted it was an advantage.

What they did wrong

  1. The April 2015 relaunch destroyed volunteer work. Shapes were simplified, and descriptions, photos and history were dropped. Objects were imported from old snapshots, so months of work were lost. Volunteers wrote: "Years of volunteering down the drain!" and "90% of almost four years of work down the drain" (Club, 2015). Wikireality reports that dozens of previously active users left (Wikireality). Consequence: a lasting loss of trust among veterans and a "Pre-2015 veteran" badge as a consolation.
  2. A closed licence alienated the open-data community. "The licence is brutal: the user transfers to Yandex the rights to the data in full" (OSM forum, Apr 8 2010). Russian OSM leaders campaigned against it (OSM wiki, Zverik). Members cannot export their own work, which is lock-in that also caps member ownership (score 3).
  3. Product neglect from 2012 to 2015. A count of nearly 2,000 club posts found almost no feature announcements between March 2012 and April 2014. Staff filled the gap with statistics and printed maps (shtosm.ru, Apr 2014).
  4. Platform churn. The community's home moved from ya.ru to the Yandex blog club, then Yandex Q (2021, about 1,400 members versus about 10,000 in the club in 2014), then VK (2023), each time because Yandex shut down its own platform. In February 2024 the Messenger chat was reset and its history disappeared (Telegram, Feb 13 2024). Every move broke archives and threads.
  5. Censoring exits and dissent. The rule against announcing that you are leaving suppresses the very signal a community team most needs.
  6. Programs started and dropped. The mentoring school closed after six months for lack of mentors. The "Patient mentor" badge was retired, and user 3D models were paused for years until 2025. Offline meetups stopped for six years (2020–2026).
  7. Geopolitics leak into the map. Yandex showed Crimea differently by country from March 2014 (AIN, 2014). It dropped national borders from Maps in June 2022 (TechCrunch, 2022), and restricted editing in the Kursk region in 2024. This limits the community's reach and appeal outside Russia's political sphere.
  8. Free labour beside paid labour. Volunteers moderate the same queue that piece-rate junior cartographers are paid to work (Pikabu, 2023). The tension is visible but has not caused a revolt.

Lessons for building a product community

Copy these

  1. Tie each company release to a time-boxed member event. New data, feature or need, then a 7–14 day drive with a live counter, a midpoint post, a final recap with names and a badge. Yandex does this every few weeks and it reliably produces 50,000–145,000 edits per run.
  2. Ration new powers as rewards. Per-area and per-feature permissions, granted on application and reviewed by staff, give veterans a reason to come back and a sense of being trusted.
  3. Recognise widely, in layers. Badges for volume, tenure, events and yearly titles. Include a newcomer category and send something physical. Then keep the cadence for years.
  4. Publish the rulebook and treat rule changes as news. Clear rules make moderators effective and make volunteer work defensible.
  5. Make the impact visible. Show contributors exactly where their work lands in the main product and how many people it serves.

Avoid these

  1. Never migrate or relaunch in a way that destroys contributors' work. Freeze, migrate the latest state, and keep history, or expect to lose the veterans.
  2. Don't keep moving the community's home or erasing its archives. Pick a durable space and own the export.
  3. Don't suppress exit and dissent signals, such as bans on posting that you are leaving. Measure and answer them instead. And if contributors can never take their work with them, expect the open-source crowd to organise against you.
